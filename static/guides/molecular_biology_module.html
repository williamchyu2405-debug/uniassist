2. Part 1: Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids store and carry genetic information

🔊-0:00

Nucleic acids are the information-carrying molecules of the cell. There are two types: deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), the stable long-term store of genetic information, and ribonucleic acid (RNA), a shorter-lived working copy used to build proteins. Both are polymers built from repeating units called nucleotides.

The nucleotide is the building block

Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleotides join through their sugar and phosphate groups to form a sugar–phosphate backbone, leaving the bases free to pair across the two strands.

Bases pair in a specific way

DNA uses four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). The bases pair by hydrogen bonding following complementary base pairing rules: A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G pairs with C (three hydrogen bonds). Because pairing is specific, one strand automatically specifies the sequence of the other.

RNA differs from DNA in three key ways

  • RNA uses the sugar ribose instead of deoxyribose.
  • RNA uses the base uracil (U) in place of thymine — so U pairs with A.
  • RNA is usually single-stranded and much shorter than DNA.

3. Part 2: The Central Dogma

Information flows from DNA to RNA to protein

🔊-0:00

The central dogma describes how genetic information is used. DNA is transcribed into a messenger RNA (mRNA) copy, and that mRNA is translated into a chain of amino acids that folds into a protein. DNA is also replicated so a full copy can be passed to each new cell during division.

Genes and the genome

A gene is a segment of DNA that codes for a functional product — usually a protein. The complete set of an organism's DNA is its genome. Although every cell in a multicellular organism carries the same genome, different cell types switch on different genes, which is why a nerve cell and a muscle cell look and behave so differently.

Key idea: The sequence of bases in a gene ultimately determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein. A change in the DNA sequence (a mutation) can therefore change the protein.

Reading a template strand

During transcription only one DNA strand — the template strand — is read. Because pairing is complementary, the mRNA sequence matches the other (coding) strand, except that uracil replaces thymine.

4. Part 3: Key Molecular Processes

DNA Replication

🔊-0:00

Before a cell divides it must copy its entire genome. The double helix is unwound by helicase, and each old strand acts as a template. The enzyme DNA polymerase adds new nucleotides following base-pairing rules, producing two identical double helices. Because each new molecule keeps one old strand and one new strand, replication is described as semi-conservative.

Transcription

Transcription takes place in the nucleus. RNA polymerase binds to a start signal (the promoter), unwinds a short stretch of DNA, and builds an mRNA copy of the template strand. In eukaryotes the new mRNA is processed — non-coding regions are removed and protective caps and tails are added — before it leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.

The genetic code

The mRNA is read in groups of three bases called codons. Each codon specifies one amino acid, and there are 64 possible codons for 20 amino acids, so the code is redundant (several codons can specify the same amino acid). A special start codon (AUG) begins translation, and three stop codons end it.

Translation

Translation happens on ribosomes. The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time. Molecules of transfer RNA (tRNA), each carrying a specific amino acid, recognise their matching codon through a complementary anticodon. The ribosome joins the amino acids with peptide bonds, building the growing polypeptide chain until it reaches a stop codon.

Regulating gene expression

Cells do not express every gene at once. Gene expression is controlled so that the right proteins are made in the right cells at the right time. Regulation can occur at transcription (whether a gene is read), during RNA processing, or at translation. This control is what allows one genome to produce hundreds of specialised cell types.
