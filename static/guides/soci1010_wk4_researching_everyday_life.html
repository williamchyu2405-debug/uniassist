§0Bridge from Week 3

Wrapping up: Garfinkel & Goffman

Two micro-sociologists who read the unwritten rules of interaction.

Week 4 opens by finishing the two thinkers Week 3 left hanging — both from the University of Chicago, both focused on the micro scale of face-to-face life. The point they share: what looks like natural, orderly behaviour is actually a fragile achievement, held together by rules nobody wrote down. This is the same “work of a comedian” the convenor described in Week 1 — using Curb Your Enthusiasm to make the taken-for-granted visible.

§0.1Garfinkel & ethnomethodology

Harold Garfinkel coined ethnomethodology in Studies in Ethnomethodology (1967). Decode the word: ethno = people, method = means, ology = study of — the study of the methods people themselves use to make sense and meaning out of the potential chaos of social life. It is a micro, actor-centred approach interested in reflexivity and agency.

  • Order is only apparent — social life looks very orderly but is potentially chaotic; the rules are not fixed and must be created and re-created in every interaction. It is the individual who, by responding to events and filling in the gaps, constructs social order.
  • Formal vs informal norms — formal norms are the written rules; informal norms are the unwritten rules, and those are what ethnomethodology hunts.
  • Breaching experiments — the signature technique: deliberately break a tacit rule of interaction to decode it by the reaction it provokes.
Reasoning · why breaking a rule reveals it

You cannot see an invisible rule while everyone obeys it — it only becomes visible when it is violated. That is the logic of the breaching experiment. In Garfinkel’s Case 6, a student answers “How are you?” with “How am I in regard to what — my health, my finances, my schoolwork…?”, and the other person becomes “red in the face and out of control.” The over-literal answer breaks the rule that “How are you?” is a ritual greeting, not a genuine question — and the anger exposes the rule. Same with standing too close in a queue, or sitting right beside a stranger on a half-empty bus: the discomfort is the data. Crucially, the awkwardness “repairs itself very quickly” — social order is resilient because we constantly re-make it.

Applied · the Curb Your Enthusiasm breaches

The lecture screens Larry David as an accidental ethnomethodologist — every scene is a breach that surfaces an “unwritten law.” The Pig Parker (one car over the line — “society can’t function like this”); Trick or Treat (17-year-olds with no costumes — is there a “cutoff”? the officer names the “social contract” of opening the door); the bow (“a bow is a bow” — was it authentic?); the caviar at the party (“you take a little, then step away…” — an “unwritten law”); the Sorry Window (is condolence “closed” two years on?). Each is useful for the assignment: it models how to take a trivial scene and ask what rule is being enforced here?

§0.2Goffman & dramaturgy

Erving Goffman (Canadian, 1922–1982) is renowned for unmasking how social actors ‘present the self’ to others. His dramaturgical theory reads social life as drama — a theatre play. In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959) he studies how “the individual in ordinary situations presents him or herself… to others.” Interactions involve the performance of roles; the situations that shape them are the stages. (His PhD fieldwork was in the Shetland Islands; other books include Asylums 1961, Stigma 1963, Frame Analysis 1974, Forms of Talk 1981.)

Goffman’s dramaturgical toolkit
TermWhat it means
Front stageThe self performed to an audience, following the role’s conventions — the waiter interacting with diners.
Back stageOut of role, where the performance relaxes and is prepared — the same waiter in the kitchen.
Impression managementActively controlling the impression others form of us — we would rather avoid social embarrassment.
Props & scriptsObjects that signal a role (laptop = student; gym bag = gym) and set lines for each context (“hello” / “bye”).
Interactive ritualExtending Durkheim’s sacred/profane: all interaction is ritual (a greeting), governed by ground rules.
Reasoning · the self is produced in the performance

Goffman’s deeper claim is counter-intuitive: the self is not a fixed thing that pre-exists the acting and then gets displayed. Rather, the self is a product of the dramatic interaction between actor and audience — it is developed through the performance. Because actors must stay attuned to the audience’s reaction (just as a stage actor does), interaction is a running loop of impression management. Ground rules make it work like traffic: “hello” and “goodbye” are the green and red lights that signal when someone is available, and we obey them for the same reason we stop at a red light — so the flow stays orderly.

Memory hook · stage directions

Front stage = for the audience; back stage = off the record. Ethnomethodology = the method people use to make order; breach it to reveal it.

Applied · social media & the take-home exam

Impression management explodes online: the carefully-crafted profile picture, the curated Instagram post — all front-stage performance for an audience. The convenor flags that a Goffman question “will definitely” be on the take-home exam, framed as a scenario asking you to think sociologically about a taken-for-granted scene. Reach for front/back stage, impression management and interactive ritual to unpack it.

R Recall checklist — Garfinkel & Goffman
  • Ethnomethodology (Garfinkel 1967) — study of how people make order out of chaos; order is created & re-created, not fixed.
  • Breaching experiment — break a tacit rule (over-answering “How are you?”) to decode it; awkwardness = the data.
  • Formal vs informal norms — written vs unwritten rules; micro-sociology targets the unwritten.
  • Goffman’s dramaturgy — social life as theatre; front stage vs back stage; impression management; props & scripts.
  • Interactive ritual — extending Durkheim, all interaction (greetings) is ritual with ground rules.
§1The everyday hook

The sociology of self-checkouts

An ordinary machine that quietly rewires shopping, labour and interaction.

Like the bus stop, the queue and brushing teeth, the self-checkout is a mundane object worth de-familiarising. A self-service checkout is a machine that lets customers complete their own transaction without a staffed till. It arrived astonishingly fast: ~217,000 units worldwide in 2023, with ~2 million predicted by 2029; invented by David R. Humble (the Florida firm CheckRobot Inc.), with NCR Corporation holding the largest market share. The sociological question: what changes when a person is replaced by a thing?

Reading the self-checkout sociologically
ObservationThe sociological reading
Things, not peopleSelf-service increasingly means transactions with machines rather than staff — human–machine interaction displaces human–human interaction.
The iron cageWeber’s “iron cage of rationality”: the shift to rational, bureaucratic organisation — deemed convenient/efficient; lets business curb rising labour costs.
Who does the work?Do-it-yourself implies customers perform unpaid work (scanning, bagging) once done by a paid cashier.
Rationalisation of space10 staffed lanes become 20 self-checkout machines — the shop floor is re-engineered around the machines.
End of interaction?Shopping becomes isolating and removed — Simmel’s thinning of social ties in the metropolis.
Irrationality of rationalityEfficient but frustrating/dehumanising when it fails; consumer deviance / retail crime costs Australia over A$4.5 billion a year.
Reasoning · rationalisation, made physical

The self-checkout is Weber’s rationalisation you can touch. The whole point is efficiency — more throughput, fewer wages. But rationalisation has a boomerang Weber called the irrationality of rationality: the system built to be smooth becomes maddening the moment something jams (“unexpected item in the bagging area”) and you must fetch a human anyway. The convenor’s sharpest point is about hidden labour: when a cashier scanned your groceries, the shop paid for that work; now you do it for free, and even “free” delivery is paid for in a delivery fee or a minimum spend. The machine also watches — integrated accounting tells head office that 20 kg of banana sold, and AI face-recognition can replay footage if you scanned a cucumber as a (cheaper) zucchini.

Applied · the human cost, and human ingenuity

Two real-world consequences. First, a lost rung: the supermarket checkout was the classic first part-time job for students and school-leavers — harder to find now. Second, the rationalisation is “curtailed by irrational outcomes that increase costs”: chronic staff turnover (often young workers now patrolling machines — Macquarie Centre reportedly runs ~54 staff over the machines), theft, service and maintenance. And theft itself shows agency: scanning oranges as lemons is people testing how far the rules bend — the same “going against the grain” Garfinkel would notice.

R Recall checklist — self-checkouts
  • Scale — ~217k units (2023) → ~2m predicted (2029); invented by David R. Humble; NCR = largest share.
  • Weber’s iron cage — rationalisation/efficiency, curbing labour costs; things not people.
  • Unpaid work + rationalisation of space (10 lanes → 20 machines); Simmel’s end of interaction.
  • Irrationality of rationality — frustrating when it fails; retail crime > A$4.5bn/yr; chronic turnover.
§2Research design

How can we study the everyday?

Theory, direction of reasoning, and choosing a method.

Sociology is not personal opinion — it studies systematically. Before picking a tool, three distinctions. First, ‘theory’ is simply a framework for understanding phenomena; it can be contemplative/speculative or grounded in empirical evidence — often both. Then two axes: which direction your reasoning runs, and which scale and kind of data you gather.

Deductive vs inductive · macro vs micro
ContrastDeductive / MacroInductive / Micro
Direction of reasoningDeductive — top-down: start with a hypothesis/theory and test whether data fit it (“I think students use phones in class” → go and check).Inductive — bottom-up: start from specific observations and infer general principles. Everyday-life study is usually built from the ground up.
Scale of analysisMacro-sociology — large-scale, impersonal features: institutions, organisations, culture (Weber’s rationalisation).Micro-sociology — personal, immediate, face-to-face encounters in daily life.

Note: macro/micro is not a clean dichotomy — they overlap (Weber’s rationalisation applies to everyday activities like the self-checkout too).

§2.1Quantitative, qualitative & mixed methods

Sociologists use social research methods to study everyday life, of two broad kinds — often combined as mixed methods.

The two families of method (and their blend)
MethodGoalTools
QuantitativeMeasure & generalise social phenomena — identify trends (e.g. the rate of rural youth suicide by region).Statistics, survey, poll, census.
QualitativeDelve into subjective experience, meaning & interaction — the why behind the trend.Ethnography, observation, interviews, personal documents, films, images.
MixedCombine both — a trend from the numbers, its meaning from the words.e.g. a suicide-rate survey then interviews with young men in remote farms.
Reasoning · why qualitative suits the everyday

A survey can tell you how many — 10 million young Australians have a social-media account — but a survey answer is thin: yes/no, “often/sometimes,” a rank. It cannot capture what it feels like. To learn whether social media inspires people or leads to bullying and body-shaming, you have to ask them, and listen. Qualitative research prioritises lived experience and the meanings people attach to things — exactly the fabric of everyday life. That is why ethnography, not the census, is the everyday sociologist’s main tool. Quantitative maps the what; qualitative explains the why.

Applied · a portable, employable skill

The convenor stresses this is a trained skill, not common sense — surveying needs statistics; interviewing and focus groups need method. It pays off beyond academia: employers in the public and private sector hire sociology graduates to interpret data, run focus groups and do market research (fashion, colour trends, generational attitudes). For SOCI1010, the immediately usable slice is participant observation — the skill you practise on the assignment’s two images and in the tutorial field exercise on campus.

R Recall checklist — studying the everyday
  • Theory = a framework for understanding; speculative and/or empirical.
  • Deductive (top-down, test a hypothesis) vs inductive (bottom-up, infer from observation — the everyday).
  • Macro (institutions, impersonal) vs micro (face-to-face) — overlapping, not a clean split.
  • Quantitative (measure/generalise) vs qualitative (meaning/experience) vs mixed; qualitative fits the everyday.
§3The core method

What is ethnography?

Being there, and writing it down — from the native point of view.

Ethnography is the central qualitative method, borrowed from anthropology. Decode it like ethnomethodology: ethno = people / culture, graphy = writing. It is how sociologists and anthropologists explore people’s social worlds and ask: how does everyday life make sense to people? Its aim is ‘thick description’ (Geertz) — “to understand another way of life from the native point of view” (Spradley 1979). It is an in-context investigation, usually over a long stretch — hours, days, months, even years.

§3.1The two components

Ethnography has two components, and the name captures both: ethno (observing people) + graphy (writing it down).

The two components of ethnographic research
ComponentWhat it involvesAnchor
1. Participant observation“You are directly involved in community life, observing and talking with people as you learn from them their view of reality.” A direct relationship with social actors, approached in their own environment; describe & participate in everyday events/rituals; understand the meaning of actions.Agar (1996)
2. Recording & documentingFieldnotes of what one sees and hears in a setting — “What does the ethnographer do? He or she writes.” The written record can then be interpreted (eye contact made, people shifting, the queue forming).Emerson, Fretz & Shaw (1995); Geertz (1973)
Reasoning · emic vs etic — the wink and the blink

Participant observation carries a double meaning: you participate in people’s lives and keep enough professional distance to spot patterns. The prize is the emic perspective — the insider’s point of view — as against the etic, the outsider’s/sociologist’s view (Gobo 2011). The textbook illustration is the difference between a wink and a blink: physically identical, but a wink carries a coded meaning only insiders share (“police around”), while a blink is just dust in the eye. Miss the emic layer and you misread the scene. To get inside meaning you adopt the Verstehen approach — empathetic understanding: put yourself in the subject’s place. This is how you “learn the hidden rules”: the logic of everyday life is understood implicitly by members but hidden to outsiders (subcultures like punks, goths, or a Freemason handshake).

§3.2The positioned subject — ethnography is not objective

A defining commitment: ethnographers do not try to be objective observers standing outside social reality. They try to become part of the world they research — they are ‘positioned subjects’.

  • Experiential — research changes in the process of doing it; it is lived, not extracted.
  • Dialogical — it goes backward and forward between observation and reflection (Kusenbach’s “street phenomenology” is the example — see §7).
  • Subjective — an exercise in interpretation of social and cultural worlds.
  • The researcher is the research tool — ‘meaning’ is negotiated between researcher and researched, recognising the symbolic nature of reality (much of social life is art, music, ritual, style, fashion, emotion — not amenable to natural-science positivism).
Memory hook · ethno-GRAPHY

Ethno = watch the people · graphy = write it down. Get the emic (insider) view, not just the etic (outsider) view. You are a positioned subject — the tool, not a fly on the wall.

Applied · the ethics of being the instrument

If the researcher is the instrument and meaning is negotiated, then objectivity can’t be the guarantee of quality — reflexivity is. Positioned subjectivity has real ethical weight: your presence alters what you study (see Kusenbach on researcher reactivity, §7), so you must be transparent about your standpoint, mindful of consent and power when entering others’ environments, and honest that your interpretation is an interpretation. For the assignment, this is licence to write in the first person and reflect on your own position when reading the image — but it is also a duty to represent your subjects fairly.

R Recall checklist — ethnography
  • Ethnography = ethno (people) + graphy (writing); ‘thick description’ (Geertz); the native point of view (Spradley).
  • Two components — (1) participant observation (Agar) in the participants’ own environment; (2) recording fieldnotes (Emerson/Geertz — “he writes”).
  • Emic vs etic — insider vs outsider view (wink vs blink); Verstehen = empathetic understanding.
  • Positioned subject — not objective; experiential, dialogical, subjective; the researcher is the research tool; meaning is negotiated.
§4Origins

A short history of ethnography

From the Trobriand Islands to the streets of Chicago.

Ethnography evolved in the early 20th century in anthropology, then migrated into sociology. Two origin stories matter: Malinowski going to the Pacific, and the Chicago School turning the method on the modern city.

§4.1Malinowski & the Trobriand Islands

The method is attributed especially to Bronisław Malinowski and his fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands (east of Papua New Guinea, 1915–1919), which produced best-sellers including Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) and The Sexual Life of Savages (1929).

Reasoning · why he had to go there

Until the 1920s, most writing about non-Western cultures came from armchair anthropologists — Europeans back home working up the second-hand reports of explorers (Cook, Vasco da Gama and the rest). Malinowski’s revolution was to insist the researcher must live with the people studied — literally isolate himself from his own society and share their life, however “boring, difficult and depressing,” in order to grasp “the native point of view.” His approach: daily diary entries recording observations and reflections; learn the local language; a gradual move from stranger to belonging; and stay a long time in the field so villagers themselves explain how kinship, gender and religion work. Second-hand knowledge cannot deliver that — presence can.

§4.2The Chicago School

Ethnography first took hold in sociology at the University of Chicago, Department of Sociology. Faced with a 1920s–30s city transformed by migration, they were dissatisfied with the survey method and treated the city itself as a ‘social experiment’ — a living laboratory — studied via small-scale participant observation. Their subjects were the city’s less-accessible arenas: immigrants, the homeless, gangs, the underclass, crime and deviance (this is Al Capone’s Chicago). The classic dictum, from Robert Park:

Applied · “get the seat of your pants dirty”

“Go and sit in the lounges of the luxury hotels and on the doorsteps of the flophouses; sit on the Gold Coast settees and on the slum shakedowns… In short, gentlemen, go get the seat of your pants dirty in real research” (Park, cited in Bulmer 1984). The message: real sociology happens in the field, not at your desk. The exemplar is Whyte’s Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum (1943).

Reasoning · inductive, and de-familiarising

Chicago liked ethnography precisely because it is inductive rather than deductive — understanding grows from the lived experience of people on the ground, not from a hypothesis imposed from above. And there is a neat asymmetry the convenor highlights: for the anthropologist abroad, ethnography makes the ‘unfamiliar familiar’; for the sociologist at home, it must make the ‘familiar unfamiliar’ — going beneath stereotypes and common sense to recover meaning from the ground up. The mental trick: see your own bus commute as an alien would.

§4.3Ethnography in sociology, from the 1970s

Participant observation was gradually accepted across the social sciences. From the 1970s the method is called ‘ethnography’ and combines a range of qualitative techniques — observation, formal and informal interviews, ongoing interaction. In sociology, cultural studies and human geography it need not rest on long-term residence (unlike classic anthropology); it is engaged qualitative research using participant observation and other methods. Hence the qualified variants: focused, practical, digital, visual and auto-ethnography.

R Recall checklist — history of ethnography
  • Malinowski — Trobriand Islands (1915–19); Argonauts (1922); rejected armchair anthropology; live with the people, learn the language, stay long.
  • Chicago School — dissatisfied with surveys; city as a ‘social experiment’; inductive participant observation; “pants dirty in real research” (Park).
  • Familiar unfamiliar (sociology) vs unfamiliar familiar (anthropology).
  • From the 1970s — ‘ethnography’ = mixed qualitative techniques; not necessarily long-term; focused/digital/visual/auto-ethnography.
§5Case studies

Ethnography in action — four studies

Asylums, the ‘lads’, the boxing gym, and a Chicago gang.

Four famous ethnographies show the method’s reach — from a mental institution to a housing project — and what going inside reveals that standing outside never could.

Four ethnographies compared
StudySite & methodKey concept / finding
Goffman, Asylums (1961)A mental institution, ~1 year, largely covert observation (posed as an assistant).Total institution; the “unofficial reality”; mortification & stripping of identity; the patients’ point of view.
Willis, Learning to Labour (1977)12 working-class ‘lads’ at Hammertown Boys; observation, informal interviews, diaries, taped parent/teacher interviews.Culture of resistance; the lads “let themselves” get working-class jobs — class reproduced.
Wacquant, Body & Soul (2004)A Chicago boxing gym; the researcher trains — embodied participation (Bourdieu).“We learn by body”; body as a site of struggle; masculinity; the gym as resistance.

§5.1Goffman’s total institution

Extending his interaction work, Goffman’s Asylums theorised the total institution: “a place of work and residence where a great number of similarly-situated people, cut off from the wider community for a considerable time, together lead an enclosed, formally administered round of life.” Inside, the everyday rules from outside no longer hold: inmates lose the usual accoutrements of identity — shaved heads, tagged/institutional uniforms, standardised address, no control over their daily rhythm, little outside contact. Other examples: prisons, monasteries, cadet schools, army training camps (and cults).

Reasoning · why covert, and why the patient’s view

Goffman got in as an assistant director; inmates and staff did not know he was researching (he had management’s permission). The payoff was a view of the “unofficial reality” from the patients’ point of view, rather than through the psychiatric categories imposed on them. His striking conclusion: what looks insane to an outside observer is not irrational when seen in the context of the hospital — behaviour that seems mad from outside is a rational adaptation to a bizarre, total environment. Only immersion could surface that.

§5.2Willis’s Learning to Labour

Paul Willis studied 12 non-academic working-class ‘lads’ at “Hammertown Boys,” asking how and why working-class kids get working-class jobs. He found a conscious counter-school culture of resistance — disruption, bravado, humour, a celebration of male physicality and style — through which bottom-stream pupils win status among peers. His famous line: “The difficult thing to explain about how working-class kids get working-class jobs is why they let themselves.”

Reasoning · choosing your own cage

The orthodox Marxist view was that schools process passive working-class kids into labour. Willis inverts it: the lads are not docile dupes — they actively reject school and choose semi-/unskilled work, seeing through school’s promises and refusing its “failure” label (“I’m my own person”). The tragic irony: this act of agency reproduces the very class position it rebels against — resistance becomes the mechanism of reproduction. Only classroom ethnography (observation + interviews + diaries), not a survey, could catch that inner logic.

§5.3Wacquant, Bourdieu & carnal sociology

Loïc Wacquant (a student of Pierre Bourdieu) asked why young working-class men take up boxing — and to find out, he joined the gym and trained. His warrant is Bourdieu’s: “if it is true that ‘we learn by body’ and that the social order inscribes itself in bodies… then it is imperative that the sociologist submit himself to the fire of action in situ.” Themes: the body as a site of struggle (physically, through training; symbolically, over self and place in society); the socio-economic context of impoverished neighbourhoods (poverty, racial inequality); masculinity and violence (toughness masking emotional complexity); and the gym as a space of cultural resistance and forged bonds.

Reasoning · some knowledge lives only in the body

Standing outside the gym, you get a partial picture. But boxing is embodied knowledge — how to take a punch, the discipline, the fear — that cannot be grasped by detached observation or a questionnaire. So the method must match the object: to study a bodily practice you must participate bodily. This “carnal” or embodied sociology is the logical extreme of participant observation, and it directly foreshadows Kusenbach’s point (§7) that pre-reflective, bodily experience escapes both pure observation and the sit-down interview.

§5.4Venkatesh’s Gang Leader for a Day

Sudhir Venkatesh, a PhD student at Chicago, set out to study economic disadvantage among African-Americans in the south-side Robert Taylor Homes. Approaching residents with a survey clipboard, he was mistaken for a rival and held; the gang told him “you really need to live with people… you can’t ask them silly questions and hope you’ll get the real answers.” He returned, met leader JT (who, tellingly, had a college degree), and shadowed him for ~7–8 years.

Applied · the gang as informal government

Venkatesh’s finding reframes the “gang and drug problem” of the news: in a community of ~40,000 that “can’t rely on the government” — no services, ignored by politicians — the same gang that deals crack and inflicts violence also hands residents cash and help to win favour. People turn to the gang to survive day to day. It is a portrait of an “awful place” people are put in when the state withdraws — an insight available only because the researcher, disastrously at first, went inside. It also stages the §0 lesson vividly: surveys fail where trust and access succeed.

Memory hook · four studies, four verbs

Goffman = confined (total institution) · Willis = resisted (the lads) · Wacquant = fought (learn by body) · Venkatesh = survived (gang for a day).

R Recall checklist — the four case studies
  • Goffman, Asylums (1961) — total institution; mortification/identity-stripping; covert; the patient’s point of view.
  • Willis (1977) — Hammertown lads; counter-school culture of resistance; they “let themselves” → class reproduced.
  • Wacquant (2004) — boxing; Bourdieu’s “we learn by body”; body as a site of struggle; carnal sociology.
  • Venkatesh (2008) — Robert Taylor Homes; project shifted to the gang; gang as informal government where the state withdrew.
§6The payoff

Why ethnography matters for the everyday

What we can learn that numbers never will.

The Summary slide answers its own question — what can we learn from ethnography? — with three things: the hidden rules of everyday life; the meanings people attach to things; and their social relationships and interactions.

  • Hidden rules — the implicit logic members follow but outsiders miss (queue etiquette, the “unwritten laws” the Curb clips expose).
  • Meanings — the symbolic texture of reality (art, ritual, style, emotion) that positivist methods cannot reach.
  • Social relationships & interactions — how people relate, cooperate and rank one another in real settings.
Reasoning · capturing the moment numbers can’t

Ethnography’s power is studying a phenomenon up close — capturing the mood, feeling and moment that quantitative data cannot “sufficiently capture.” It works by de-familiarising the taken-for-granted: on the bus you feel what commuting is like (the emic view), then step back to see the “overarching system” that orchestrates ‘nothing much happening’. A sharp observer of a queue sees not just “people standing” but a social contract being negotiated in real time — turn-taking, eye contact, someone making the first move.

Applied · your assignment & take-home exam

This is the whole skill SOCI1010 is training. Don’t just describe the image (“a bunch of people sitting around”); read it fine-grained — who is entering/leaving, and their apparent age, class, gender, the time of day — then name a theory in what you see: “X and Y are doing this, which speaks to Hochschild’s emotional labour” or Weber’s rationalisation. The convenor calls this doing participant observation on a still image: imagine the scenario, not just the photo. That move — observation + theory — is what earns the marks.

R Recall checklist — why ethnography matters
  • Three payoffs — hidden rules; meanings; social relationships & interactions.
  • Up close — captures mood/feeling/moment quantitative data misses; recognises the symbolic nature of reality.
  • De-familiarise — see the orchestration behind “nothing much happening.”
  • Exam move — read the scene fine-grained, then name the theory it illustrates.
§7The reading · Kusenbach 2003

The go-along: street phenomenology

Don’t sit them down — walk with them.

The week’s reading — Margarethe Kusenbach, “Street Phenomenology: The Go-Along as Ethnographic Research Tool” (Ethnography 2003) — proposes a method that fixes a gap in the classic toolkit. In a go-along, the fieldworker accompanies an informant on their natural everyday outing and — by asking, listening and observing — actively explores their stream of experience as they move through and interact with their physical and social environment. It is a hybrid of participant observation and interviewing, and its edge is access to the “transcendent and reflexive aspects of lived experience in situ” — a “street phenomenology” that gives place its due (building on Schutz).

Reasoning · why neither pure observation nor the sit-down interview is enough

Kusenbach dissects the two classic methods and finds each blind in a different way. Pure observation: because people don’t narrate “what is going on” as they act, a watcher can record what happens but not what it means to the person — even a well-immersed insider ends up projecting their own reference points (her fieldnotes read a swapped realtor sign as a “turf war” — but she couldn’t know it meant that to residents). The sit-down interview: it is static, makes talking the whole event, and — fatally — pulls people out of the environment the talk is about, so pre-reflective, hard-to-narrate detail (the “most trivial details of day-to-day environmental experience”) never surfaces. The go-along’s trick is to do both at once: observe spatial practice in situ while the place itself prompts the informant’s interpretation.

§7.1Natural vs contrived · walk-alongs & ride-alongs

Doing a go-along
DistinctionWhat it means
NaturalFollow trips the informant would take anyway — matching the day, time of day and route as closely as possible. Preserves authenticity.
Contrived / experimentalSteering informants into unfamiliar territory or non-routine activity — may produce “appealing” data, but not authentic practice.
Walk-alongOn foot — ~3/4 of Kusenbach’s 50 go-alongs; better than ride-alongs (slower pace lets you ask & track).
Ride-alongOn wheels — faster, more urgent pace; audio-recording helps; found less effective overall.

Practicalities: a productive window is ~60–90 minutes; expand jottings into full descriptive fieldnotes as soon as possible; give the informant minimal direction (“comment on whatever comes to mind”). The study itself: a 3-year project in Hollywood (LA) neighbourhoods — 61 interviews, ~18 months of observation, and 50 go-alongs covering 30 residents.

Applied · “natural” is never fully natural — reactivity & ethics

Kusenbach is candid that even a natural go-along is a contrived social occasion: people aren’t normally trailed and quizzed on their routine trips, and the researcher’s “presence and curiosity… intrudes upon and alters” a private, delicate dimension of experience — the problem of researcher reactivity (going along with a couple can ease discomfort but only swaps one artificiality for another). This is the positioned-subject ethic from §3 made concrete: you cannot pretend to be a neutral fly on the wall, so the honest response is to combine methods — “the strengths of participant observation, interviewing and go-alongs accumulate when pursued in combination” (Becker’s “many kinds of evidence”).

R Recall checklist — the go-along
  • Go-along (Kusenbach 2003) — accompany an informant on a natural outing; ask + listen + observe in situ; hybrid of observation & interview.
  • Why — observation misses meaning; interviews are static & remove people from place; the go-along gets both at once.
  • Natural vs contrived; walk-alongs (~3/4, better) vs ride-alongs; ~60–90 min; 50 go-alongs / 30 residents in Hollywood.
  • Reactivity — never fully “natural”; combine methods for “many kinds of evidence.”
§8The reading · payoff

The five themes the go-along unlocks

What walking with someone reveals that sitting with them hides.

Kusenbach’s core contribution is naming five themes the go-along is uniquely suited to explore — precisely because they are pre-reflective and visually elusive, hard to catch by observation or interview alone. Memorise the list, then the mechanism behind each.

The five themes (Kusenbach 2003)
#ThemeWhat the go-along reveals
1PerceptionHow personal “relevances” (practical knowledge, tastes/values) filter what a person even notices in the environment.
2Spatial practicesThe varying degree & quality of engagement with place, and how people “thicken” routines with meaning.
3BiographiesHow places store life-history — memories and anticipations triggered by personal landmarks.

§8.11 · Perception — the veils of relevance

Perception is filtered through “veils” of relevance (Schutz) — practical knowledge and tastes/values shaped by who we are. The realtor Tom “sees” invisible things (geology, property values, earthquake risk); ex-Street-Lighting worker Ross always notices the street lamps (their type, “Cobra,” their height). Most vividly, Ross and the musician Jill use the same word for one overgrown house — a “jungle” — but Ross means an eyesore (wild, uncivilised, neglected) and Jill means an exotic treasure (lush, full of secrets, well cared-for).

Reasoning · the judgment pre-exists the seeing

The “jungle” house proves the point: Ross and Jill did not form their opposite reactions on seeing the house — their aesthetic and moral values were in place long before, and the walk-along merely made them explicit. So the go-along doesn’t just record what people see; it exposes the machinery that decides what becomes visible at all and how it is read. A lone observer would have to imagine how others perceive the street; an interviewer would struggle because environmental perception is fragile and pre-reflective. Walking there, in place, surfaces it.

§8.22 · Spatial practices — thickening the routine

Our engagement with the environment varies in strength — Seamon’s continuum from person–environment separateness to mergence. Go-alongs reveal how people “thicken” a functional routine with layers of meaning. Tony (80s) reframes his walk as a timed, measured competition (he has clocked his routes at 2.1 and 2.25 miles) and buys a lottery ticket along the way for “fun.” Andrea reframes walking as politics — a way to “take back your neighbourhood” from fear, an almost subversive act of reclaiming a fundamental right.

Reasoning · meaning you can’t observe or (easily) interview out

These peripheral layers are “impossible to observe” — from outside, Tony is just a man walking — and they didn’t even surface when Kusenbach interviewed him about his routine. They only emerged during the walk, as Tony freely elaborated in place. That is the go-along’s signature: mundane details “too trivial to think and talk about” in a formal setting become obvious when the practice is actually underway. A spatial practice can even become a symbol of identity (Andrea’s walking as a lifelong stance of independence).

§8.33 · Biographies — the environment as biographer

The lived-in environment is a “personal biographer”: landmarks preserve life history, and navigating them is like turning the pages of a photo album. Tony points to a bungalow now fronted by a strip club’s “NUDE GIRLS” sign — “that is where I lived once!” — and it unlocks the post-war years, his late wife, her sister’s generosity, and the pet duck that guarded the children. He thinks of it “every time” he passes.

Reasoning · memory triggered by place

Biographical meaning is anchored in place and released by encountering it. In Tony’s memory the strip club barely exists — only his former home does. Such landmark-triggered memory (and anticipation of the future) is “very difficult to retrieve through interviews and almost impossible to observe”: an off-site interview lacks the trigger, and an observer sees only an old man glancing at a building. Being there when the landmark does its work is what makes the biography visible — the “presence of absences” (de Certeau: sites “haunted” by what is no more).

§8.44 · Social architecture — the invisible web

Social architecture is the web of relationships, groupings and hierarchies among the people who use an area. Sit-down interviews capture strong ties well but miss the weak, functional ones nobody thinks to mention. Moving through the neighbourhood, Jill reads the front yards as a status map: the messy “rental” marks its inhabitants as “underdogs,” while the top model’s house looks “always wonderful” — and Jill positions her own home near the top (a contractor once copied her design).

Reasoning · comparing & positioning

Even without meeting anyone, spatial practice is social: we read physical features as animated, standing for people (Milligan’s “interactional past/potential”). Two operations recur — comparing other places and positioning oneself relative to them — through which locals assess their own status. Because informants spontaneously narrate these connections as they walk, the go-along lets a researcher piece together a “mosaic” of an area’s weak ties — the part of the social map interviews leave blank.

§8.55 · Social realms — spheres of reality

Social realms are the distinct spheres of reality shaped by different patterns of interaction (Lofland 1998) — the anonymous public realm, the semi-familiar parochial realm of neighbourhood, and the private realm.

Reasoning · matching the observer’s position to the realm

Method should fit the realm. The lone, transient observer suits public space, where anonymity reigns and conduct rests on categoric (not personal) knowing. But for the communal, parochial and private realms — where interaction is personal — the walk-along has “a significant edge,” because going along with locals gives a grounded, intimate vantage point on interaction patterns you could access neither as a detached outsider nor as a full-member practitioner. In short: the go-along lets you study the neighbourhood’s in-between realm on its own terms.

Memory hook · the five themes

Walk with someone and you read their P.S. B.S.S. — Perception · Spatial practices · Biographies · Social architecture · Social realms.

Applied · from Kusenbach back to your fieldwork

The five themes are a ready-made observation checklist for the tutorial go-along on campus (or for reading the assignment image as a place): what does this person notice and why (perception); how are they engaging with the space and are they “thickening” it (spatial practice); what history might this spot hold for them (biography); what relationships/hierarchies does the setting encode (social architecture); and which realm — public, parochial or private — are we in, and how does that shape conduct (social realms)?
