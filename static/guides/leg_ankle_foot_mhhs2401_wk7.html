§0Bones & the ankle mortise

Leg bones, tibiofibular joints & the mortise

Two bones, bound into a socket that grips the talus.

The leg (knee to ankle) has the weight-bearing tibia medially and the slender fibula laterally, tied together along their shafts by the interosseous membrane. What matters for the ankle is how their distal ends form a socket.

  • Proximal tibiofibular joint — a synovial plane joint (small gliding movements).
  • Distal tibiofibular joint — a fibrous joint (syndesmosis) that permits no movement and gives strong support to the ankle mortise, reinforced by anterior and posterior tibiofibular ligaments.

The ankle mortise is a deep socket formed by the distal tibia and fibula (with part of the posterior tibiofibular ligament). It grips the trochlea (pulley) of the talus, the medial malleolus holding the medial talar surface and the lateral malleolus the lateral surface.

Reasoning · why the distal joint must be immovable

The whole point of a mortise is a rigid, form-fitting socket. If the distal tibiofibular joint could move, the two malleoli would splay apart under load and the talus would rattle. Making it a fibrous syndesmosis (no movement) keeps the fork clamped tightly around the talus — which is exactly why a "high ankle sprain" that tears this syndesmosis is so destabilising.

R Recall checklist — bones & mortise
  • Tibia vs fibula — tibia = medial, weight-bearing (medial malleolus); fibula = lateral (lateral malleolus).
  • Proximal tibiofibular joint — synovial plane (gliding).
  • Distal tibiofibular joint — fibrous syndesmosis, no movement, supports the mortise.
  • Mortise — distal tibia + fibula grip the trochlea of the talus.
§1Muscle compartments

The three compartments of the leg

Front dorsiflexes, side everts, back plantarflexes — each on its own nerve.

As in the thigh, deep fascia and septa wall the leg into compartments, each a package of muscles, a nerve, and an action. Get the three packages and every muscle and its likely nerve lesion falls into place.

§1.1Anterior & lateral compartments

  • Anterior compartment — tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, fibularis tertius. They dorsiflex the ankle and extend the toes. Nerve: deep fibular nerve.
  • Lateral compartment — fibularis (peroneus) longus and brevis. They evert the foot. Nerve: superficial fibular nerve.

§1.2Posterior compartment

Supplied by the tibial nerve; together these plantar flex the ankle, flex the toes and invert the foot. Two layers:

  • Superficial (triceps surae) — gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris; the powerful plantarflexors, ending in the calcaneal (Achilles) tendon.
  • Deep — popliteus (laterally rotates the femur on the tibia to unlock the knee), tibialis posterior (inverts and plantarflexes), flexor digitorum longus (flexes the lateral four toes), and flexor hallucis longus (flexes the great toe).
Clinical / real-world · flexor hallucis longus & toe-off

Flexor hallucis longus matters most at the toe-off phase of walking: the great toe is the last part of the foot to leave the ground and propels the body forward. Weakness here degrades push-off — and its long tendon, running behind the medial malleolus and under the sustentaculum tali, is a common site of tendinopathy in dancers ("dancer's tendinitis").

§1.3The tarsal tunnel

Behind the medial malleolus, a fibro-osseous tarsal tunnel — floored by the medial malleolus, the talus and calcaneus and the sustentaculum tali, and roofed by the flexor retinaculum — funnels the deep posterior tendons, the posterior tibial artery and the tibial nerve into the sole.

Memory hook · behind the medial malleolus

"Tom, Dick, And a Nervous Harry." Front→back: Tibialis posterior, flexor Digitorum longus, posterior tibial Artery, tibial Nerve, flexor Hallucis longus.

Clinical / real-world · compartment syndrome

Because the fascia is inelastic, bleeding or swelling inside a leg compartment raises pressure until it chokes the vessels and nerves within — compartment syndrome. Learn the 6 Ps: pain (severe, out of proportion), paraesthesia, poikilothermia, pallor, paralysis and pulselessness — the late signs. It is a surgical emergency relieved by fasciotomy. Separately, a deep fibular nerve lesion causes foot-drop (lost dorsiflexion).

  • Anterior — deep fibular nerve; dorsiflex + extend toes (TA, EHL, EDL, fibularis tertius).
  • Lateral — superficial fibular nerve; evert (fibularis longus & brevis).

§2Foot skeleton & joints

Bones & joints of the foot

Seven tarsals, five metatarsals, the phalanges — and the joints that let the foot adapt.

The foot skeleton is three tiers: the tarsals (talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, and the medial, intermediate and lateral cuneiforms), the five metatarsals, and the phalanges. The bones are tied by dorsal and plantar ligaments and meet at a series of joints whose type predicts their movement.

Two functional groupings matter. The subtalar joint (talus on calcaneus) drives inversion/eversion (§3). The transverse tarsal joint is the calcaneocuboid + talocalcaneonavicular joints combined — a line across the midfoot that lets the forefoot swivel on the hindfoot, and the plane used for surgical amputation of the foot.

Intrinsic muscles & the plantar aponeurosis

On the dorsum, the intrinsics are extensor hallucis brevis and extensor digitorum brevis (deep fibular nerve). On the sole, the plantar intrinsics lie in four layers and flex, adduct and abduct the digits, all bound by the tough plantar aponeurosis that protects the sole and supports the arch.

Reasoning · why joint type tells you the movement

Shape sets freedom. The many plane joints between tarsals allow only small gliding shifts — but summed across the midfoot they let the foot mould to uneven ground. The hinge interphalangeal joints give the toes simple flex/extend; the condyloid MTP joints add some side-to-side splay. Read the type and you can predict what each joint contributes to a foot that must be both a rigid lever and an adaptable platform.

R Recall checklist — foot bones & joints
  • Tarsals — talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, 3 cuneiforms.
  • Transverse tarsal joint — calcaneocuboid + talocalcaneonavicular; site of surgical amputation.
  • MTP joints — condyloid; IP joints — hinge.
  • Plantar intrinsics — four layers; bound by the plantar aponeurosis.
§3Ankle & subtalar joints

The ankle & subtalar joints

A hinge that is tightest when dorsiflexed — and sprains that follow the weaker ligaments.

The ankle (talocrural) joint is a hinge between the mortise and the talus; the subtalar joint beneath it turns the sole in and out. Their movements and their ligaments explain the commonest injury in the body.

§3.1Movements & stability

  • Dorsiflexion — anterior compartment muscles.
  • Plantar flexion — posterior compartment muscles.
  • Inversion & eversion happen at the subtalar joint (talus on calcaneus, held by interosseous talocalcaneal ligaments), not the ankle hinge.

The ankle is more stable in dorsiflexion. The talar trochlea is wider anteriorly and narrower posteriorly, so dorsiflexion draws the wide part into the mortise and wedges it tight; plantar flexion brings the narrow part in, loosening the grip and allowing a little side-to-side play.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

In which position is a sudden twist most likely to sprain the ankle — dorsiflexed, or plantarflexed?

Plantarflexed. The narrow posterior trochlea sits in the mortise, so the joint is loose and can wobble — which is why so many sprains happen going down stairs or landing on tip-toe, when the ankle is plantarflexed and unstable.

§3.2Ligaments & injuries

The medial side is guarded by the strong, fan-shaped deltoid ligament; the lateral side by three weaker lateral ligaments (anterior talofibular, calcaneofibular, posterior talofibular).

The ankle is the most frequently injured major joint. A sprain (tearing ligament fibres) is nearly always an inversion injury, tearing the weaker lateral ligaments. Forced eversion is resisted by the very strong deltoid ligament, so instead of tearing it, the pull avulses the medial malleolus, destabilising the joint and letting the talus move — the mechanism of a Pott's fracture-dislocation.

Reasoning · why inversion sprains, but eversion fractures

A joint fails at its weakest link. On inversion, the load falls on the thin lateral ligaments (especially the anterior talofibular) and they tear — a sprain. On eversion, the deltoid ligament is stronger than the bone it pulls on, so the force is transmitted to the medial malleolus, which snaps off instead. Same joint, opposite mechanisms — ligament tear one way, bone avulsion the other.

R Recall checklist — ankle & subtalar
  • Ankle — hinge; dorsiflexion (anterior) + plantar flexion (posterior).
  • Subtalar — talus on calcaneus; inversion & eversion.
  • Stability — most stable in dorsiflexion (wide anterior trochlea wedges in).
  • Ligaments — strong medial deltoid; weaker lateral (ATFL, CFL, PTFL).
  • Injury — sprain = inversion (lateral ligaments); forced eversion → medial malleolus avulsion (Pott's).
§4Arches

Arches of the foot

Springs that carry the body — held up by bone shape, ligaments and muscle.

The foot is not a flat plate but a set of arches — medial and lateral longitudinal arches and a transverse arch — that distribute body weight and absorb shock as a spring. They are held up by three things: bone shape, ligaments, and muscle/tendon support.

  • Plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament — supports the head of the talus and is key to maintaining the medial longitudinal arch.
  • Long plantar ligament — the longest; forms a tunnel for the fibularis longus tendon.
  • Short plantar (plantar calcaneocuboid) ligament and the plantar aponeurosis — add further support.

Dynamic support comes from muscles whose tendons sling under the arch — notably tibialis posterior and fibularis longus. When support fails, the arch drops (pes planus, flat foot); an abnormally high arch is pes cavus.

Clinical / real-world · the spring ligament & tibialis posterior

The medial arch depends on the spring ligament and the tibialis posterior tendon that reinforces it. Tibialis posterior dysfunction is the commonest cause of acquired adult flat foot: as the tendon fails, the spring ligament is overloaded, the talar head drops, and the medial arch collapses into pes planus — a direct clinical read-out of this section's anatomy.

R Recall checklist — arches
  • Arches — medial & lateral longitudinal + transverse; distribute weight, absorb shock.
  • Spring ligament — plantar calcaneonavicular; supports the talar head, maintains the medial longitudinal arch.
  • Long plantar ligament — tunnel for the fibularis longus tendon.
  • Failure — dropped arch = pes planus; high arch = pes cavus.
