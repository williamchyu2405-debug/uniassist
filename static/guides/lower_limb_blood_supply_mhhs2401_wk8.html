§0Orientation

The arterial highway of the lower limb

One vessel, renamed at each landmark — learn the landmarks and the names follow.

Just as in the arm, the lower limb is essentially one continuous artery that changes name as it crosses anatomical landmarks. Learn the sequence and the whole supply falls into place; then trace the two returns — venous and lymphatic — back out.

The main arterial pathway and its name-changes
ArteryBecomes…At…
External iliacFemoral arteryThe inguinal ligament
FemoralPopliteal arteryThe adductor hiatus
PoplitealAnterior + posterior tibialBelow the knee
Anterior tibialDorsalis pedisThe ankle / dorsum of foot
Reasoning · why one vessel, many names

A single main channel is simplest to supply a limb, but surgeons and clinicians need to name where along it they are working — so the artery is renamed at the fixed landmarks (inguinal ligament, adductor hiatus, knee) that you can find on a body. Learn the landmark and both the name and the accessible pulse point come with it.

R Recall checklist — the highway
  • Order — external iliac → femoral → popliteal → anterior/posterior tibial → foot.
  • Name-changes — inguinal ligament (→ femoral); adductor hiatus (→ popliteal).
  • Two returns — venous (superficial + deep) and lymphatic (superficial + deep).
§1Thigh

Femoral triangle, profunda femoris & adductor canal

The gateway into the thigh, the chief artery of the thigh, and the tunnel to the knee.

§1.1The femoral triangle

The femoral triangle is the entry gateway of the thigh (bounded by the inguinal ligament, sartorius and adductor longus). Its neurovascular contents, from medial to lateral, are the femoral Vein, Artery and Nerve — "VAN". The femoral pulse is felt by pressing backward at the midpoint between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis, best with the thigh flexed, abducted and laterally rotated.

Memory hook · VAN, medial → lateral

"VAN drives medial to lateral." Femoral Vein, Artery, Nerve — the vein is most medial (nearest the pubis), the nerve most lateral. So the artery (the pulse) sits between them, and a needle aimed just medial to the pulse finds the vein.

Clinical / real-world · femoral hernia

Medial to the vein is the femoral canal, whose upper opening (the femoral ring) is a weak point of the abdominal wall. A femoral hernia pushes through it and appears as a mass inferolateral to the pubic tubercle — the key sign distinguishing it from an inguinal hernia (which is superomedial to the tubercle). It is commoner in females (broader pelvic inlet) and readily strangulates because the ring is narrow.

§1.2Profunda femoris & the adductor canal

The profunda femoris (deep femoral artery) is the largest branch of the femoral artery — arising from its lateral/posterior side in the triangle — and the chief artery of the thigh. It gives:

  • 3–4 perforating arteries that wrap around the back of the femur and supply muscles of all three compartments.
  • Medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries that ring the proximal femur and supply the head and neck of the femur (via posterior retinacular arteries) — the medial circumflex is the dominant supply.

The obturator artery (from the internal iliac) helps supply the adductors. Beyond the triangle, the femoral vessels run in the adductor canal (~15 cm, apex of the triangle → adductor hiatus in adductor magnus), carrying the femoral artery, femoral vein and saphenous nerve. The vessels exit through the hiatus to become the popliteal vessels; the saphenous nerve leaves to accompany the great saphenous vein.

Clinical / real-world · the femoral-neck blood supply

Because the medial circumflex femoral artery (a profunda branch) is the main supply to the femoral head, a displaced femoral neck fracture tears its retinacular vessels and risks avascular necrosis of the head — tying this artery straight back to the hip lecture.

R Recall checklist — thigh vessels
  • Femoral triangle — contents medial→lateral = Vein, Artery, Nerve (VAN); pulse at the mid-inguinal point.
  • Femoral hernia — through the femoral ring; mass inferolateral to the pubic tubercle; commoner in females.
  • Profunda femoris — chief artery of the thigh; perforating + circumflex femoral branches.
  • Medial circumflex femoral — main supply to the femoral head.
  • Adductor canal — femoral artery, vein & saphenous nerve; ends at the adductor hiatus.
§2Knee, leg & foot

Popliteal fossa & the arteries of the leg and foot

The diamond behind the knee, and the pulses that report the limb's circulation.

Emerging from the adductor hiatus, the artery crosses the popliteal fossa behind the knee, then divides to supply the leg and foot — giving the distal pulses used to assess the whole limb's blood flow.

Popliteal fossa — boundaries & contents

  • Superomedial — semitendinosus and semimembranosus.
  • Superolateral — biceps femoris.
  • Inferior — the medial and lateral heads of gastrocnemius.

Contents from superficial to deep: tibial nerve → popliteal vein → popliteal artery (the artery is deepest, against the joint capsule). A rich anastomosis around the knee provides collateral flow.

Leg & foot arteries and their pulses

The popliteal artery divides into the anterior tibial artery (which becomes the dorsalis pedis on the dorsum) and the posterior tibial artery (which gives the fibular artery and continues to the sole). Four pulses report the highway:

Lower-limb pulse points
PulseWhere to feel it
FemoralMidpoint of ASIS to pubic symphysis
PoplitealDeep in the popliteal fossa (knee flexed)
Posterior tibialBehind the medial malleolus (midway to the heel)
Dorsalis pedisDorsum of foot, lateral to the EHL tendon
Reasoning · why the popliteal artery is deepest (and its pulse hard to feel)

The popliteal artery is the direct continuation coming through the adductor hiatus onto the back of the femur, so it necessarily lies deepest, against the bone and capsule, with the vein and nerve stacked more superficially. That depth makes its pulse hard to feel (you must flex the knee and press firmly) and, clinically, means a posterior knee dislocation can tear it — a limb-threatening emergency.

Clinical / real-world · intermittent claudication

Narrowing of these arteries by atherosclerosis (peripheral arterial disease) starves the leg muscles on exertion, producing intermittent claudication — cramping calf or thigh pain that comes on with walking and is relieved by rest. Checking the four pulses (and comparing sides) is how the level of disease is localised at the bedside.

R Recall checklist — popliteal, leg & foot
  • Popliteal boundaries — semitendinosus/semimembranosus (superomedial), biceps femoris (superolateral), gastrocnemius heads (inferior).
  • Contents — superficial→deep: tibial nerve, popliteal vein, popliteal artery.
  • Branches — anterior tibial → dorsalis pedis; posterior tibial → fibular artery.
  • Pulses — femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial (behind medial malleolus), dorsalis pedis (lateral to EHL).
  • Claudication — exertional leg pain from peripheral arterial disease.
§3Venous return

Venous drainage

Two systems — superficial and deep — linked by valved perforators.

Venous return from the leg uses two sets of channels that communicate freely:

  • Superficial veins — the great (long) and small (short) saphenous veins, running in the superficial fascia.
  • Deep veins — venae comitantes that follow the arteries as paired veins within the muscle compartments.

The great saphenous vein ascends the medial side of the limb (from the dorsal venous arch, passing anterior to the medial malleolus) to drain into the femoral vein at the saphenous opening; the small saphenous vein ascends the posterior calf to the popliteal vein. Perforating veins link the two systems, their valves allowing flow only from superficial to deep.

Clinical / real-world · varicose veins, DVT & PE

When the valves fail, blood pools and the superficial veins dilate into tortuous varicose veins. In the deep system, stasis and hypercoagulability cause deep venous thrombosis (DVT), and a fragment can embolise to the lungs as a pulmonary embolism (PE) — the reason calf swelling and tenderness are taken seriously. The great saphenous vein is also a favourite for surgery: a landmark for venous "cutdown" anterior to the medial malleolus, and a graft for coronary bypass.

R Recall checklist — venous drainage
  • Two sets — superficial (great & small saphenous, in superficial fascia) + deep (venae comitantes in the compartments).
  • Great saphenous — up the medial limb → femoral vein.
  • Small saphenous — up the posterior calf → popliteal vein.
  • Perforators — valves allow flow only superficial → deep.
  • Disease — valve failure → varicose veins; deep stasis → DVT → PE.
§4Lymphatics

Lymphatic drainage

Two sets of vessels, converging on the inguinal nodes.

Like the veins, the lymphatics come in two sets — superficial and deep lymph vessels and nodes — and both ultimately drain to the inguinal lymph nodes in the groin (the deep set also passing through popliteal nodes behind the knee).

Clinical / real-world · lymphangitis & lymphoedema

Infection tracking along the lymph vessels causes lymphangitis — tender red streaks running proximally toward the groin with enlarged inguinal nodes, a sign that infection is spreading. If lymph drainage is obstructed (after node removal, infection or filariasis), fluid accumulates and the limb swells with lymphoedema. Because the groin nodes drain the whole limb (and perineum), enlarged inguinal nodes are a routine part of examining the lower limb.

R Recall checklist — lymphatic drainage
  • Two sets — superficial + deep lymph vessels and nodes.
  • Destination — the inguinal nodes (deep set via popliteal nodes).
  • Lymphangitis — inflamed lymph vessels; red streaks tracking proximally.
