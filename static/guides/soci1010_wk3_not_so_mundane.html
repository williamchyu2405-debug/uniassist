Not So Mundane — the thinkers who make the everyday strange

This week is the theoretical toolkit. It runs through the sociologists whose concepts let us unpack the everyday — Weber, Hochschild, Simmel, Lefebvre, Foucault, Garfinkel and Goffman — plus a framework for studying ordinary events systematically and a worked case (the footpath). The aim, in the lecturer’s words, is to remind ourselves the world is not as natural and inevitable as it appears, but socially constructed. These notes cover the lecture, the slides, and the Week-3 reading (Scott on the swimming pool). These are the concepts to reach for in the reflection essay.

§0Framing

Not so mundane

Why the routine, familiar and repetitive is worth theorising.

The everyday looks like nothing worth studying — which is exactly the point. Highmore asks: “What’s going on when nothing much is happening?” and Finkelstein notes that “what we all do everyday, what we take for granted and can rarely explain, are the most interesting things about us.” The Week-3 warning from Susie Scott: although the sites of everyday life are marked by repetition, routine and familiarity, this does not make them devoid of political interests, ideologies and subtle forms of social control.

Why study the everyday?
ReasonWhat it means
How society functionsDurkheim’s moral consensus — the shared norms essential to society’s smooth functioning.
Hidden rulesThe norms & expectations of everyday practices — tied to political interests, ideology, power and social control.
Site of conflictThe everyday is where class, gender, race and sexuality are contested.
A new point of viewBreak from familiar routines — move from the individual’s problems to the social circumstances producing them (the sociological imagination).
Reasoning · reflexivity — why the everyday makes itself

The everyday is not just something that happens to us; we continuously remake it. This is reflexivity: people are shaped by interaction and shape it in turn. First day at work in jeans while everyone’s in suits — the manager’s comment and colleagues’ reactions teach you the dress norm (society shapes you); but if enough employees push for casual Friday and the workplace accepts it, the meaning of ‘appropriate attire’ shifts (you shape society). Thoughts shape interaction, and interaction reshapes thoughts and identities — the loop that keeps social order both stable and changeable.

Memory hook · the wager of the week

The world is not natural or inevitable — it is socially constructed. Theory is the tool that makes the constructed-ness visible.

R Recall checklist — not so mundane
  • Highmore / Finkelstein — the taken-for-granted is the most revealing thing about us.
  • Scott’s warning — routine sites still carry power, ideology and social control.
  • Four reasons — moral consensus (Durkheim); hidden rules; site of conflict; the sociological imagination.
  • Reflexivity — we are shaped by interaction and shape it (the workplace-dress example).
§1A framework

Studying the everyday analytically

Sztompka’s systematic way to read any social event.

To study the everyday analytically rather than just describe it, Piotr Sztompka (2008) offers five things to attend to in any social event. It is the checklist to run over the reflection-essay images: who, where, in what context, in what kind of place, and in what larger configuration.

Sztompka’s five points for analysing a social event
#Attend to…Key questions / notes
1Participants & rolesWho are the actors and their roles? Direct face-to-face co-presence, or distant/mediated (phone, internet, social media)? Number of participants is key (a dyad differs from a crowd; turn-taking).
2The immediate situationSome are directly involved, others are bystanders/witnesses. Their presence distinguishes private from public events (we lower our voice among strangers; a crowd can inflame conduct).
3The wider social contextFamily, work, consumption, education, religion, leisure, sport — each demands different behaviour, dress, language, emotional tone.
4Locales / arenasEvents in the same kind of place have similar content — we read at the library, pray at worship, walk/shop on the street.
5Complex configurationsDifferent people in different roles perform together in a coordinated way — a wedding, funeral, surgery, the lecture theatre — often following set procedures.
Reasoning · rites of passage, rituals & projects

Everyday life is stitched together by small rites of passage that mark the change of context: logging on at work, putting on slippers or having a drink at home, changing shoes for the tennis club, pyjamas before bed — plus the linguistic bookends “how’s it going?” on entry and “see you later” on exit. When symbolic gesture matters more than practical outcome — how something is done over what is done — we have a ritual (purest in religion, but also in politics and the military). Michael Billig’s banal nationalism is the everyday version: the routine raising of a flag quietly reproduces national belonging. And events that run over many planned steps toward an intended end are projects — a degree, a career, this very commute. The mundane is scaffolded by ritual, and ritual is where meaning hides.

R Recall checklist — analysing the everyday
  • Sztompka’s 5 — participants/roles; immediate situation (bystanders → public vs private); wider context; locales; complex configurations.
  • Co-presence — direct face-to-face vs distant/mediated; number of participants matters.
  • Rites of passage — small rituals mark transitions between contexts.
  • Banal nationalism (Billig) — e.g. flag-raising; projects — multi-step everyday journeys (a degree).
§2Worked case

The footpath & the politics of public space

A whole book about the sidewalk — and it’s political.

Nothing seems more trivial than the footpath — yet the urban geographer Nicholas Blomley wrote a whole book on it (Rights of Passage: Sidewalks and the Regulation of Public Flow). The footpath is historically recent (Melbourne advised pedestrians to keep right as early as 1863; footpaths proliferated once cars made roads dangerous), and it is governed by an unspoken logic Blomley calls pedestrianism.

Reasoning · pedestrianism — flow over assembly

We assume the sidewalk is a democratic public space where you can do as you like. Blomley shows it is really treated as a logistical channel: “pedestrianism understands the sidewalk as a finite public resource always threatened by multiple competing interests,” whose golden rule is unhindered passage — the orderly movement of bodies from A to B. So loiterers “without authorization” can be moved on (huddling reads as ‘visual clutter’ and obstruction); cafés must apply for a permit to place chairs; there’s no cycling on the footpath. This is a pointed revision of the usual civic-humanist view of the sidewalk as a space of democracy and protest: to the city’s engineers, it is a transport corridor, and political consciousness that halts the flow is treated like stalled traffic.

Everyday case · Jim Crow & the gentrifying street

Public-space rules are never neutral. Under Jim Crow segregation (1877–1950s, US South), racial etiquette required Black pedestrians to step off the sidewalk or “give whites the wall” — the footpath encoding a racial order. And streets change: the LSE’s Ordinary Streets study of Rye Lane, Peckham shows urban mutualism (a shop subdivided into tiny compatible units — a migrant starts at a 1-metre table and ends up owning the building) meeting external forces of gentrification and regeneration. Read closely, an ordinary street carries history, biography, migration and power.

R Recall checklist — the footpath
  • Blomley — Rights of Passage; the sidewalk is recent and politically governed.
  • Pedestrianism — the unspoken logic prioritising smooth flow (A→B) over democratic assembly.
  • Consequences — move-on for loitering; café permits; no protest that halts flow.
  • Never neutral — Jim Crow sidewalk etiquette; Peckham’s urban mutualism vs gentrification.
§3Theorist 1

Max Weber: rationalisation

Efficiency, bureaucracy, and the iron cage.

Max Weber (German, 1864–1920) argued in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism that Protestant (especially Calvinist) values — hard work, discipline, self-control, frugality, and duty to one’s calling — fostered a mindset that aligned with capitalism’s rise. He is not saying Protestantism caused capitalism, but that its values encouraged a rational, disciplined attitude to work. From this grows his master theme: rationalisation — the process by which modern life becomes organised around calculation, efficiency, rules and systems.

Weber’s four dimensions of rationalisation
DimensionMeaning
EfficiencyMaximum results for minimum effort.
PredictabilityA desire to know what will happen in future.
CalculabilityA concern with numbers — statistics, scoring.
Dehumanisation / controlUsing technology to control human behaviour.
Reasoning · bureaucracy — rationalisation’s highest form

Bureaucracy is the purest expression of rationalisation: a system built on efficiency, rules and calculation rather than personal ties. Weber gives it five features — (1) formal rules and procedures (apply for a licence: fill this form, do this test); (2) hierarchy of authority (each level supervised by the one above); (3) specialisation (people assigned roles by training/qualification — tutors, lecturers, deans; the McDonald’s line); (4) impersonality (universal rules, no favouritism — often not even a person, but a portal); (5) records and documentation (birth certificates, passports, files that track you). The point: modern authority attaches to the role and its rules, not the person.

Everyday case · the iron cage

Weber warned that over-rationalisation traps us: the iron cage, or the “irrationality of rationality.” When your bank card fails, you try everything online, go to the branch — and the teller sends you back to log in and fix it yourself. The rules meant to serve efficiency end up imprisoning the person. (We meet this again with fast food’s efficiency/predictability/calculability/control.)

Memory hook · Weber’s spine

Protestant ethic → rationalisation (efficiency · predictability · calculability · control) → bureaucracy (obey the role) → the iron cage.

R Recall checklist — Weber
  • Protestant Ethic — Calvinist values aligned with (did not cause) capitalism.
  • Rationalisation — efficiency, predictability, calculability, dehumanisation/control.
  • Bureaucracy’s 5 — formal rules; hierarchy; specialisation; impersonality; records.
  • Iron cage — the irrationality of rationality; imprisoned by the rules.
§4Theorist 2

Arlie Hochschild: emotional labour

When feelings are rationalised and sold for a wage.

Extending Weber’s rationalisation to feeling, the American sociologist Arlie Hochschild (The Managed Heart, 1983) coined emotional labour: “the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display; emotional labour is sold for a wage and therefore has exchange value.” Workers — flight attendants, carers, retail and hospitality staff — are paid to manage their own emotions to produce a desired emotional state in customers.

The components of emotional labour
ComponentWhat it is
Display rulesOrganisationally prescribed guidelines — how you must express emotion (cabin crew appear calm even in emergencies).
Feeling rulesBroader societal norms about what one should feel in a situation.
Surface actingModifying outward expression only — a smile plastered over annoyance.
Deep actingTrying to actually feel the required emotion (the insurance seller who seems genuinely to care).
Reasoning · why emotional labour matters

If Weber’s worry was that calculation crowds out spontaneity, Hochschild shows the cost lands on the worker’s inner life. Three consequences: work intensification (managing feeling is mental work on top of physical/cognitive work); the authenticity dilemma (a gap opens between the ‘genuine self’ and the ‘work self’ — am I the real person or the role?); and occupational health (chronic emotional labour causes exhaustion, burnout and depersonalisation, especially in care work). Our emotions in public are increasingly policed — which is why profanity on a train now feels shocking.

Memory hook · display vs feeling, surface vs deep

Display rules = the company’s rules; feeling rules = society’s. Surface acting = fake the face; deep acting = change the feeling.

R Recall checklist — Hochschild
  • The Managed Heart (1983) — emotional labour = managing feeling for a wage (exchange value).
  • Display rules (organisational) vs feeling rules (societal).
  • Surface acting (outward only) vs deep acting (feel it for real).
  • Why it matters — work intensification; authenticity dilemma; burnout/depersonalisation.
§5Theorist 3

Georg Simmel: forms of interaction

The stranger, the metropolis, and money.

Georg Simmel (German, 1858–1918) studied the forms of social interaction rather than their content: “there is no such thing as society ‘as such’… there are only specific kinds of interaction.” He works bottom-up from small-scale interaction to see how larger institutions emerge — noticing, for instance, that the number of parties changes the interaction (a two-person dyad differs from a three-plus group).

Three of Simmel’s key studies
IdeaThe argument
The StrangerA figure who is both near and far at once — distance and closeness combined — a distinct role in modern life.
Metropolis & Mental LifeThe city’s overstimulation forces a protective blasé attitude and reserve; anonymity frees individuality (subcultures) yet makes standing out hard.
The Philosophy of MoneyMoney makes relationships instrumental and calculable, reducing quality to quantity — freedom bought at the price of thinned, fragmented ties.
Reasoning · the metropolis — freedom and its price

Moving from the close-knit rural village to the sprawling modern city (London, Paris) makes you anonymous — nobody on George Street. Simmel says this cuts two ways. Good: anonymity releases individuality — the village polices conformity, but the city lets punks, subcultures and varied identities emerge, and frees the personal (religious, political, sexual) from scrutiny. Hard: if everyone is unusual, it’s hard to stand out, and the sheer overstimulation forces the blasé attitude — senses numbed, encounters made short and functional, people apathetic. We gain individual freedom but at the cost of alienation and fragmentation.

Everyday case · money trivialises the tie

In The Philosophy of Money, Simmel argues money makes calculative rationality pervade interaction: everything has a price, and we relate to people through single activities (paying for the coffee, lending money). You can’t have the old butcher-shop yarn about “how Daisy the cow is doing” — the sheer number of people and high turnover reduce ties to a superficial “hi, latte, bye.” The greater the range of people we depend on, the more anonymous and interchangeable they become — and the more we are thrown back on our own autonomous personality.

R Recall checklist — Simmel
  • Forms not content — study kinds of interaction; the number of parties matters (dyad vs triad).
  • The Stranger — near and far at once.
  • Metropolis & Mental Life — overstimulation → the blasé attitude & reserve; anonymity frees yet isolates.
  • Philosophy of Money — instrumental, calculable ties; quantity over quality; freedom vs fragmentation.
§6Theorists 4 & 5

Space & power: Lefebvre and Foucault

Who produces space — and who is watching.

Two French thinkers turn our attention to space and power in the everyday. Henri Lefebvre (Marxist philosopher, 1901–1991) argued the everyday was dismissed as trivial since the Enlightenment, which prized the ‘higher’ faculties of reason (art, philosophy, science). In The Production of Space (1974) he insists space is not a neutral container but is socially produced, via a triad.

Lefebvre’s spatial triad
ElementWhat it is
Spatial practiceThe social practices through which space is materially produced (the meaning we attribute to a space in use).
Representations of spaceHow space is abstractly conceived — the plans of engineers, planners, cartographers.
Representational spacesThe lived, phenomenological space of experience — how we sense and inhabit it.
Reasoning · abstract space & the drive-through

Lefebvre’s Marxist point: capitalism produces abstract space in which mental/planned space dominates natural and social space. The drive-through is the perfect case — a large, unkempt, concrete space with one purpose (let motorists order, collect, leave) that exists because US car-culture prioritised private transport and lets the outlet extract more profit. Space is increasingly controlled by engineers and planners, privatised, and organised for productive consumption rather than for community — apartment blocks stacked with units but no parks or green space. Space is political, not given.

Everyday case · Foucault’s panopticon & internalised power

Michel Foucault (1926–1984) traces, in Discipline and Punish, a shift in how power works. In the Middle Ages, punishment was public torture — power was visible and sat outside the body (the king’s spectacle deterring the crowd). The modern prison’s panopticon (a central watchtower from which any cell might be observed) inverts this: because you never know if you are being watched, you internalise the gaze and discipline yourself. Power becomes invisible and productive, not merely repressive. The classroom is the everyday version — six weeks in, the teacher need only stand still and the children self-regulate. (Foucault also gives us discourse, biopower and governmentality.)

R Recall checklist — Lefebvre & Foucault
  • Lefebvre — The Production of Space (1974); space is socially produced, not neutral.
  • Spatial triad — spatial practice (material) · representations of space (conceived/planned) · representational spaces (lived).
  • Abstract space — capitalist, planner-controlled, privatised (the drive-through).
  • Foucault — Discipline & Punish; visible power (public torture) → invisible power (the panopticon) → self-discipline.
§7Theorists 6 & 7a

Making order visible: Garfinkel & symbolic interactionism

Break the rule to see the rule.

Harold Garfinkel founded ethnomethodology (Studies in Ethnomethodology, 1967) — ethno = people, method = means, ology = study of: the study of how people make sense and meaning out of the potential chaos of social life. Its crucial claim: social life appears orderly but is in fact potentially chaotic, and order is continually created and re-created in everyday interaction — an actor-centred approach turning on reflexivity and agency.

Reasoning · breaching experiments

How do you study rules that are invisible because everyone follows them? Garfinkel’s method is the breaching experiment — deliberately breaking a rule of interaction to expose it by the reaction it provokes. His famous Case 6: to the casual “How are you?” the student replies, “How am I in regard to what? My health, my finances, my schoolwork…?” — and the questioner, red in the face, snaps that they were “just trying to be polite.” The breach makes the tacit rule roar into view. Other everyday breaches: standing too close to a stranger in a queue; sitting right beside someone on a half-empty bus; slamming a door in the face of the person behind you. We keep order by quietly enforcing rules we can’t normally see.

Everyday case · symbolic interactionism

The companion micro-approach — symbolic interactionism (Chicago School, 1960s) — holds that people act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them; those meanings arise from interaction with others and can be modified through interpretation. We rely on symbols to give meaning to stimuli, and we reflect on ourselves and thereby shape our own actions. Formal norms are society’s written rules; informal norms are the unwritten ones — and it is the unwritten rules that ethnomethodology and interactionism specialise in.

R Recall checklist — Garfinkel & SI
  • Ethnomethodology (Garfinkel, 1967) — how people make sense of social life; order is achieved, not given.
  • Breaching experiments — break a rule to reveal it (Case 6 0how are you?1; queue/bus/door).
  • Formal vs informal norms — written vs unwritten rules.
  • Symbolic interactionism (Chicago) — act on meanings; meanings from interaction, modified by interpretation; symbols.
§8Theorist 7b + reading

Goffman: dramaturgy & the swimming pool

Life as theatre — front stage, back stage, and the polite fiction.

Erving Goffman (Canadian, 1922–1982) is renowned for unmasking how we ‘present the self’. In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959) he offers a dramaturgical model: social life is like theatre. Interactions involve the performance of roles, and the situations shaping them are the stages on which social life is enacted.

Goffman’s dramaturgy
TermMeaning
Front stageWhere we perform to an audience, following the role’s conventions — the waiter attentive at the table.
Back stageWhere the performance relaxes and is prepared — the waiter’s manner in the kitchen.
Impression managementThe work of controlling the impression others form of us.
Interaction orderThe domain of face-to-face co-presence — how people “do things together” — studied by micro-analysis.
Reasoning · interaction as ritual

Where Durkheim (Elementary Forms of Religious Life) tied ritual to separating the sacred from the profane, Goffman argues ritual is not just religious — all interaction consists of ‘interactive rituals’. A greeting is a ritual: it repeats, and has explicit rules about what is acceptable. The Curb Your Enthusiasm “dismissive bow” makes the point — a bow of the wrong depth reads as “not sorry at all,” a ‘shit bow’; the ritual’s precise form carries the meaning. Goffman’s micro-analysis forces us to notice the concrete ways things get done: gesture, eye contact, bodily comportment, speech — and how these shape our identities.

Everyday case · the swimming pool as a negotiated order (Scott 2009)

The Week-3 reading, Susie Scott’s “Re-clothing the Emperor”, applies Goffman, Foucault and symbolic interactionism to the pool. Strangers in near-nakedness and co-presence somehow produce an orderly scene — a negotiated order (Strauss) upheld by a polite fiction nobody names. Scott identifies three tacit norms: (1) respect for personal space, (2) respect for individuals’ disciplinary regimes (their ‘body projects’ and lane discipline), and (3) the desexualisation of encounters. Like the emperor’s subjects who dare not admit they see nothing, swimmers cannot consciously attend to the precarious reality they are jointly constructing, yet remain poised to defend it — the everyday order is a shared performance.

Memory hook · Goffman on a stage

Front stage = performing to the audience; back stage = out of role. We’re all doing impression management — and every greeting is a little ritual.

R Recall checklist — Goffman & the pool
  • Dramaturgy — Presentation of Self (1959); performance of roles on stages.
  • Front stage / back stage (the waiter); impression management; the interaction order.
  • Interactive rituals — all interaction is ritual (greetings; the 0dismissive bow1); Durkheim sacred/profane.
  • Scott’s pool — a negotiated order & polite fiction; 3 norms: personal space, disciplinary regimes, desexualisation.

Week 3 in one line. The everyday is ‘not so mundane’: a battery of thinkers gives us the tools to unpack it — Weber’s rationalisation and iron cage, Hochschild’s emotional labour, Simmel’s forms and blasé metropolis, Lefebvre’s produced space and Foucault’s internalised power, Garfinkel’s breaching experiments, and Goffman’s dramaturgy — all showing that a world which feels natural is in fact a socially constructed, power-laden performance we jointly sustain.
