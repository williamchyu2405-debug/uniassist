SOCI1010 · Week 2 · The Meaning of Everyday Life — A/Prof Selvaraj Velayutham (Raj)

The Meaning of Everyday Life — repetition, ritual, and the making of modernity

Week 1 asked what the everyday is; Week 2 asks what it means and where it comes from. First we pin the everyday down through seven characteristics, then read two ordinary sites — the bus stop and the queue — as loaded social systems, and finally place the whole thing inside modernity, following the essential reading (Inglis, Modern Culture and Everyday Life). The throughline: our ‘normal’ is one peculiar, historically specific way of living, shaped by rationalization on one side and chaos on the other.

§0Defining the field

What everyday life is (and isn’t)

Not the leftover of ‘important’ life — the whole of it.

A common mistake is to treat the everyday as the trivial residue left over once the ‘important’ stuff is removed. So it is worth being clear about what everyday life is not — and why the wide definition matters.

Reasoning · the everyday is not the opposite of the important

Everyday life is not confined to: important moments in life; major events and incidents; festivities; holidays; the merely ‘profane’; so-called ‘ordinary’ people; or private life. But — in the wide meaning of the term — all of these are episodes of everyday life. The concept should include both the profane and the sacred, the private and the public, and people from all walks of life. The everyday is not a small corner of experience; it is the medium in which all of experience is lived. (Think of life under COVID becoming the ‘new normal’ — a new everyday.)

The lecture ‘trip itinerary’ for Week 2 follows four stops: defining the everyday (its seven characteristics), the politics of waiting, the sociology of the queue, and situating the everyday in modernity. And, as Plate 0.1 shows, the everyday is now a whole scholarly field with its own library.

  • NOT just important moments, events, festivities, holidays, the profane, ‘ordinary’ people, or private life.
  • BUT the wide meaning includes profane and sacred, private and public, all walks of life.

§1The core framework

Seven characteristics of the everyday

Time, habit, space, normality, democracy, power, history.

The heart of Week 2 is a set of seven features that, together, characterize everyday life. Learn these with their theorists — they are prime quiz material.

The seven characteristics of everyday life
#CharacteristicKey idea / theorist
1Repetitive, punctuated by the unpredictableA temporal term; repetition forms identity — “we become who we are through acts of repetition” (Durkheim); can also enable creativity
2Synonymous with habitHabit = action + attitude; Mauss’ ‘techniques of the body’ & habitus; Bourdieu’s ‘durable dispositions’ shaped by class & gender

4The feel of ‘normality’Rituals & routines accepted as normal; shaped by cultural & economic forces
5Secular & democraticNo longer magical; democratic because no one escapes it — “even the Queen”

Reasoning · why habit does so much work (Mauss & Bourdieu)

Habit isn’t just a repeated action — it is also an attitude or state of mind. The anthropologist Marcel Mauss (‘Techniques of the Body’, 1914) noted we can distinguish “squatting mankind and sitting mankind… people with chairs and people without chairs”: even how we hold our bodies is learned and cultural, “assembled by and for social authority” (who sits where at the dinner table?). Mauss coined habitus — acquired ability, something stronger than habit. Pierre Bourdieu developed it as ‘durable dispositions’, taught not by words but by imitation, and strongly shaped by class and gender — governing everything from how we eat to how we decorate the home to the TV we watch. Habitus is how the social order gets inside the body and comes to feel like personal taste.

Bigger picture · ontological security & the politics of the ‘natural’

Home (characteristic 3) is the privileged symbol of everyday space — a taken-for-granted base from which we make forays into other worlds. Giddens calls the trust this gives us ontological security: confidence in the things and people we share our lives with, which lends stability and continuity to our identity. Yet (characteristic 6) that same ‘natural’-feeling home is inhabited by power: its gendered spaces, objects and hierarchies are political — as is the lecture room, with its spatial layout and teacher–student relationship.

Memory hook · the seven, chained

Time (repetition) → habit (habitus) → space (home/security) → normality → democratic (even the Queen) → political (feels natural, isn’t) → history (born with modernity).

R Recall checklist — the seven characteristics
  • 1 Time — repetitive but punctuated by the unpredictable; repetition forms identity (Durkheim).
  • 2 Habit — Mauss’ techniques of the body & habitus; Bourdieu’s durable dispositions (class/gender).
  • 3 Space — home as base; Giddens’ ontological security.
  • 4 Normality; 5 Secular & democratic (no one escapes it); 6 Political (feels natural but power-laden).
  • 7 History — an intellectual history; emerges as a category with modernity (Lefebvre).
§2Reading a site

The politics of waiting

The bus stop as contested public space.

Waiting for the bus seems like dead time in a neutral place. But the humble bus shelter is a dense political object — at once street furniture (or clutter?), a corporate space, and a social space.

Reasoning · who waits, and where, is never neutral

The bus stop is unglamorous, low-status and even disempowering, and it embodies social difference — waiting is something some people do far more than others. It raises pointed questions about the delivery of public services: the disproportionate spending of public money (Western Sydney, for instance, is highly car-dependent with poor transport infrastructure), and the privatization of public services, as advertising firms like JCDecaux and Adshel build and own the shelters, turning public space into commercialized, bland private/public space. Yet the counter-case is equally everyday: public transport is affordable and the most efficient mode of travel, it minimizes congestion, and it helps the climate. Even the shelter’s ads become contested — vandalism or social activism? (see the Brandalism Project, which subverts corporate advertising).

R Recall checklist — the politics of waiting
  • The bus stop — street furniture / corporate space (JCDecaux, Adshel) / social space at once.
  • Politics — low status, embodies social difference; unequal public spending (car-dependent Western Sydney).
  • Privatization — commercialized public space; ads contested (Brandalism).
  • Counter-case — affordable, efficient, cuts congestion, better for climate.
§3Reading a site

The sociology of the queue

An ‘embryonic social system’ with its own morality.

A queue looks like nothing — people standing in a line. Sociologically it is an embryonic social system: a small but real pattern of social order, whose violation (queue-jumping) is punishable. Plate 3.1 shows how differently a queue can be engineered.

Label Key — the queue as a designed social system
Old system (one line per desk)
A new arrival joins the currently shortest queue — but if it turns out slow, ‘the system is unfair’. Luck decides.
New system (single feeder line)
One queue feeds whichever desk frees up next. Processing is faster (the maths saved ~half a minute per person) and, crucially, it feels fairer — so people are less grumpy.
The lesson
Fairness is designed. The same crowd, re-organised, produces a different moral experience.
Reasoning · order without a ruler

The queue is self-managed at two levels — by the individual, and collectively by the ‘queue-community’. As Goffman put it, the individual must regulate their own actions in the presence of others; the other members’ eyes serve as an informal government of each person’s conduct. No official enforces the line, yet a moral order — ‘queue ethics’ — is produced and policed from within. Queuing is treated as exemplary behaviour of ‘civilized societies’, and it comes in many types (physical, virtual, mobile, informal, regulated). (For more: Schwartz, Queuing and Waiting, 1975. For a comic violation: the ‘chat and cut’ in Curb Your Enthusiasm.)

R Recall checklist — the sociology of the queue
  • Embryonic social system — a pattern of order; violation is punishable.
  • Self-managed — by the individual and the queue-community; Goffman: regulate oneself before others’ eyes.
  • Moral order / queue ethics; many types (physical, virtual, mobile, informal, regulated).
  • Saarbrücken — a single feeder line is faster and feels fairer; fairness is designed.
§4The reading — orientation

Situating the everyday: modernity

Our ‘normal’ is the culture of modern society.

The essential reading (Inglis, Modern Culture and Everyday Life, Ch.2) makes the core Week-2 claim: our everyday activities are not natural or universal but are expressive of the specific cultural characteristics of modernity. As Inglis (2005:40) puts it, “dealing with bureaucracies, making music, going to the supermarket, playing sport, driving a car, walking through the city, choosing clothes, decorating your home, going on holiday and saying ‘I love you’ to someone” are all shaped by modern culture.

Inglis’ method is to denaturalize the everyday. We each live “like a fish in our own cultural water”, taking our habits to be simply normal; comparing them across time and place reveals how strange and specific they are.

Pre-modern / traditional → modern society
Pre-modernModern
Authority / worldviewProvidence, religion, traditionProgress, science & rationality
Drivers—Enlightenment (17–18th c), industrialization, capitalism, the democratic state
SocietyRural, stable, slow-changingUrban, a break from the past, a new way of thinking
Reasoning · modernization vs modernity

Two terms that are easy to confuse and worth pinning down. Modernization = the process — the weakening of traditional patterns of living and their associated world-views. Modernity = the kind of society that came into being with industrialization and urbanization. As Hall et al (2003) summarise the shift: as cities grew there were changes in where and how people lived, worked, and spent leisure — “a growth in rationality, increasing routinization of everyday life, and the development of market society and individualism.” Those four — rationality, routinization, market society, individualism — are the cultural signature of the modern everyday.

R Recall checklist — situating the everyday
  • Inglis’ claim — everyday activities are expressive of modernity; the method is to denaturalize them.
  • Pre-modern → modern — from providence/religion/tradition to progress/science/rationality; Enlightenment, industrialization, capitalism, the state.
  • Modernization (the process) vs modernity (the society).
  • Signature — rationality, routinization, market society, individualism (Hall et al).
§5The reading — Weber

The rationalization of everyday life

Rules, roles and the iron cage.

For Max Weber (1864–1920), what makes modern Western culture unique is its exceptionally high level of rationality — no other society has been so organised around rational procedures for doing things.

Weber’s two types of rationality
ZweckrationalWertrational
Oriented toThe most efficient means to a given endAn ultimate value (moral or spiritual)
ExampleBusiness seeking profit, morality secondaryThe devout believer acting to glorify God
ModernityDominantIn retreat

Weber located capitalism’s origins in religious ideas — the Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905): the protestant valuing of hard work, of all work as holy, and the ‘disenchantment of the world’ (no more magic or miracles; science paves the way for progress). Once unleashed, rational calculation became the pivotal force of modern life.

Reasoning · why we obey the role, not the person (bureaucracy)

Rationalization changes how power works. Older societies governed through tradition (obey the king because it has always been so) or religion (obey because he is appointed by God). Modernity replaces these with legal-rational authority: we are governed not by the whims of despots but by the systematic application of rules and procedures. Its archetype is bureaucracy — literally ‘control through regulations’. The key move: I pay the tax inspector, and children obey the schoolteacher, not because of anyone’s personal qualities but because of the role and the rules it enforces. Officials become “embodied rules” — walking incarnations of their bureaucracy. Little wonder Weber thought bureaucratic principles had colonized huge tracts of everyday life.

Bigger picture · professionalization & the iron cage

Modern work culture is uniquely professionalized: only a credentialed ‘expert’ — a ‘doctor’, a ‘lawyer’ — is deemed competent, certified by educational bureaucracies that issue the paper that ‘proves’ it. That is why the exposure of a fake doctor provokes such horror. Weber’s dark verdict on all this rationalization is the iron cage: a bloodless world stripped of spiritual and moral content, peopled by “specialists without spirit, sensualists without heart”. The lecture adds a modern gloss (Ritzer’s ‘McDonaldization’): the drive for efficiency, predictability, calculability and dehumanization/control.

Memory hook · Weber in one line

Zweck = eZ (means-ends efficiency); Wert = Worth (values). Modernity swaps tradition/religion for rules → bureaucracy → the iron cage.

R Recall checklist — rationalization
  • Weber — modern West = uniquely rational.
  • Zweckrational (means-end) vs Wertrational (value-rational).
  • Protestant Ethic (1905) — capitalism’s religious origins; the disenchantment of the world.
  • Legal-rational authority → bureaucracy — we obey the role, not the person; it colonizes everyday life.

§6The reading — reach of reason

Rationalizing emotions, money & space

Reason reaches your feelings, your wallet and your streets.

The striking claim of the reading is that rationalization penetrates even the zones that feel most personal — our emotions, our relationships, and the very spaces we move through.

Reasoning · feeling rules — even emotions are bureaucratized

Modern work demands not just that you are professional but that you seem it. A doctor must manage a role ambivalence (Merton) — the detached scientist versus the caring carer — and even that human warmth is itself demanded and regulated by the medical bureaucracy. Airline cabin staff are drilled to display friendly, upbeat emotions (Hochschild): there are professionalized ‘feeling rules’ that make emotional responses prescribed and predictable — the hallmarks of bureaucracy. The friendly “have a nice day!” is emotion on a rulebook.

Bigger picture · Simmel’s money mentality

Weber’s contemporary Georg Simmel traced the same logic to money. Money is impersonal and universalizing — anyone, high or low, can use it — and it breeds a mentality of rational calculation that judges things and people by quantity, not quality. So our dealings with the supermarket cashier, the bus driver, the bank clerk become pure monetary transactions in which we are just another customer to be ‘processed’; the “have a nice day” is a thin friendly veneer over an impersonal exchange. This is the everyday texture of Weber’s iron cage.

Rationalizing space. The same rationality is built into our environments. In sport, Eichberg shows how loose pre-modern ball-games (played across the open land between villages) became standardized, rule-governed, demarcated pitches engineered for ‘goal results’ and ‘tension’ — and then commercialized for profit. On the roads, Lefebvre argues ‘car culture’ has colonized the city into rationalized ‘geometric space’, impoverishing the driver’s experience; Augé adds that motorways bypass the very places they name, so places become mere signs flashing by.

Memory hook · reason’s three reaches

Feelings (Hochschild’s feeling rules) · money (Simmel’s quantity-over-quality) · space (Eichberg’s pitch, Lefebvre’s road).

R Recall checklist — emotions, money & space
  • Feeling rules (Hochschild) — emotions prescribed at work; doctor’s role ambivalence (Merton).
  • Money (Simmel) — impersonal, universalizing; quantity over quality; monetary relationships (cashier, clerk).
  • Space — sport’s standardized pitch (Eichberg); car culture & geometric space (Lefebvre); places as signs (Augé).
§7The reading — the other face

Culture & chaos: modernity’s other face

Not only control — also flux, overload and style.

It would be one-sided to see modernity only as rational control. Inglis insists modern culture is dualistic — just as much about movement, change and uncertainty as about order.

Reasoning · ‘all that is solid melts into air’ (Marx) and the blasé attitude (Simmel)

Marx captured modernity’s flux: capitalist society allows nothing to remain stable — “all that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned”; the 19th-century city was in constant upheaval of buildings, fashions and ideas. Simmel asked how the individual copes with the metropolis’s bombardment of stimuli. The answer is the blasé attitude — a protective ‘seen it all’ detachment that blocks out all but the essential. Hence the paradox that the bigger the crowd, the lonelier we feel, and the unwritten city code: “leave me alone and I’ll leave you alone” (why asking a stranger for directions feels like an intrusion).

Bigger picture · revolt into style — individuality off the peg

Modernity holds two forces in tension: rationalization pushes us to be predictable and conform, while individualization unleashes the cult of the unique self (“Look at me, there is no-one like me”). Style — in clothes, haircut, interiors, music, cars — is a revolt against conformity. But Simmel’s twist is that style is also general to a group: goths, subcultures, ‘lifestyles’ let you buy individuality ‘off the peg’ — different enough to stand out, similar enough to belong, without inventing yourself from scratch. Home decoration works the same way. The deep paradox: uniqueness is the other side of sameness — the horror of two women in the same designer dress each thought was a one-off.

R Recall checklist — culture & chaos
  • Modernity is dualistic — rational control and flux/uncertainty.
  • Marx — “all that is solid melts into air”; constant change.
  • Simmel — the blasé attitude copes with stimuli overload; crowded yet lonely; the ‘leave me alone’ code.
  • Revolt into style — rationalization vs individualization; style as group-based ‘individuality off the peg’; uniqueness ↔ sameness.
§8The reading — the (post/late) modern

Authenticity & the (post/late) modern

Invented traditions, tourism, and the age of irony.

If modernity dissolves traditions, it also drives a restless search for the authentic — and, paradoxically, keeps manufacturing it.

Reasoning · invented traditions & the tourism paradox

Modernity destroys traditions but also invents them (Hobsbawm & Ranger’s ‘invented traditions’): the ‘traditional family Christmas’ is a Victorian creation; ‘timeless’ Scottish tartan and bagpipes were assembled in the early 19th century — myths of stability offered to a public unsettled by change. Tourism is the mass search for the exotic and authentic as an escape from the mundane everyday. Its paradox: tourists destroy what they seek — the ‘quaint’ fishing village re-orients itself around the tourist dollar and commodifies its own culture. The ‘traveller’ who scorns the ‘tourist’ chases the same authenticity to ever more obscure places — until those, too, are commercialized.

Bigger picture · the post-tourist and the age of irony

The post-tourist is taken as emblematic of what some call post-modernity (Lyotard) and others late modernity (Giddens): rather than chasing the ‘real’, they knowingly and ironically embrace the fake, accepting that no uncommodified ‘reality’ is left. This culture runs on irony, knowingness and self-consciousness — pastiche, parody and bricolage (Jameson; think Tarantino replaying old genres). Umberto Eco’s example nails it: a man cannot say “I love you madly” to a cultivated woman without it sounding like a cliché, so he says “As Barbara Cartland would put it, I love you madly” — sincerity smuggled back in through self-conscious irony. (But Inglis cautions: such irony is unevenly distributed across classes and contexts.)

R Recall checklist — authenticity & the post/late modern
  • Invented traditions (Hobsbawm & Ranger) — the Victorian Christmas; Scottish tartan/bagpipes.
  • Tourism paradox — seeking the authentic destroys it; traveller vs tourist.
  • Post-tourist — knowingly, ironically embraces the fake; post-modernity (Lyotard) / late modernity (Giddens).
  • Age of irony — pastiche/parody/bricolage (Jameson); Eco’s “As Barbara Cartland would put it…”.

Week 2 in one line. The everyday is not the trivial leftover of ‘important’ life but its very medium — repetitive, habitual, familiar, and only felt to be natural. Situated in history, it is the culture of modernity: a dualistic world of rationalization (bureaucracy, feeling rules, the iron cage) and chaos (flux, the blasé crowd, revolt into style), now shading into an ironic, self-conscious late/post-modern age. To study it is to denaturalize it.
