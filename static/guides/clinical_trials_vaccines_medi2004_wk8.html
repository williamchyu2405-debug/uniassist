§0Orientation · the pipeline

Where clinical trials sit in drug development

Human trials are one stage of a long, lossy pipeline — knowing the whole map tells you what each phase is for.

Before we dissect the trial phases, place them on the map. A clinical trial is not a standalone event — it is the human-testing middle of a pipeline that starts with thousands of chemical ideas and ends, years later, with a single approved medicine. This lecture's learning outcomes are to define clinical trials and their objectives and the placebo effect (ULO 3), and to identify the phases of clinical trials (ULO 4). Everything below hangs off those two goals.

Label Key — reading Plate 0.1 left to right
Drug Discovery
Target selection, lead-finding, lead optimisation and pharmacological profiling. Function: generate and refine candidate molecules. Why it matters: the ~100 projects here are where the funnel starts — most die before ever reaching a human. Takes 2–5 years.
Preclinical Development
Pharmacokinetics, short-term toxicology, formulation and synthesis scale-up (~1.5 years). Function: lab/animal safety and manufacturability checks. Why it matters: a compound must survive this to become a "development compound" cleared for first human dosing.
Clinical Development (Phases I–III)
The human-testing block, 5–7 years. Function: Phase I safety in healthy volunteers, Phase II small-scale efficacy/dose in patients, Phase III large-scale controlled trials. Why it matters: this is the entire subject of §3 — the paid-for evidence that the drug works and is safe.
Regulatory Approval
Submission of the full dossier and review by regulatory agencies (1–2 years). Function: independent gatekeeping. Why it matters: the "1.2" at submission becomes "1" approved for marketing — the last cut.
Phase IV
Post-marketing surveillance (yellow band). Function: watch the drug in the real, general population after launch. Why it matters: the only phase that runs once the medicine is on the market — see §3.4.
Attrition row (100 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 2 → 1.2 → 1)
The purple numbers under each stage. Function: shows how many candidates survive each gate. Why it matters: ~100 discovery projects yield a single marketed drug — the economics that make each surviving trial precious.

Trace the bottom row of Plate 0.1: ~100 projects → 20 compounds → 10 → 5 → 2 → 1.2 → 1. That funnel is the single most important idea in the whole map — the vast majority of promising molecules fail, and they fail at every stage. The clinical phases in the middle are simply the human filters that decide which few survive.

Reasoning · why the numbers shrink and the timeline is so long

Each gate answers a harder, more expensive question than the last, so you only pay for the next question on candidates that passed the previous one. You would never run a several-thousand-person Phase III on a molecule whose basic human safety (Phase I) is unknown — it would be unethical and wasteful. The staged design is a risk-and-cost filter: cheap questions first, on few people; expensive questions last, on many. That sequencing is exactly why the whole journey takes 10–15 years.

Worked example · how lossy is the pipeline?

Using Plate 0.1's funnel, quantify the survival rate from discovery project to marketed drug, and from the first clinical candidate.

  1. Read the endpoints: 100 discovery projects at the start, 1 drug approved for marketing at the end.
  2. Overall survival = 1 / 100 = 0.01 = 1%. So ~99% of discovery projects never become a medicine.
  3. Now from the first clinical candidate: 10 enter Phase I, 1 is approved → 1 / 10 = 10% of candidates entering human trials reach the market.
  4. Sanity-check the middle: 10 → 5 → 2 means roughly half are lost across Phase I→II and more than half again across II→III — attrition is heaviest in the clinical block.

Answer: ~1% of discovery projects (and ~10% of first-in-human candidates) reach approval — the trial phases are where most survivors are still culled.

Clinical / real-world

This attrition explains why medicines are expensive and why a failed Phase III is catastrophic for a company: the cost of the 99 that failed is carried by the 1 that succeeds. It also frames the COVID-19 story in §6 — the "why so fast?" answer is partly that huge upfront money let developers run steps in parallel and absorb the risk of a candidate failing late, rather than waiting to de-risk one gate at a time.

§1Definition · objectives

What is a clinical trial?

A controlled, forward-looking experiment in humans — the four words in the definition each do real work.

The lecture opens the topic with a short video and then a WHO-style definition. Strip it to its load-bearing words and the whole rest of the topic follows.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

If you just gave a new drug to 500 patients and 400 improved, why can't you yet claim the drug works?

Because you have no comparator. Many of those 400 might have recovered anyway, or improved through expectation (the placebo effect, §5). Only a controlled design — the drug measured against a placebo or standard care — lets you attribute the improvement to the drug itself. That single missing ingredient is why "controlled" sits at the heart of the definition.

  • A clinical trial is — a controlled, prospective research study comparing the effect, safety and value of a new intervention against a control, in human beings.
  • Controlled — the new intervention is judged against a comparator (placebo or standard care), never against nothing. This is what supplies the counterfactual: what would have happened without the drug.
  • Prospective — participants are enrolled and then followed forward in time, so exposure is recorded before outcomes occur (unlike looking backward through old records).
  • In human beings — this is what separates a trial from the preclinical lab and animal work of §0; the whole point is evidence in people.
  • A powerful experimental technique — the lecture stresses that a properly planned and executed trial is powerful. The design is only as good as its control and its conduct.
Reasoning · why "controlled + prospective" is the whole game

Disease is noisy: symptoms wax and wane, people recover spontaneously, and simply being treated can make someone feel better. A single-arm before-and-after study cannot separate the drug's real effect from that noise. Running a concurrent control arm gives you a parallel group experiencing the same noise without the active drug, so any difference between arms is attributable to the intervention. Following both prospectively removes the recall and selection biases that plague backward-looking studies. Take either property away and the "powerful technique" collapses into an anecdote.

Clinical / real-world

This is why regulators (§2, §6) will not register a drug on uncontrolled case series alone, and why "it worked for my patients" is not evidence. Every phase in §3 is a variation on the same controlled-comparison logic, scaled up as confidence grows.

§2Ethics · regulation · GCP

Ethics, regulation and Good Clinical Practice

Two jobs at once — protect the participant and protect the science; in Australia, an HREC signs off before anyone is dosed.

Because trials expose healthy or sick volunteers to unknowns, they are tightly governed. In Australia the framework is built on the NHMRC's guidance, enforced through a mandatory ethics review, and harmonised internationally through ICH Good Clinical Practice.

  • Ethical guidelines serve two purposes — to protect study participants and to preserve the integrity of the research. Hold both in mind: safety alone isn't enough if the data are junk, and perfect data aren't acceptable if participants are harmed.
  • Safe with favourable risk–benefit — trials must be reasonably safe to participants and carry a favourable risk–benefit ratio.
  • Mandatory HREC approval — every Australian trial must be approved by a Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) before it starts. The HREC checks the research conforms to the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research.

The Australian ethics & TGA standards (the documents that govern a trial)

  • National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research — the core ethics document the HREC checks against.
  • Australian Code for Responsible Conduct of Research — sets researcher conduct standards.
  • International guidelines as adopted by the TGA — e.g. ICH-GCP (below), imported into Australian practice.
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander research ethics — Ethical conduct in research with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and communities, plus Keeping research on track — guidelines ensuring culturally safe, community-accountable research.
  • SPIRIT — Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials — how to write the protocol.
  • CONSORT — Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials — how to report the results transparently.
  • National Operating Procedures for Clinical Trials — the operational rulebook for running trials.
Reasoning · why an independent committee, and why SPIRIT vs CONSORT

Investigators have a stake in their own trial succeeding, which is precisely why the ethics review must be independent — an outside body with no interest in the result guards the participant. The document pairing is deliberate too: SPIRIT disciplines what you promise up front (the protocol), and CONSORT disciplines what you report afterwards. Locking both ends prevents the classic abuse of quietly changing the analysis once the data don't cooperate — a direct defence of "research integrity".

Clinical / real-world

The dedicated Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander guidelines exist because historically research was done on rather than with communities, causing harm and mistrust. Requiring community accountability is an ethics safeguard with real consequences for whether a trial can proceed and whether its findings are trusted and used.

§2.1Good Clinical Practice — the 11 core principles (ICH-GCP E6(R3))

ICH-GCP E6(R3) distils good trial conduct into 11 core principles. Their shared purpose: protect participants, ensure scientific integrity, build quality in from the start, and generate reliable evidence through accountable, risk-proportionate conduct. "Protect the person, protect the proof."

Sort the 11 principles into the two purposes and they stop being a list to memorise. Protect the person: ethical conduct, informed consent, independent ethics review, risk-proportionate approach. Protect the proof: scientific validity, qualified personnel, quality by design, clear protocol, reliable/transparent results, defined roles, GMP product management. Every principle is one or the other.

§3Phases I–IV

The four phases — and why participant numbers grow

Each phase answers one question with the smallest crowd that can answer it; the crowd grows as risk falls and the question widens.

The guiding rule the lecture states up front: use the smallest number of participants needed for the purpose of the trial. Numbers rise across the phases not by accident but because the question changes — from "is it safe at all?" (few people needed) to "does it work across a whole population?" (many needed). Plate 3.1 lays the numbers and timelines side by side; Plate 3.2 is the one-glance summary.

Now the phases one at a time.

Reasoning · why enrol the smallest number, not the most?

More participants means more people exposed to an incompletely-understood drug, more cost, and more time. Ethically and practically you want the fewest people who can still give a statistically meaningful answer to that phase's question. A safety-and-dose question (Phase I) needs only tens; a "beats standard care across a population" question (Phase III) is a smaller effect measured against noise, so it needs thousands to reach statistical power. The number is set by the question, not by ambition.

Worked example · why Phase III needs thousands when Phase I needed dozens

Intuit the scale jump using the numbers in Plates 3.1–3.2.

  1. Phase I asks "is the drug grossly unsafe / what dose?" — a large, obvious signal. A handful of serious events in 20–80 people answers it, so tens suffice.
  2. Phase III asks "is the new drug better than standard care?" — often a modest difference, e.g. improving response from 50% to 60%.
  3. Detecting a small difference against biological noise needs statistical power, and power scales with sample size: small effect ⇒ many participants.
  4. Add the need to catch uncommon adverse effects: a 1-in-1000 harm is essentially invisible in 80 people but shows up in several thousand.

Answer: the sample size is driven by the size of the effect you must detect (and the rarity of harms you must catch) — small effects and rare events force Phase III into the thousands.

§3.1Phase I — first in humans

Phase I is the first time the new drug is given to humans. It typically uses healthy volunteers, 20–80 participants (numbers vary by therapeutic area). The primary concern is safety, side effects, best dose and formulation method for the new drug — not whether it cures anything yet.

  • Primary aim — identify the maximum tolerated dose (MTD), the dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), and the recommended Phase II dose (RP2D).
  • Secondary aims — describe the toxicity profile of the new therapy in the schedule under evaluation; assess pharmacokinetics (PK) — what the body does to the drug; assess pharmacodynamic (PD) effects — what the drug does to the body.
  • Pitfalls — because the sample is small and exposure short, chronic toxicities usually cannot be assessed, cumulative toxicities usually cannot be identified, and uncommon toxicities will be missed.
Reasoning · why healthy volunteers, and why the MTD is the goal

Using healthy volunteers isolates the drug's effects from the confounding effects of illness and other medications — you learn the clean safety and PK/PD picture. And because a Phase I dose-finding study deliberately climbs the dose until toxicity limits it, the outputs are naturally a triple: the highest dose people tolerate (MTD), the toxicities that stop you going higher (DLTs), and the dose you'll carry into Phase II (RP2D). The pitfalls are the flip side of the same coin: a short study in dozens of people simply cannot see harms that take months to build up or that strike 1 person in 1000.

Clinical / real-world

In oncology, Phase I often dispenses with healthy volunteers and enrols patients, because the drugs are too toxic to give to the healthy — a reminder that "healthy volunteers" is the typical case, and numbers/design "vary by therapeutic area". The chronic-toxicity blind spot is also why real safety knowledge keeps accruing right through Phase IV.

§3.2Phase II — initial assessment of efficacy

At the recommended Phase II dose, Phase II tests efficacy for the first time and refines the toxicity profile of the agent. It typically enrols 40–100 patients in a multi-stage design.

  • Screen out ineffective drugs — the first goal: kill candidates that don't actually work before they cost a fortune in Phase III.
  • Send promising agents to Phase III — the second goal: pass forward only the drugs worth the pivotal test.
Reasoning · why a "multi-stage design"?

A multi-stage design builds in interim looks: if the drug shows no sign of efficacy after the first stage, the trial stops early. This is both ethical (stop exposing patients to a useless drug) and efficient (don't spend the rest of the budget). It's the statistical embodiment of Phase II's job as a filter — fail fast, fail cheap.

Clinical / real-world

Phase II is where most drugs die — it is the gatekeeper between "safe in a few people" and the multi-thousand-person commitment of Phase III. A drug that looks promising in Phase II but stumbles in Phase III is the single most expensive failure in the pipeline (recall the 5 → 2 cut in Plate 0.1).

§3.3Phase III — efficacy in large groups

Phase III studies efficacy in large groups of participants. It is randomised and controlled, preferably double-blind, and relatively large — usually several hundred or thousands of people.

  • Definitive answers — whether the new treatment is better than the control group or non-interventional standard care, or is similarly effective but has other advantages (e.g. fewer side effects, easier dosing).
  • Monitor adverse effects and collect information that will allow the intervention to be used safely in practice.
Reasoning · why "randomised, controlled, double-blind" — the three shields

Each word blocks a specific bias. Randomised: assigning participants to arms by chance balances known and unknown confounders across groups, so the arms are comparable. Controlled: the comparator supplies the counterfactual (§1). Double-blind: neither participant nor assessor knows who got the drug, so the placebo effect (§5) and observer bias hit both arms equally and cancel out. Together they make the between-arm difference attributable to the drug alone — which is exactly why regulators treat Phase III as the pivotal evidence.

Memory hook · what "definitive" allows

"Better — or just as good but nicer."

A Phase III win is not only "beats the control". A drug that is similarly effective but has other advantages also passes. Don't equate success solely with superiority.

Clinical / real-world

Double-blinding is why placebo tablets are made to look, taste and be dosed identically to the active drug. Where blinding is impossible (e.g. surgery vs no surgery), trials lean harder on objective endpoints and blinded outcome assessors to protect against the same biases.

§3.4Phase IV — post-marketing surveillance

Phase IV runs after registration and marketing — the only phase that happens once the drug is on the market. It is designed to monitor the effectiveness of the approved intervention in the general population and to collect information about adverse effects associated with widespread use over longer periods. Like Phase III it is relatively large (usually several hundred or thousand people).

  • Continue monitoring efficacy and safety once the new treatment has been adopted into routine practice (this ongoing safety work is pharmacovigilance, §4).
  • Identify uncommon adverse effects not seen in the preceding Phase III trials — exactly the harms Phase I–III were too small or too short to catch.
  • Investigate new uses — the potential of the intervention in a different condition, or in combination with other therapies.
Reasoning · why we still study a drug after approval

Phase III, for all its size, still enrols a selected, carefully-monitored population for a limited time. The real world is messier and vastly larger: millions of diverse patients, comorbidities, drug interactions and years of exposure. That scale is the only thing that can surface a 1-in-10,000 adverse effect or a harm that emerges after two years — neither of which a few-thousand-person, months-to-years trial could ever detect. Phase IV is where the population itself becomes the study.

Clinical / real-world

Drugs have been withdrawn years after approval when Phase IV surveillance revealed rare cardiovascular or liver harms invisible in pre-approval trials. This is the structural reason "approved" never means "proven completely safe" — safety knowledge is provisional and keeps updating.

§4Boundaries · other designs

Phase IV vs pharmacovigilance, and other trial types

A Phase IV trial is a tool; pharmacovigilance is the whole toolbox — and some trials (Phase 0) come before Phase I.

Two distinctions the lecture is careful to draw. First, don't confuse a single study type with the entire safety system it lives inside. Second, the four numbered phases aren't the whole story — an exploratory Phase 0 can precede them.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

Are "Phase IV trial" and "pharmacovigilance" two names for the same thing?

No. Pharmacovigilance is the entire post-approval safety-monitoring system; a Phase IV trial is just one method within it. Treating them as synonyms is a classic exam trap.

Reasoning · why you need more than Phase IV trials to keep a drug safe

A designed Phase IV study still watches a defined cohort under a protocol. But the rarest harms surface unpredictably, from clinicians and patients who were never enrolled in any study. Spontaneous reporting catches those signals from the whole treated population; risk management plans act on them. A single trial can't do that job — which is why pharmacovigilance is a system of methods, not one study.

Other clinical trials — exploratory / Phase 0 studies

  • Exploratory / Phase 0 (pilot) studies — these come before Phase I trials and test how the body responds to an experimental drug.
  • Very small doses — sub-therapeutic amounts, given once or for a short time, to a very limited number of people (Plate 3.1: 15 or fewer).
Reasoning · what a Phase 0 buys you

By giving a tiny, sub-therapeutic dose you get an early human read on pharmacokinetics and target engagement without meaningful risk of toxicity. If the drug doesn't even behave as hoped in the body at micro-doses, you can drop it before committing to a full Phase I — pushing the "fail fast, fail cheap" logic even earlier in the pipeline.

Clinical / real-world

Phase 0 microdosing lets developers compare several candidate molecules head-to-head in humans very early and carry only the best-behaving one forward — saving the enormous cost of running full Phase I programs on molecules that were never going to work.

§5The control's secret weapon

The placebo effect

A real, common improvement from an inert treatment — the very reason a control group exists.

A placebo is a substance, pill or other treatment that appears to be a medical intervention but isn't one — a sugar pill or a saline infusion. In a trial the placebo group is the control group. The catch that makes trials hard: the placebo often works.

  • Placebo effect — any improvement in a symptom or physiological condition, or reported side effects, in a participant receiving a placebo treatment.
  • How common — it's estimated that about 1 in 3 people experience the placebo effect.
  • Extent (where it shows up) — measurable in outcomes like blood pressure, chronic pain, depression and fatigue, but not in objective biomarkers such as tumour size.

Why does a sugar pill help? Two mechanisms

  • Classical conditioning — you associate a thing with a specific response. Example: you associate the doctor's office with receiving treatment or with feeling better, so the setting alone triggers improvement.
  • Expectations — set by verbal information, actions, social context and labelling. Being told (or shown) that a treatment is powerful shapes how you actually feel.
Reasoning · why the placebo effect spares tumours but moves pain

The distinction the lecture draws — real on blood pressure, pain, depression, fatigue; absent on tumour size — is not arbitrary. Expectation and conditioning act through the brain: attention, autonomic tone and endogenous pain/mood pathways, which genuinely modulate subjective and physiologically-labile outcomes. They cannot, however, shrink a tumour or otherwise rewrite hard biological structure. So the placebo effect is strongest exactly where the outcome is self-reported or neurally modifiable, and negligible where it is a fixed, objective measurement.

Clinical / real-world

Because roughly 1 in 3 people improve on placebo, an uncontrolled study will always look like the drug works. This is the whole justification for the control group and for double-blinding (§3.3): only by giving the placebo arm the same expectations and conditioning can you subtract the placebo effect and reveal the drug's true added benefit. The placebo isn't a nuisance — it's the measuring stick.

Memory hook · one in three, in the mind

"A third feel it — but only where the mind can reach."

~1 in 3 respond; the effect touches mind-mediated outcomes (pain, mood, fatigue, blood pressure) and stops at hard biology (tumour size). Two drivers: Conditioning and Expectation.

§6Approval · COVID-19

Vaccines: approval standards and the COVID-19 example

Same high bar as any medicine, held even higher — and a case study in going fast without skipping a step.

Vaccines run the same trial pipeline as any drug, but the approval bar sits especially high because they are given to healthy people. In Australia the gatekeeper is the TGA (Therapeutic Goods Administration).

TGA approval standards

  • Rigorous assessment — the TGA assesses vaccines for safety, quality and efficacy before use in Australia, applying the same high scrutiny as other prescription medicines.
  • Best available evidence — the TGA uses the best available scientific evidence to weigh each vaccine's risks and benefits.
  • Evidence requirements based on EMA guidelines — international guidelines developed by the European Medicines Agency (EMA).
  • Assesses the trials and how they were run — both the results and the way the trials were conducted.
  • Trial requirements — well-designed trials of sufficient length, with a sufficient number of people who represent the intended population; the results must show benefits greatly outweigh the risks.
  • ACV advice — registration decisions are informed by the Advisory Committee on Vaccines (ACV).
  • Post-supply monitoring — the TGA keeps monitoring safety after supply; adverse events should be reported (seek help from a health professional and report to the TGA).
Reasoning · why the vaccine bar is set higher than for most drugs

A treatment drug is given to someone already ill, who stands to gain a lot, so a degree of risk is acceptable. A vaccine is given to people who are otherwise healthy and specifically free from the illness — they have no current disease to offset any harm. With little to gain in the moment and given to millions, the tolerable risk is tiny, so the benefit must greatly outweigh the risk. Same pipeline, stricter threshold.

Clinical / real-world

Requiring trial populations to represent the intended recipients is why vaccine trials deliberately enrol across ages, ethnicities and comorbidities — a vaccine proven only in healthy young adults can't be assumed safe or effective in the elderly, who often need it most. Post-supply TGA monitoring is the vaccine equivalent of Phase IV/pharmacovigilance (§3.4, §4).

COVID-19: emergency use and why development was so fast

  • Emergency Use Listing (EUL) — during global health emergencies, the WHO EUL procedure may allow emergency use of a vaccine.
  • mRNA built on decades of research — mRNA vaccines are based on decades of prior research; scientists had been studying them long before COVID-19.
  • Rapid spike-protein encoding — as soon as the virus was sequenced, researchers built the unique spike-protein code into an mRNA vaccine.
  • Beyond vaccines — cancer research has used mRNA to trigger the immune system to target specific cancer cells.

The headline: clinical trial phases overlapped, but no phase was omitted. The speed came from running work in parallel and from money and collaboration — not from cutting scientific corners.

  • Phases in parallel — several phases were worked on at the same time to speed up results, rather than strictly one-after-another.
  • Scale of commitment — the financial and political commitment to vaccine development is what allowed this accelerated development.
  • International collaboration — nations and international health organisations worked together through COVAX to invest in development capacity upfront (streamlining the process) and to ensure equitable distribution.
Reasoning · how you compress 10 years without skipping a phase

Normally each phase waits for the previous one to finish and for funding to be secured before the next starts — see the sequential funnel in Plate 0.1. Two things collapsed that timeline for COVID-19. First, overlapping phases: manufacturing scale-up and the next trial phase were begun before the prior phase fully reported, so the calendar time shrank even though every phase still ran in full. Second, upfront money removed the waiting: massive financial and political commitment (and COVAX pooling) paid for capacity in advance, absorbing the risk that a candidate might fail late. Add decades of pre-existing mRNA science and a genome that could be turned into a spike-protein sequence in days, and the acceleration comes from parallelism and pre-investment — the safety and efficacy questions were still all answered.

Memory hook · fast, not skipped

"Overlapped, not omitted."

The one line to carry out of §6: COVID-19 vaccine phases overlapped (run in parallel, funded upfront) — none was omitted. Speed came from money, collaboration and prior mRNA research, not from dropping steps.

Clinical / real-world

The EUL is a controlled fast-track, not a bypass of evidence — it lets a vaccine be used in an emergency once enough safety/efficacy data exist, while full review continues. This is the practical bridge between "the bar is extremely high" and "people are dying now", and it sits alongside continued TGA post-supply monitoring.

R Recall checklist — clinical trials & vaccines
  • Pipeline attrition — ~100 discovery projects yield 1 approved drug, over roughly 10–15 years.
  • Clinical trial definition — a controlled, prospective study comparing a new intervention against a control in humans.
  • Why "controlled" — the comparator supplies the counterfactual, separating the drug's effect from natural recovery and placebo.
  • HREC — the ethics committee that must approve an Australian trial, checking the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research.
  • Two purposes of ethics guidelines — protect participants and preserve research integrity.
  • SPIRIT vs CONSORT — SPIRIT governs the protocol up front; CONSORT governs transparent reporting afterwards.
  • ICH-GCP E6(R3) — 11 core principles: protect the person and protect the proof, with GMP-standard investigational product.
  • Phase I — first-in-humans, 20–80 healthy volunteers; find MTD, DLTs and the recommended Phase II dose.
  • Phase I blind spot — chronic, cumulative and uncommon toxicities are usually missed.
  • Phase II — 40–100 patients, multi-stage; screen out ineffective drugs, send promising ones to Phase III.
  • Phase III — randomised, controlled, preferably double-blind, hundreds–thousands; definitive better-than-or-as-good-as answer.
  • Why randomise, control, blind — balance confounders, supply a counterfactual, and cancel placebo/observer bias.
  • Phase IV — post-marketing surveillance in the general population; catches uncommon and long-term adverse effects.
  • Phase IV vs pharmacovigilance — a Phase IV trial is one tool within pharmacovigilance, the whole safety-monitoring system.
  • Phase 0 — exploratory pre-Phase-I microdosing: tiny doses, once/short, very few people, to see how the body responds.
  • Placebo effect — ~1 in 3 improve on an inert treatment, via classical conditioning and expectation.
  • Placebo extent — real on pain, mood, fatigue and blood pressure; absent on objective measures like tumour size.
  • TGA vaccine bar — safety, quality and efficacy assessed on EMA-based guidelines; benefits must greatly outweigh risks.
  • Why the vaccine bar is higher — vaccines are given to healthy people free of the illness, so little upfront benefit offsets any risk.
  • COVID-19 speed — phases overlapped but none were omitted; driven by funding, COVAX collaboration and decades of prior mRNA research.
  • WHO EUL — the Emergency Use Listing that permits emergency vaccine use during a global health emergency.
