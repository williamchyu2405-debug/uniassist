SOCI1010 · Week 1 · Introduction to Sociology: Understanding Everyday Life — A/Prof Selvaraj Velayutham (Raj)

What is Everyday Life? — making something out of nothing

Sociology is the study of society and how it shapes our lives. This unit teaches it through the things we least notice — a plastic chair, a toothbrush, breakfast, a sitcom. The wager of the course is that a critical look at the ordinary reveals a surprisingly complex social world underneath. These are the full Week-1 notes: the sociological imagination, the everyday as an object of study, structure and agency, and the two Week-1 readings (everyday life & comedy).

§0Orientation

What this unit is

Learning to think sociologically — through the everyday.

SOCI1010 is one of two introductory sociology units. Its single goal, by Week 13, is that you think sociologically — that you can see the relationship between an individual life and the wider society around it. Where the companion unit SOCI1000 introduces the key scholars, traditions and approaches head-on, SOCI1010 teaches the same theories and ideas through a particular lens: the everyday. Ordinary objects, activities, rituals and encounters become the object of analysis, and through them the unit reaches the big questions — gender, race and ethnicity, capitalism, class.

Sociology is a couple of centuries old. It produces knowledge about the social world we live in, and it is a genuinely useful tool — in or out of academia — for critically understanding how society works and how it shapes individuals like us. That relationship between the individual and the social is the main lens through which sociology explores the world, and it is the thread running through every topic in this unit.

Reasoning · why teach through the trivial?

Starting with wars, elections and inequality would seem the “serious” choice. But those forces are abstract and easy to keep at arm’s length. The everyday is where they actually land — the rising grocery bill, the fuel price, the anxious choice of degree in an age of AI. Teaching through the everyday isn’t teaching the trivial instead of the important; it is showing that the important is already inside the ordinary, filtering through everything we do.

R Recall checklist — orientation
  • Goal — by Week 13, to think sociologically: to see the link between individual and society.
  • The lens — the everyday; ordinary things become the object of analysis for bigger questions.
  • SOCI1000 vs 1010 — same theories; 1000 head-on, 1010 through the everyday.
§1Core concept

Sociology & the sociological imagination

Seeing the social structure behind a personal life.

Sociology = the study of society and how society shapes our lives; and equally, a tool or approach for understanding the world. As a sociologist you are equipped with concepts to apply to the lived world, giving you a more critical insight into why social relations are the way they are and why people act the way they do.

The American sociologist C. Wright Mills named the quality of mind this requires the sociological imagination: cultivating a vivid awareness of the relationship between personal experience and the wider society. It means thinking away from the purely personal, to see how the individual and the large social forces acting on them are connected. In Mills’ formula: neither the life of an individual nor the history of a society can be understood without understanding both.

Bigger picture · two worked examples from the lecture

“I chose to come to uni.” Did you? It may have been your third choice; who gets the opportunity, and how it is distributed, is structured by society, not simply willed by the individual.
Unemployment. We point the finger at the unemployed person — lazy, not trying. That loses sight of the larger forces: the job market, governments not creating jobs, work relocating offshore, and now AI reshaping the nature of work. Is a jobless life the individual’s fault, or are social forces shaping their life course?

Reasoning · why “private troubles” are “public issues”

The imagination works by relocating a problem. A private trouble (my unemployment, my toothache, my breakup) feels like a personal, individual matter. The sociological move is to ask what social pattern it reflects — to see that what happens to us immediately is a minute point at which our personal life and society intersect. Underneath the messy reality of everyday life sits a social structure we are usually unaware of; the imagination is the tool that makes it visible.

Memory hook · Mills in five words

“Personal experience ↔ wider society.” If you can only hold one line for the quiz, hold Mills’: understand both, or you understand neither.

R Recall checklist — the sociological imagination
  • Sociology — study of society + how it shapes us; a critical tool for understanding the world.
  • Sociological imagination (C. Wright Mills) — awareness of the link between personal experience and wider society.
  • Key line — neither an individual’s life nor a society’s history is understood without understanding both.
  • Examples — the “choice” of university; unemployment blamed on the person vs the job market/AI.
§2The method, illustrated

Making something out of nothing: the monobloc chair

A cheap plastic chair as a foil for the whole unit.

The lecture’s motif is the ubiquitous white/coloured plastic chair — the monobloc, probably the best-selling chair in history, and widely despised. It is so hated that in Basel, Switzerland it was for years forbidden by law in the outdoor areas of restaurants, because people complained it destroyed the cityscape. Yet you find it everywhere: outside cafés in Denmark, in an office in Tanzania, at the Western Wall in Jerusalem. Plate 2.1 shows it doing what it does best — being unremarkable.

Label Key — the sociological questions to ask of one object
Origins
Where and when did it come from? First monobloc arguably by Canadian designer D.C. Simpson (1946); the technical predecessor was Verner Panton’s single-piece, stackable plastic chair; mass production took off with the 1983 Grosfillex resin garden chair.
Production
Injection moulding: polypropylene granulate heated to ~320 °C and injected into a single mould (hence “mono-bloc” = one unbroken piece). Moulds cost hundreds of thousands to millions, but each chair costs ~US$3 to make and sells for ~US$10.
Function & aesthetics
Weatherproof, stackable, light, durable — arguably a “perfect” design; yet despised by designers, set against exquisite modernist chairs.
Global capitalism
A prime symbol of globalisation and cheap mass production — and a reminder of how locally distinctive other objects still are.
Bigger picture · the “context-free object”

Media scholar Ethan Zuckerman calls the monobloc a context-free object. Most objects in a photo hint at a time and place; the monobloc gives nothing away — a chair on a beach could have been carried there or washed up from the other side of the planet. That very placelessness is the sociological hook: it makes the chair a foil for the everyday — the taken-for-granted background we never notice.

Reasoning · why a chair is worth a lecture

“We’re making a lot of fuss about a chair — but that’s the point.” The chair carries a genuine debate: critics read it as unsustainable, uniform mass-consumption and a decline of local culture; defenders note its success flows from affordability, which enables social equality and economic advancement — making it, in one view, deeply democratic. Neither reading is available until you stop and interrogate an object you were trained to ignore. That move — asking where, why, how, and at whose profit — is the method the whole unit will apply.

R Recall checklist — the monobloc
  • The move — “making something out of nothing”: interrogate a mundane object to reach big questions.
  • Context-free object (Ethan Zuckerman) — reveals nothing of time/place; a symbol of globalisation.
  • Debate — unsustainable uniformity vs affordable and “deeply democratic”.
  • Facts — polypropylene, ~320 °C injection moulding; ~US$3 to make, ~US$10 to buy.
§3Defining the object

What is ‘the everyday’?

Everywhere and nowhere — mundane, habitual, strangely elusive.

At first glance the everyday seems to be everywhere — but also nowhere, because it has no clear boundaries and is hard to define. It is associated with the habitual and the mundane, yet is strangely elusive. (A fuller definition is Week 2’s job.) The sociology of everyday life explains what is going on in mundane contexts — the street, the home, public space — by treating the mundane as significant and full of meaning, and the unusual as having its own logic. It presupposes that everything is of interest to the inquiring mind: nothing is left untouched.

Why does this work? Because the social world is nothing other than an interpersonal field of encounters, interactions and relationships — spanning intimacy to interest, consensus to conflict. Those embeddings of humans in relationships with other humans occur nowhere else but in the everyday. Everyday life is therefore the observable manifestation of our social existence — which is exactly what sociology wants to study.

Reasoning · worked example — the sociology of breakfast

Take an act you’d never think twice about and run it through different social lenses:

1 · Ritual / tradition. Having breakfast at a set time, or with family, makes it a ritual — and builds a larger narrative of family life and socialising.
2 · Norms. Pairing it with an “acceptable” beverage (tea, coffee, juice) shows we follow norms and avoid socially questionable choices — a beer at breakfast breaks the rule (though it may be fine in Germany).
3 · Health & identity. What we choose to eat can signal dedication to good health.
4 · Social bonding. Breakfast with friends or coworkers — the “business breakfast” — is an expression of bonding.

One trivial ritual turns out to encode a whole set of unwritten rules. That is the everyday made strange.

Memory hook · the everyday’s paradox

The everyday is “everywhere but nowhere” — so obvious it becomes invisible. Sociology’s job is to make the invisible obvious again.

R Recall checklist — the everyday
  • Character — everywhere yet nowhere; habitual, mundane, elusive; no clear boundaries.
  • The stance — treat the mundane as significant and full of meaning; everything is of interest.
  • Why — social existence is observable only in the everyday field of encounters and relationships.
  • Worked example — breakfast as ritual, norm, health signal and bonding.
§4The core conundrum

Structure & agency

Do we follow the rules, or can we remake them?

Two concepts sit at the core of sociology: structure and agency. Most of social life is conforming to structure — doing what parents, teachers and the law tell us to do. Those who don’t conform are often seen as deviant. Agency is the counter-force: the capacity of an individual to express their individuality, think differently and challenge restrictive norms — and, as long as you are not breaking the law, there is a possibility to change things.

The lecture’s illustration is as close to hand as the room: almost everyone is dressed similarly, quietly conforming; a few — the student with bright red hair — stand out. So the conundrum is: when, and how often, is agency actually possible? Is presenting yourself differently a real opportunity to “be you”, or are you, in the end, just like the rest of us?

Reasoning · why the pairing matters

Structure and agency are the poles of sociology’s central question. Lean all the way to structure and people become puppets of society; lean all the way to agency and society dissolves into free individual choices. The interesting work is in between — showing how much of our behaviour is reproduced by social rules, and precisely where and when individuals can push back. A sociological view of any everyday activity therefore also raises the possibility of disrupting it: challenging what counts as a “nutritious breakfast”, or cracking open a beer at the breakfast table in front of your parents — a small, deliberate breach of a norm.

Memory hook · structure vs agency

Structure = the rules that make you conform. Agency = your capacity to break or remake them. Deviance is what structure calls agency it doesn’t like.

R Recall checklist — structure & agency
  • Structure — the social rules (parents, teachers, law) we mostly conform to; non-conformity reads as deviance.
  • Agency — the capacity to express individuality and challenge norms (within the law).
  • The conundrum — how much room for agency is there, really? (the dress-code example).
  • Sociology’s edge — analysing an everyday act also opens the option of disrupting it.
§5Extended worked example

Unpacking a ritual: brushing your teeth

From a bodily habit to class, public health and global capitalism.

Nothing is more mundane than brushing your teeth — and that is exactly why it repays the sociological lens. Follow one habit outward and it connects to socialisation, class, public health, medicine and global capitalism.

Embodied practice & socialisation

Think about how you were taught. In kindergarten and school, Department of Health nurses handed out free toothpaste and brushes; the cartoon “The Toothbrush Family” (US, also run in Australia) taught children dental hygiene. Whether at school or home, learning how and how often to brush and floss turns it into an embodied practice — you no longer think twice; it is the first ritual of the day, first nature. Sociologists call this socialisation: a ritual becomes familiar and internalised, carried in the body. Plate 5.1 is the “correct” technique that socialisation installs.

Label Key — one habit, five social threads
Socialisation
Taught at kindergarten/school/home until internalised; “The Toothbrush Family” as a socialising text.
Class & health
Dental care is expensive in Australia and the US, so many neglect it; bad teeth and gum disease correlate with income — tied to homelessness, addiction, poverty, and to age, class and gender. ~60–90% of school children have dental cavities.
Idealised body
The “perfect smile” matters for interviews and certain jobs, spawning an industry — whitening, straightening, braces — and medical tourism (a crown ~A$6,000–8,000 here vs ~A$1,000 in Malaysia/Thailand).
Medicalisation
“The unseen dentist”: medicine’s monopoly of knowledge over the body; where care is private, corporations can “run the show” (US vs Australia/UK safety nets; strained public hospitals).
Global capitalism
5–6 multinationals own the familiar brands (and cosmetics and luxury goods); the toothpaste aisle’s “choice” is largely an illusion — see Plate 5.2.

The illusion of choice

Why does one simple function — cleaning teeth — need eight varieties of toothpaste (whitening, mint, spearmint…)? Because the choice of brush or paste is wired into a much larger profit-making enterprise: global / consumer capitalism, “most brilliantly displayed” in the supermarket aisle. Just 5–6 multinational corporations own nearly all the brands you recognise — and much of the cosmetics and luxury market besides. Plate 5.2 maps that concentration.

Reasoning · how a habit becomes a structure

The chain is the argument. A private habit (brushing) is installed by socialisation; access to maintaining it is shaped by class (dental costs); its ideal form is set by an industry (the perfect smile); the knowledge governing it is held by a profession (medicalisation); and the products come from a concentrated market (global capitalism). At every step the “personal” act turns out to be patterned by a social force — which is why a simple ritual can end up being a national and global health issue tied to class.

Bigger picture · “unless you make your own”

The only way outside the corporate menu is to opt out entirely — make your own Gopal tooth powder from natural herbs. The joke lands the point: within the supermarket, your freedom is a choice among what a few corporations already decided to offer. Abundance of options is not the same as real choice.

R Recall checklist — the toothbrush
  • Embodied practice / socialisation — brushing taught until internalised (“The Toothbrush Family”).
  • Class & public health — costly dental care; bad teeth correlate with income, homelessness, addiction; ~60–90% of children have cavities.
  • Perfect smile — an industry (whitening/braces) + medical tourism.
  • Medicalisation — the “unseen dentist”; medicine’s monopoly; public vs private systems.
  • Global capitalism — 5–6 firms own the brands; the “illusion of choice”.
§6Reading 2

Sociology & comedy: Seinfeld

The significance of the insignificant.

Week 1 has two readings: a general overview of the sociology of everyday life, and a short chapter on comedy from a book on the philosophy of Seinfeld (Jorge Gracia). The chapter’s purpose is to explain why comedy makes us sit up and think — and it does so by contrast with tragedy. Plate 6.1 is the reading’s source.

Reasoning · comedy, tragedy and the sociologist

Greek tragedy is powerful and sad — killing, rivalry, bloodletting — but it is significant about significant things. Comedy does the opposite: the stand-up reflects on simple things and shows how idiosyncratic and absurd they are. Comedy looks at the insignificant — and the everyday is insignificant — in order to show that there is something significant about that insignificance (the line to find in the reading). That is precisely the parallel with the discipline: cultivate the sociological imagination and you, like the comedian, learn to see the significance of the insignificant.

The parallels run deep. Comedy questions power structures and puts authority in the spotlight — so do sociologists. Comedy puts our everyday manners, customs and idiosyncrasies under the microscope and implicitly asks do these arrangements make sense? — so do sociologists. But there is a hard difference: the comedian works from anecdote; the sociologist studies the mundane rigorously and systematically, with an emphasis on methods (§7) and a toolkit of specialised concepts.

Bigger picture · reflexivity in three episodes

“The show about nothing.” In the pitch, George and Jerry sell NBC a series with no story: “we sit, we talk, that’s the show” (the salsa/seltzer riff). Seinfeld is built on questioning the everyday rules of society.
The “I love you” return. George risks saying “I love you” — a “big matzo ball” — and gets back “I’m hungry, let’s get something to eat.” The unwritten rule of reciprocation is exposed by its breach.
The gift. What is an appropriate present for a girlfriend — a bench? a music box? a waffle maker? cash? — and what meaning will it be read to send? The show forces us to reflect on the rules of etiquette and gift-giving: are they written or unwritten, why do they exist, and what are the consequences of breaking them? That constant monitoring of the rules is what sociologists call reflexivity.

R Recall checklist — sociology & comedy
  • Tragedy — significant about significant things (death, rivalry).
  • Comedy — finds the significance of the insignificant everyday.
  • Like the comedian — sociologists question power and put everyday manners under the microscope.
  • Unlike the comedian — sociology is rigorous and systematic, with methods and concepts.
  • Seinfeld — “show about nothing”, the “I love you” return, the gift → reflexivity about unwritten rules.
§7Doing sociology

How sociologists study the everyday

Rigorous methods, and concepts as tools.

What separates the sociologist from the comedian is method. As a social science, sociology studies the mundane in a rigorous, systematic way — so there is a strong emphasis on how we come to know the social world. Broadly, the methods split in two.

Qualitative vs quantitative methods
QualitativeQuantitative
AimQuality — depth of a participant’s experience of the social worldThe bigger picture — trends and snapshots across a large sample
MethodsParticipant observation / ethnography, interviewing, fieldwork, focus groupsSurvey questionnaires
OutputRich accounts and meaningsStatistics, charts and graphs
ExampleInterviewing people about their routinesThe Australian census — households fill out set questions
Reasoning · why concepts aren’t just jargon

You wouldn’t expect a comedian to throw around capitalism, commodification, consumerism, bureaucracy, class. Sociologists do, and the words can seem abstract or esoteric — but they are enabling: they let us unpack how social life is organised and tell us what to look for. Take class. It is not just wealth. Someone poor may feel good about themselves; a well-paid tradie may not care for the opera, while the self-styled sophisticate reads widely, goes to the symphony and drinks fine wine. Because the same income can carry very different tastes, class needs more than a dollar figure — it needs the idea of taste as a marker of status (cultural capital). The concept is what makes the difference visible.

Memory hook · qual vs quant

QUALitative = quality (depth, meaning, few people). QUANTitative = quantity (numbers, trends, many people — the census).

R Recall checklist — methods & concepts
  • Qualitative — participant observation/ethnography, interviews, fieldwork, focus groups; depth of experience.
  • Quantitative — surveys/questionnaires; big-picture trends; the Australian census.
  • Concepts — capitalism, commodification, class, etc.: abstract but enabling tools.
  • Class — not just wealth; also taste/status (cultural capital).
§8Logistics

Unit map & assessment

Where the course goes, and how you’re marked.

The first four weeks are a broad theoretical overview; from Week 5 the unit applies the lens to specific topics.

Semester map
WeeksFocus
1–4Overview — intro (1); defining the everyday (2); key sociologists, “not so mundane” (3); researching the everyday (4)
5+Topics — gender, class & bodies; religion; consumption; everyday diversity/multiculturalism; sport; home & objects; food & eating

Bigger picture · the three assessments

1 · Reflection essay (due Week 5). A photo of an everyday activity (past prompts: the self-checkout machine; a McDonald’s drive-through), using Week 1–4 concepts to explain its deeper meaning.
2 · Take-home exam (Week 9, open book). 6 questions, answer 4, ~200 words (¼ page) each — applying sociological concepts to everyday things.
3 · Multiple-choice quiz. 60 questions across topics, open book, checking you attended lectures and did the readings (e.g. “who coined the term cultural capital?”).

Reasoning · why the assessment looks like this

The tasks are the unit’s thesis in practice. Asking you to read a photographed everyday activity forces you to apply concepts to the mundane — the exact move the course teaches. Keeping the exam and quiz open book shifts the test away from memorising and toward whether you can actually think sociologically with the material in front of you — attention in lectures and doing the readings, not recall under pressure.

R Recall checklist — unit & assessment
  • Weeks 1–4 — theory/overview; 5+ — applied topics; 9 — public holiday + take-home window.
  • Reflection essay (Wk 5) — read an everyday photo with course concepts.
  • Take-home exam (Wk 9) — 4 of 6 questions, ~200 words each, open book.
  • Quiz — 60 MCQs, open book, on lectures + readings.

Week 1 in one line. Sociology is the study of society and how it shapes us; the sociological imagination lets us see the social structure behind a personal life; and by studying the everyday — a chair, a toothbrush, a sitcom — we make something out of nothing, finding the significance of the insignificant.
