Your recurring error patterns are rescheduled with spaced repetition.

Task A gives you a set of quotes on a shared theme and asks for an argumentative, analytical response. The markers reward one thing above all: a piece of writing that thinks — a clear contention, developed with depth, in controlled prose. Here is how to get there in 35 minutes.

1Unpack the quotes before you write anything

Spend your five reading minutes actively. For each quote, jot a two-or-three-word paraphrase of its claim — what it asserts about the theme, not just what it mentions. Then ask: do the quotes agree? Usually at least one pushes against the others, and that friction is your way in.

  • Don't treat the quotes as decoration — they define the intellectual territory you must engage with.
  • You are not required to reference every quote; engaging deeply with one or two beats name-checking all five.
  • Circle the quote you disagree with most — arguing against something often produces your sharpest thinking.
2Find the theme — and the tension inside it

The quotes always share one underlying theme (power, conformity, progress, freedom…), but a good essay is never "about power" — it is about a tension within the theme: security vs liberty, individual conscience vs collective duty, ambition as fuel vs ambition as corrosion.

Name the tension in one sentence before you start. If you can phrase your essay's territory as "X, but at the cost of Y" or "X only when Y", you have something arguable rather than a topic summary.

3Build a thesis, not a topic

Your contention should be a position someone could disagree with, stated in your own voice. Test it: if the opposite of your thesis is obviously absurd ("censorship is sometimes bad"), it isn't a thesis yet — sharpen it until a reasonable person could argue the other side.

Weak Technology has both good and bad effects on society.
Strong Technology does not erode human connection — it exposes how shallow many of our connections already were, and forces us to choose which ones to rebuild deliberately.
4Structure: intro → developed bodies → counter → conclusion
  • Introduction (3–4 sentences): open with the tension, not a dictionary definition; state your contention plainly by the last sentence. You may springboard off one quote.
  • Two developed body paragraphs beat four thin ones. Each: a claim that advances the thesis → reasoning → a concrete example → a link back. Depth over coverage.
  • Counter-argument or nuance paragraph: concede the strongest opposing point honestly, then show why your position still holds — or refine it. This is where Band 5–6 essays separate from Band 4.
  • Conclusion (2–3 sentences): don't summarise — extend. End on the implication of your argument: what follows if you're right?
5Integrate ideas with examples that do work

An example earns its place only if you analyse it — one well-worked example (historical, literary, scientific, or contemporary) explored for three sentences outweighs three examples mentioned in one. Draw from what you genuinely know: a physiology concept, a historical turning point, a novel you actually read.

  • Avoid the over-used set (Hitler, Einstein, "studies show…"). Fresher material signals genuine thought.
  • Personal observation is legitimate in Task A if it is analysed, not merely recounted.
  • After every example, write the "so what" sentence: what does this show about your contention?
6Common pitfalls
✗Quote-summarising. Walking through each quote in turn ("The first quote says… the second quote says…") reads as comprehension, not argument.
✗The balanced fence-sit. "There are two sides" is an observation, not a contention. Take a side; handle the other side in your counter paragraph.
✗Pre-fabricated essays. Markers spot a memorised essay bent onto the theme. Prepare ideas and examples, not paragraphs.
✗Overreaching vocabulary. A plain word used precisely outranks an impressive word used almost-correctly. Error density caps your language band.
✗No time for the counter. Budget: ~5 min planning, ~25 writing, ~5 re-reading. Cut a third example before you cut the counter-argument.
7A short worked example

Suppose the quotes circle censorship and free expression, and one reads: "The peace of a society is worth more than the noise of its loudest voices."

Tension Social harmony vs the discomfort that genuine free expression requires.
Contention A society that silences its loudest voices to keep the peace does not become peaceful — it becomes quiet, and mistakes the quiet for consent.
Skeleton Body 1: suppression hides problems rather than resolving them (e.g. early warnings silenced in public-health crises). Body 2: friction is how ideas improve — the offensive idea of one generation is often the orthodoxy of the next. Counter: expression that incites harm is a real limit — so the line belongs at incitement, not at offence. Conclusion: the cost of noise is real, but it is the price of a society that can still correct itself.

Notice what makes this work: the contention reframes the quote's own language ("peace" becomes "quiet"), each body advances one step of the argument, and the counter concedes something real without surrendering the thesis.

8Sentence moves, annotated — technique, not templates

Each line below is a single move, on a deliberately different theme, to show how a sentence earns its place — not a paragraph to reuse. Copying phrasing is easy for a marker to spot and defeats the practice: steal the move, write it in your own words.

Opening move "We call it 'progress' when a machine does our thinking, and 'laziness' when a person lets it — the same act, judged by who profits."
Why it works: opens on a live tension and quietly plants the contention — no throat-clearing ("Since the dawn of time…").
Counter move "The obvious objection is that some limits protect us — and that is right: a limit that prevents harm is not censorship but care. The disagreement is only over where the line sits."
Why it works: concedes something genuinely true, then narrows the dispute instead of surrendering the thesis.
Landing move "Certainty is comfortable, but it is doubt that keeps a mind honest — and a society awake."
Why it works: returns to the essay's key word, resolves it in one image, and stops — no "in conclusion, I have shown…".
