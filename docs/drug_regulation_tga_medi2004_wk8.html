§0Orientation · from last lecture

Where regulation sits in the drug lifecycle

Last lecture built the drug; this one gets it approved.

Last week's lecture on clinical trials walked a candidate molecule from the lab bench toward the clinic. This week picks up the story at the point where the trials are finished and someone has to decide: is this drug allowed onto the Australian market at all? That decision-maker is the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), and to understand what it does you first need to see where its gate sits in the whole journey of a drug.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

Of roughly 100 drug-discovery projects that start, how many do you think end up as an approved, marketed medicine — and how many years does that take?

About one. The funnel runs ~100 discovery projects → ~20 preclinical compounds → ~10 into Phase I → ~5 Phase II → ~2 Phase III → ~1.2 at submission → ~1 approved, over roughly 10–15 years end to end. Regulatory approval is the very last narrow point of that funnel — which is exactly why the gatekeeper matters.
Label Key — the development pipeline (read left to right)
Drug discovery
Target selection, lead-finding, lead optimisation and pharmacological profiling. Why it matters: this is where ~100 projects start — the widest part of the funnel, before any human ever takes the compound. Takes 2–5 years.
Preclinical development
Pharmacokinetics, short-term toxicology, formulation and synthesis scale-up. Why it matters: the first safety filter — done in the lab/animals before the "drug candidate" is chosen for humans. ~1.5 years, ~20 compounds.
Phase I
Pharmacokinetics, tolerability and side effects in healthy volunteers — the first-in-human step; ~10 compounds enter.
Phase II
Small-scale trials in patients to assess efficacy and dosage, plus long-term toxicology studies; ~5 compounds.
Phase III
Large-scale controlled clinical trials — the pivotal efficacy/safety evidence; ~2 compounds. Clinical development as a whole spans 5–7 years.
Regulatory approval
Submission of the full dossier and review by regulatory agencies — this is the TGA's step in Australia. ~1.2 compounds, 1–2 years.
Phase IV
Post-marketing surveillance — watching the ~1 approved drug in the real, marketed population. Why it matters: proves regulation does not stop at the approval stamp.

Trace the bottom row of Plate 0.1: the boxed numbers (~100 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 2 → 1.2 → 1) are the surviving compounds, and the orange callouts mark the two hand-off points — the drug candidate leaving discovery and the regulatory submission entering approval. The whole point of this guide sits in that fifth band, Regulatory Approval: everything the TGA does happens there and in the Phase IV band that follows.

Reasoning · why a separate approval gate exists at all

A drug company has spent 10+ years and a fortune to reach submission — it is the last party you would trust to rule impartially on its own product's safety. The regulatory-approval band is deliberately a separate, independent gate so that the decision to expose the whole population rests with an agency that has nothing to sell. The narrowness of the funnel (100 → 1) is also the reason: by the time a molecule reaches this gate it has already survived enormous attrition, so the remaining risk is subtle and needs expert eyes, not a rubber stamp.

Clinical / real-world

Because Phase III enrols at most a few thousand patients, a harm that occurs once in 10,000 users may never appear before approval. That is precisely why Phase IV surveillance exists — thalidomide and later drug withdrawals (e.g. rofecoxib) were caught only after marketing, when millions were exposed. For a prescriber, this means "TGA-approved" is not a guarantee of zero risk; it is a statement that the benefit-risk balance looked acceptable on the evidence available so far.

R Recall — regulation in the lifecycle
  • TGA's stage — the regulatory approval band, after Phase III and before Phase IV.
  • Attrition funnel — roughly 100 → 1 projects survive from discovery to approval.
  • Why Phase IV — rare/late harms surface only once large populations are exposed.
§1Who checks your medicines

The TGA, therapeutic goods & the ARTG

One agency, one register, one Act — the scaffolding everything else hangs on.

Before we can talk about how a medicine is approved, we need the three fixed pieces: the agency that does it, the register a product must get onto, and the law that makes it compulsory. Get these three names right and the rest of the lecture is just detail hanging off them.

Who is checking your medicines?

  • The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) — the organisation responsible for the import, supply, or export of medicines in Australia. It is the Australian counterpart of the FDA in the USA.
  • The Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 — the law that requires products to be entered on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) before they can be supplied.
  • The sponsor — usually the company that will supply or manufacture the product. The sponsor must submit an application and pay fees to the TGA to make an entry happen.
Reasoning · why "the ARTG" and not just "TGA approval"

Making the register — not the agency's opinion — the legal object is a deliberate design. A single, public, searchable list means a pharmacist, prescriber or customs officer can check one place to know whether a specific product is legally supplied in Australia, and under what conditions. It turns a diffuse "is this drug OK?" question into a concrete "is it on the ARTG, and as what?" This is also why the sponsor pays: the register is a service the government provides, funded by the industry that benefits from access to the market.

What are "therapeutic goods"?

The term is broad and generally falls under three categories, plus a catch-all:

  • Medicines — including prescription, over-the-counter and complementary medicines (e.g. paracetamol and echinacea).
  • Biologicals — something made from or containing human cells or tissues, such as human stem cells or skin.
  • Medical devices — instruments, implants and appliances, such as pacemakers and sterile bandages.
  • Other therapeutic goods (OTGs) — the catch-all: items such as tampons and disinfectants.

Which medicines are regulated?

Under "medicines" the TGA regulates a wide spread of products:

  • Prescription medicines — including original, generic brands, biologics and biosimilars.
  • Over-the-counter (OTC) medicines — including pharmacy, pharmacist-only and unscheduled medicines.
  • Complementary medicines — vitamins, herbal and traditional products.
  • Vaccines.
  • Blood, blood components and plasma derivatives.
Memory hook · "Medicines, Bios, Devices — MBD"

The three legs of "therapeutic goods": Medicines · Biologicals · Devices — then OTG mops up the rest (tampons, disinfectants). "MBD + the leftovers."

Clinical / real-world

Patients routinely assume "natural = unregulated" or "supplement = safe". In fact a bottle of fish oil, a stem-cell product and a pacemaker are all therapeutic goods on the ARTG — but, as §2 shows, held to very different standards. Knowing echinacea is regulated (as a complementary medicine) but only lightly is exactly the nuance that lets you counsel a patient who is mixing supplements with a prescription drug.

R Recall — TGA, ARTG & therapeutic goods
  • TGA — regulates import, supply and export of medicines in Australia (the FDA equivalent).
  • Legal trigger — the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 requires ARTG entry before supply.
  • Sponsor — the company that submits the application and pays the fees.
  • Three categories — medicines, biologicals, medical devices (plus OTGs).
§2Two tiers · AUST R vs AUST L

Registered vs Listed medicines

One register, two doors — and the door you come through is set by risk.

The ARTG is not one-size-fits-all. The TGA's whole approach is risk-based, and in the simplest terms it has two tiers. Higher-risk medicines are registered; lower-risk medicines are listed. Everything downstream — how hard they are checked, what claims they can make, whether they can be scheduled — flows from which tier a product sits in.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

Both tiers are checked for quality and safety. What is the one thing the TGA evaluates for a registered medicine that it does not fully evaluate for a plain listed one?

Efficacy — whether the medicine actually works. Registered medicines are evaluated for quality, safety and efficacy; plain listed medicines are evaluated only for quality and safety. That single gap is why a listed product cannot claim to treat a serious illness — its efficacy was never assessed.

*Assessed listed medicines (AUST L(A)) are the exception: they are tested for efficacy and so can make bigger claims than a plain listed product.

Reasoning · why bother having two tiers?

Evaluating efficacy is slow and expensive — the ~11-month, evidence-heavy process of §3. If every vitamin C tablet had to clear that bar, either they would vanish from shelves or the TGA would drown. The two-tier system is a proportionality device: spend the heavy scrutiny where the risk is high (a new prescription drug), and for genuinely low-risk products restrict them to pre-approved ingredients and limited claims instead. The trade-off is explicit — a listed product buys its easy path by giving up the right to claim it treats anything serious.

§2.1Registered medicines & the AUST R number

Registered medicines carry the higher level of risk. The distribution across product types is worth memorising:

  • All prescription medicines are registered.
  • Most over-the-counter medicines are registered.
  • Few complementary medicines are registered.

They carry an 'AUST R' number on the label and/or packaging. As Plate 2.1 shows, that number is your quick visual tell that a product cleared the full quality-safety-efficacy evaluation.

Clinical / real-world

When a patient shows you a supplement and asks "is this the real thing?", the fastest check is the label number. AUST R = it was assessed for whether it works. AUST L = only quality and safety were checked, so any efficacy claim on the box is either the "limited claims" a listed product is allowed, or an over-reach you should be sceptical of.

§2.2Listed medicines & the AUST L number

Listed medicines carry the lower level of risk. Their distribution mirrors the registered picture:

  • Some over-the-counter medicines are listed.
  • Most complementary medicines are listed.

Key features of a listed medicine:

  • Carries an 'AUST L' number on the label; recently the 'AUST L(A)' ("assessed listed") number was introduced for products whose efficacy has been tested.
  • Must NOT contain substances that are scheduled in the Poisons Standard.
  • Must contain pre-approved ingredients only.

How listed medicines are regulated pre-market

For listed medicines, regulation does not centre on efficacy — it centres on the safety of the ingredients and the consistency of manufacturing:

  • Only pre-approved low-risk ingredients may be used; listed medicines (including most complementary medicines) receive a lesser degree of checking than higher-risk products.
  • Independent expert advice may be sought on new ingredients proposed for use in listed medicines.
  • Sponsors must hold evidence to support their claims — even though the TGA does not pre-check it.
  • Listing on the ARTG is the final stage of the process and usually occurs once the sponsor has paid their fees and signed a statutory declaration.

Conditions for supplying low-risk medicines

A listed (low-risk) medicine — the lecture's examples are echinacea, fish oil, vitamin C tablets, iron tablets — must satisfy two conditions:

  1. Contains pre-approved low-risk ingredients.
  2. Does not claim or imply it will be useful in the treatment or prevention of serious illnesses (except assessed-listed products).

Separately, the TGA inspects and licenses manufacturing sites in Australia and assesses sites overseas. This lets it enforce standards for the sterility and quality of the ingredients used and the process followed — so even a lightly-checked product is made in a controlled facility.

Reasoning · a "statutory declaration" instead of an inspection?

For a genuinely low-risk product, having the TGA independently verify every claim would be disproportionate. So the system shifts the burden: the sponsor signs a legal declaration that they hold the evidence, and must produce it if audited. It is regulation by accountability rather than pre-approval — cheap and fast up front, but with real legal teeth if the declaration turns out to be false. Manufacturing site licensing is kept mandatory because a contamination or sterility failure is a safety risk regardless of how low-risk the ingredient is.

Clinical / real-world

The gap this creates is why the TGA can, and does, later recall or cancel listed products when post-market testing finds an unsupported claim or a quality failure — the up-front check was light, so the back-end enforcement carries the load. Counsel patients that an AUST L number means "made properly, ingredients allowed" — not "proven to work".

R Recall — registered vs listed
  • Registered — higher risk; quality, safety AND efficacy; AUST R.
  • Listed — lower risk; quality and safety only; AUST L.
  • Prescription meds — all registered; most complementary meds are listed.
  • Listed claim limit — cannot imply treatment of serious illness (unless AUST L(A)).
  • Listed listing trigger — usually after fees paid + statutory declaration signed.
§3The evaluation process

Registering a new medicine

Roughly eleven months of scientists reading data — turned into a five-step loop.

This is the heavy machinery: what actually happens when a sponsor submits a new higher-risk prescription medicine for registration. It takes approximately 11 months to evaluate one, and it is run by scientists and clinicians who examine data on quality, and on safety and efficacy. The process is drawn as a cycle for a reason.

Label Key — the five-step evaluation cycle
1. Preparation & lodgement
The sponsor assembles and submits the dossier. Why it matters: the clock and the fees start here — a badly-prepared dossier fails screening.
2. Screening
A completeness check: is the application even suitable to evaluate? Why it matters: weeds out incomplete submissions before scarce expert time is spent.
3. Evaluation
The core scientific review of quality, safety and efficacy data by chemists, toxicologists and doctors. Why it matters: this is where the ~11 months mostly go.
4. Decision
The TGA delegate weighs benefit against risk and decides to register, register with conditions, or reject.
5. Finalisation
Entry on the ARTG, approval of the Product Information, and communication of the outcome.
Central circle
Process of checking and communicating with the applicant — the two-headed arrows show every step is a dialogue, not a one-way verdict. Why it matters: the sponsor answers questions throughout, which is why it's a loop, not a line.

Follow the arrows in Plate 3.1: the five numbered boxes ring a central hub of checking and communicating with the applicant. That layout is the teaching point — evaluation is not a conveyor belt where the sponsor drops off a box and waits; at every step the TGA can go back to the applicant for clarification, which is part of why one medicine takes ~11 months.

Memory hook · "PS-EDF" — Please Screen, Evaluate, Decide, Finalise

Preparation → Screening → Evaluation → Decision → Finalisation. Say it as "Prepare, Screen, Evaluate, Decide, Finalise" — five steps, all orbiting the applicant conversation in the middle.

§3.1Decisions are based on evidence

The evaluation (step 3) rests on two evidence streams, both supplied by the applicant but scrutinised by TGA experts.

Quality data

Evaluated by chemists, biochemists, microbiologists and others working for the TGA. It covers:

  • the composition of the substance and the product;
  • batch consistency — is every batch the same?
  • the impurity content;
  • stability data — does it survive shelf storage?
  • sterility data — if applicable (e.g. injectables).

Safety and efficacy data

This stream splits in two:

Safety & efficacy evidence — two arms
AspectNonclinical dataClinical data
Evaluated byToxicologists and pharmaceutical chemistsUsually a medical doctor
What it isLaboratory data: pharmacology (efficacy), pharmacokinetic, and toxicology (safety) dataMostly results of clinical trials in volunteers
Run byLab studiesPharmaceutical companies and/or hospitals or research organisations

Note the neat pairing inside nonclinical data: pharmacology data investigates efficacy, while toxicology data investigates safety — the two questions the whole exercise is trying to answer, addressed first in the lab and then in humans.

Reasoning · why the applicant supplies the very data being judged

It looks like a conflict of interest — the company that wants approval also generates the evidence. But the regulator has neither the labs nor the patients to run trials itself; only the sponsor can. The safeguard is not who collects the data but who judges it: independent TGA chemists, toxicologists and doctors re-analyse the raw data, and the quality-data checks (batch consistency, impurities, stability) exist precisely to confirm the product actually is what the paperwork says. Trust, then verify.

Worked example · reading the attrition funnel as probabilities

The development figure (Plate 0.1) shows ~10 compounds entering Phase I and ~1 finally approved. Treat those counts as a rough success cascade and estimate the chance that a compound entering Phase I ends up approved.

  1. State the relationship. Overall survival is the fraction that makes it through: P = N_out / N_in, where N_in is compounds entering Phase I and N_out is compounds approved.
  2. Read the numbers off Plate 0.1: N_in = 10 (enter Phase I), N_out = 1 (approved).
  3. Substitute: P = 1 / 10 = 0.10.
  4. Convert to a percentage: 0.10 × 100% = 10%.
  5. Sanity-check against the intermediate steps: 10 → 5 → 2 → 1.2 → 1 multiplies out to 5/10 × 2/5 × 1.2/2 × 1/1.2 ≈ 0.10 — consistent.

Answer: roughly a 10% chance that a compound entering first-in-human trials is ever approved — which is why regulatory review is a filter, not a formality.

§3.2Assessing and managing risk & the Product Information

Approval is not "is this drug safe?" (nothing is perfectly safe) but "do the benefits outweigh the risks for this population and this use?". The TGA's role in risk management is to:

  • Consider the risks — what are they?
  • Balance benefits with risks.
  • Consider whether the medicine should be used in the proposed population for the proposed indication.
  • Use the Product Information (PI) document as a risk-management tool.
  • Consider other risk-management tools.

The Product Information document — a risk-management tool

  • The PI document is approved by the TGA.
  • The product is only authorised for a specified patient population, for specified indications.
  • All other use is 'off-label' — and for off-label use the benefit-risk profile has not been considered by the TGA.
  • The precautions section of the PI gives details of some of the risks involved.
Reasoning · why the PI is called a "risk-management tool"

The TGA cannot stand in every consultation room, so it manages risk by document. By approving a precise PI — this drug, this population, this indication, these precautions — it draws a boundary inside which the benefit-risk balance has been examined. Everything outside that boundary is "off-label": still legal for a doctor to prescribe, but now the risk sits with the prescriber's clinical judgement, not the TGA's evaluation. The PI is thus how a one-off approval decision keeps shaping millions of future prescriptions.

Clinical / real-world

"Off-label" is not "illegal" or "wrong" — huge areas of paediatrics and oncology run off-label because trials in those groups are scarce. But it does mean the prescriber shoulders the benefit-risk call the TGA hasn't made, should document the rationale, and must read the PI's precautions section carefully. This is the direct clinical payoff of understanding what a PI is.

R Recall — registering a new medicine
  • Timeline — a new higher-risk prescription medicine takes ~11 months to evaluate.
  • Five steps — Preparation → Screening → Evaluation → Decision → Finalisation, around applicant dialogue.
  • Quality data judges — chemists, biochemists, microbiologists (composition, batches, impurities, stability, sterility).
  • Nonclinical vs clinical — lab pharmacology/PK/tox vs trial results in volunteers.
  • Product Information — TGA-approved; use outside it is off-label, unassessed by the TGA.
§4The Poisons Standard

Medicines scheduling & the Poisons Standard

A legal ladder: the higher you climb, the harder the drug is to reach.

Some approved medicines are still dangerous if misused. Scheduling is the separate legal machinery that decides how tightly access to a given substance is controlled. It sits alongside registration: a drug is first registered as safe/effective enough to market, and then scheduled to control how it is supplied.

  • Potentially dangerous drugs and chemicals are restricted to enable their safe and effective use.
  • Scheduling is the legal process used to achieve this restriction.
  • The higher the schedule number, the more restricted the access.
  • Scheduling decisions are published on the TGA website.
  • Remember: only registered medicines are scheduled. (The opium poppy and cannabis leaf on the slide are the classic source-plants of scheduled substances — morphine and cannabis.)

The lecture's worked examples are laid out in Plate 2.1 (the scheduling table). Read down its Schedule classification column and you climb the ladder rung by rung:

Examples of scheduled medicines (from Plate 2.1)

Memory hook · the access ladder "2-3-4-8"

Climb the counter: S2 = grab it off the pharmacy shelf, S3 = ask the pharmacist, S4 = need a prescription, S8 = locked in the safe (controlled drug). Each rung up moves the medicine one step further out of easy reach.

Reasoning · why restrict a medicine that's already been approved?

Registration answers "is the benefit-risk balance acceptable when used properly?" — but it can't stop misuse. Scheduling manages the access risk separately: a strong opioid may be perfectly safe for a cancer patient yet catastrophic if freely available, so S8 bolts on storage-and-supply controls (locked safes, registers, restricted prescribing) on top of the drug's own risk profile. Splitting "is it a good drug?" from "how tightly should we control it?" lets the same paracetamol be an unscheduled small pack yet a Schedule 2 large pack — the substance is identical; the access risk differs with quantity.

Clinical / real-world

The ladder is why the same drug can move between shelves: a medicine can be down-scheduled to widen access once it's proven safe enough (many PPIs became pharmacist-only S3), or up-scheduled when harm emerges (codeine combinations were up-scheduled to prescription-only). For a clinician, an S8 label is an instant flag for the extra legal duties — controlled-drug registers, secure storage, and stricter prescribing rules.

R Recall — scheduling
  • Poisons Standard — the legal process that restricts access to dangerous substances.
  • Direction of the ladder — higher number = more restricted access.
  • S2 / S3 / S4 / S8 — pharmacy-only / pharmacist-only / prescription-only / controlled drug.
  • S8 extra — additional storage and supply restrictions (e.g. strong analgesics).
  • Key rule — only registered medicines are scheduled.
§5Exceptions & promotion

Special access & advertising

Doors for the unapproved drug a patient genuinely needs — and hard limits on selling drugs.

The ARTG is a wall, but not an impenetrable one. Sometimes a patient needs a product that is not approved for use in Australia. The TGA provides controlled routes for exactly this — and, at the other end, tightly polices how any medicine may be advertised.

Patients sometimes need special access

The TGA helps health professionals gain access to products their patients need but which have not been approved for use in Australia, via three schemes:

  • Clinical Trial Exemption (CTX) and Clinical Trial Notification (CTN) schemes — access unapproved medicines through participation in a clinical trial.
  • Special Access Scheme (SAS) — import and/or supply an unapproved therapeutic good for a single patient on a case-by-case basis.
  • Authorised Prescribers — can prescribe a specified therapeutic good (more than once) to patients with a particular medical condition.
Three routes to an unapproved product
SchemeWho / howScope
CTX / CTNPatient enrols in a clinical trialAccess via trial participation
SASHealth professional imports/suppliesSingle patient, case-by-case
Authorised PrescriberAn approved prescriberRepeatedly, for patients with a particular condition
Reasoning · why not just approve the drug if patients need it?

Full registration takes ~11 months and a complete dossier — a timeframe that is useless to a patient dying now, or to a rare-disease drug no sponsor will bother registering for a tiny Australian market. The three schemes are graded escape hatches sized to the need: a trial (structured, monitored) for research access; SAS (one patient, one decision) for the genuine one-off; and Authorised Prescriber (repeat supply, defined condition) when the same unapproved drug is needed again and again. Each keeps a paper trail so access stays controlled rather than becoming a loophole.

Clinical / real-world

These schemes are the mechanism behind headlines about patients accessing medicinal cannabis or an overseas cancer drug before local approval. The practical distinction to remember: SAS = one patient, one time; Authorised Prescriber = same drug, many times, defined condition. Choosing the right pathway is a real clinical-administrative decision.

Advertising medicines

  • The labelling, packaging and description of what a medicine should be used for are all regulated by the TGA.
  • Advertising of prescription medicines to consumers is illegal in Australia.
Reasoning · why ban direct-to-consumer prescription ads?

A prescription medicine is, by definition, one where a professional's judgement is needed to weigh benefit against risk (that's why it's S4). Advertising it straight to consumers would drive demand for a decision that the patient isn't equipped to make alone, pressuring prescribers and inflating use of newer, dearer drugs. Australia (unlike the USA) draws the line hard: control the information — labelling and packaging — but forbid the selling of prescription drugs to the public.

R Recall — access & advertising
  • CTX / CTN — access to an unapproved medicine through a clinical trial.
  • SAS — single patient, case-by-case import/supply of an unapproved good.
  • Authorised Prescriber — repeat prescribing for a particular condition.
  • Advertising — TGA regulates labelling/packaging; DTC prescription-medicine ads are illegal in Australia.
§6What's in the product

Ingredients, generics & biosimilars

The vocabulary of "same drug, different brand" — and why "same" means different things for pills and biologics.

The final learning objective is definitional: active ingredient, excipient, generic medicine and biosimilar. These four terms underpin every conversation about brand substitution — and the difference between a generic and a biosimilar is one of the sharper exam distinctions in the deck.

Active and excipient ingredients

  • An active ingredient is a therapeutically active component — the part that does the pharmacological work.
  • An excipient is an ingredient in the medicine's final formulation that is NOT therapeutically active — e.g. preservatives, tablet coatings, and ingredients contributing to fragrance or flavour.
  • Therapeutic goods in Australia must use 'approved names' in their formulations.
Reasoning · why insist on "approved names"?

The same chemical can carry many trade and colloquial names across countries — a recipe for dispensing errors. Mandating a single approved name per substance means the active ingredient is identified unambiguously on every label and in every prescription, so a pharmacist in Perth reads the same ingredient name as one in Brisbane. It's a small rule that quietly prevents a large class of medication errors.

§6.1Generic medicines must be bioequivalent

A generic medicine is an additional brand of an existing medicine. It contains the same active ingredient as the existing medicine, and it must be bioequivalent.

  • Bioequivalence — whether the generic releases the active ingredient into the bloodstream at the same rate and to the same extent as the original medicine.
  • It is tested directly: blood samples are taken at different times, and the rate and extent of absorption of the active ingredient into the blood is compared for the generic and the original.

A generic and its original brand must match on a defined list:

What a generic must share with the brand-name original

Reasoning · why "bioequivalent" rather than "identical"?

Demanding an identical product would forbid a competitor from using a different (cheaper, non-infringing) excipient or coating — and there'd be no generics market at all. What actually matters clinically is the concentration-time curve the active ingredient produces in the blood: if two products put the same amount of drug into the circulation at the same speed, they will behave the same in the patient. So the law targets the outcome (bioequivalence) not the recipe (excipients), which is exactly why the excipients are allowed to differ.

Clinical / real-world

Bioequivalence is why a pharmacist can substitute a cheaper generic for the branded drug — and why that saves the health system money without changing the therapeutic effect. It's also why a patient reacting to a "new brand" is usually reacting to a changed excipient (a dye or filler), not the active drug. For a handful of "narrow therapeutic index" drugs, though, even small bioequivalence variation matters, which is why substitution isn't always automatic.

§6.2Biosimilars

A biosimilar is a version of an already-registered biological medicine. It is a biological medicine that is comparable in quality, safety and efficacy to the reference biological medicine — note "comparable", not "identical".

A biosimilar and its reference share the following characteristics, demonstrated using comprehensive comparability studies:

  • Physicochemical characteristics;
  • Biological characteristics;
  • Immunological characteristics;
  • Efficacy and safety.
Reasoning · why a biosimilar can only be "similar", never "generic"

A generic small-molecule drug (e.g. paracetamol) is a defined chemical you can synthesise atom-for-atom identically. A biological — an antibody, a hormone — is a huge, folded protein grown in living cells; the exact molecule depends on the cell line and manufacturing conditions, so no two makers can produce a truly identical copy. The best you can do is show comparability across physicochemical, biological, immunological and clinical measures. That's why the word is "biosimilar", and why proving it needs comparability studies rather than a simple bioequivalence blood test.

Memory hook · "Generic = identical twin; Biosimilar = sibling"

A generic is the same molecule (identical twin) — prove it with a bioequivalence blood test. A biosimilar is a family resemblance (sibling) — prove it with comprehensive comparability studies across physicochemical, biological, immunological, and efficacy/safety measures.

Clinical / real-world

The generic-vs-biosimilar distinction has real prescribing consequences: because biosimilars aren't identical, substitution rules are more cautious than for generics, and immunogenicity (an immune response to the protein) is monitored specifically — which is why "immunological" comparability is on the list. As biologics dominate modern therapeutics (cancer, autoimmune disease), understanding biosimilars is where much of the future cost-saving in medicine sits.

R Recall — ingredients, generics & biosimilars
  • Active vs excipient — therapeutically active vs not (preservatives, coatings, flavour).
  • Generic — same active ingredient, must be bioequivalent; excipients may differ.
  • Bioequivalence — same rate and extent of active ingredient into the bloodstream.
  • Biosimilar — comparable (not identical) version of a registered biological.
  • Proof needed — generic = bioequivalence test; biosimilar = comprehensive comparability studies.
§7After approval · affordability

The Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS)

Approved to sell is not the same as approved to subsidise.

A medicine can clear the TGA and still cost a patient hundreds of dollars. The final piece of the Australian system is the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) — a separate government program that decides which approved medicines the taxpayer will help pay for.

  • The PBS is an Australian Government program that benefits all Australians by making medicine more affordable.
  • The PBS Schedule lists all the medicines available to be dispensed to patients at a Government-subsidised price.
  • Not all medicines approved by the TGA are listed on the PBS.
  • Sometimes medicines are subsidised for one indication but not for others, or conditions apply — usually related to quality of evidence and cost.
Reasoning · why split "may be sold" from "will be subsidised"?

The two decisions answer different questions. The TGA asks a safety/efficacy question — "is this good enough to be on the market?" The PBS asks an economic question — "is it worth the public purse subsidising, at this price, for this indication?" A drug can easily pass the first and fail the second: it works, but it's not cost-effective versus what's already funded. Keeping them separate lets the government control health spending without having to ban effective-but-expensive drugs — they simply stay available at full price.

Clinical / real-world

This is why a patient can be handed a legal, TGA-approved script and then face a shock at the counter: the drug isn't PBS-listed for their indication, so they pay full price. It's also why new cancer drugs are often "approved but not yet subsidised" — the cost-effectiveness case (run by the PBAC) lags behind TGA approval by months or years. Knowing the TGA/PBS split lets you set a patient's expectations honestly.

R Recall — the PBS
  • PBS purpose — a government program making medicines more affordable via subsidy.
  • PBS Schedule — lists medicines dispensable at a government-subsidised price.
  • Key gap — not all TGA-approved medicines are PBS-listed.
  • Conditions — subsidy may be indication-specific, driven by evidence quality and cost.
  • TGA vs PBS — may it be sold? vs will it be subsidised?
R Master recall — the whole TGA picture
  • TGA role — regulates import, supply, export of medicines; requires ARTG entry under the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989.
  • Therapeutic goods — medicines, biologicals, medical devices (plus OTGs like tampons/disinfectants).
  • Two tiers — Registered (AUST R): quality, safety, efficacy; Listed (AUST L): quality, safety only.
  • New-medicine evaluation — ~11 months, five steps Preparation→Screening→Evaluation→Decision→Finalisation.
  • Evidence — quality data (chemists) + safety/efficacy (nonclinical lab tox/pharmacology + clinical trials).
  • Product Information — TGA-approved risk tool; outside it is off-label.
  • Scheduling — higher number = tighter access; S2/S3/S4/S8 = pharmacy/pharmacist/prescription/controlled; only registered meds scheduled.
  • Special access — CTX/CTN (trial), SAS (one patient), Authorised Prescriber (repeat).
  • Advertising — DTC prescription-medicine advertising is illegal in Australia.
  • Generic — same active ingredient + bioequivalent; biosimilar — comparable, not identical, biological.
  • PBS — separate subsidy scheme; not all TGA-approved medicines are PBS-listed.
