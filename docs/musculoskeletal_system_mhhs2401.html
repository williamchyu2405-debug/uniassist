§0Part A · organisation

Organisation of the musculoskeletal system

Bones, cartilage and ligaments — one framework doing five jobs, split into a protective core and a mobile periphery.

The skeletal system is the bones of the skeleton plus the cartilages and ligaments that interconnect them — 206 bones of varied shape, size and tissue organisation.

The five functions of the skeletal system

  • Support — a framework that bears weight and anchors soft tissue.
  • Protection — hard cases around organs (skull, thoracic cage, vertebral canal).
  • Storage — a reservoir of minerals (calcium, phosphate) and lipids (yellow marrow).
  • Blood cell production — haematopoiesis in red marrow.
  • Leverage — bones act as levers, changing the magnitude & direction of muscle force.
The two divisions of the skeleton
DivisionContentsBonesJob
AxialSkull, vertebral column, sacrum & coccyx, thoracic cage80Central axis — protection
AppendicularLimbs + shoulder & pelvic girdles126Attaches limbs — movement
Memory hook

Axial = the axis (head + spine + ribcage). Appendicular = the appendages (limbs + girdles). 80 + 126 = 206.

§1Part A · bone tissue

Bone tissue & cells

Two tissue textures, one repeating unit, four cell types.

Bone comes in two tissue textures: compact (cortical) — the dense outer shell — and spongy (cancellous / trabecular) — a lattice of struts that houses marrow.

§1.1Compact-bone histology — the osteon

Compact bone is built from repeating cylinders called osteons (Haversian systems):

Parts of an osteon
StructureWhat it is
Central (Haversian) canalRuns up the osteon's core — carries blood vessels & nerves.
Concentric lamellaeRings of mineralised matrix around the canal.
LacunaeSmall spaces that each hold an osteocyte (labelled 7 in the module — "space for the osteocyte").
CanaliculiTiny channels connecting lacunae — let osteocytes exchange nutrients & signals.

§1.2The four bone cells

  • Osteoprogenitor cells — stem cells that divide into osteoblasts.
  • Osteoblasts — build bone: secrete osteoid (unmineralised collagen-I matrix).
  • Osteocytes — mature osteoblasts trapped in lacunae; maintain bone & sense load.
  • Osteoclasts — large cells that resorb (break down) bone.
Reasoning · why a canal at the core

Osteocytes are walled into solid mineral, so they can't get nutrients by diffusion alone. The Haversian canal brings the blood supply into the middle of the bone, and canaliculi form a relay of tiny pipes so every osteocyte stays fed — which is why bone is living, remodelling tissue, not inert rock.

Bone develops from the mesoderm germ layer; its embryonic connective-tissue precursor is mesenchyme.

§2Part A · bone formation

Osteogenesis, growth & remodelling

Two ways to make bone, one plate that lengthens it, and a lifelong turnover.

Osteogenesis (bone development) begins from mesenchymal precursors and follows one of two routes.

Two types of ossification
EndochondralIntramembranous
RouteMesenchyme → chondroblasts → a cartilage model, later replaced by boneMesenchyme condenses → osteoblasts deposit osteoid directly (no cartilage model)
DetailChondroblasts secrete matrix & get trapped as chondrocytesOsteoblasts trapped in osteoid become osteocytes
MakesMost long bonesFlat bones (e.g. skull)

§2.1Growth in length

Bones grow in length until early adulthood at the epiphyseal plate — a thin plate of hyaline cartilage between the diaphysis and epiphysis. When longitudinal growth stops, the plate ossifies into the epiphyseal line.

§2.2Remodelling

Bone is continuously renewed by the balance of osteoblast deposition and osteoclast resorption — reshaping bone to load and repairing microdamage.

Clinical / real-world

Tip the balance and disease follows: too much osteoclast activity (or too little osteoblast) thins bone toward osteoporosis; the growth plate is also the weak point in children, so fractures there (Salter–Harris) can disturb growth.

§3Part A · bones

Bones — classification & long-bone anatomy

Two ways to sort a bone, and the anatomy of the reference model.

Bones are classified by internal tissue organisation (compact vs spongy, §1) and by shape.

  • Long — shaft + two ends (femur, humerus): leverage.
  • Short — cube-shaped (carpals, tarsals): stability with limited gliding.
  • Flat — thin, often curved (skull, sternum, ribs): protection & broad attachment.
  • Irregular — complex (vertebrae, facial bones).
  • Sesamoid — embedded in a tendon (patella): protects the tendon & improves leverage.

§3.1Anatomy of a long bone

Long-bone regions & coverings
PartWhat it is
DiaphysisThe shaft — compact bone around the medullary cavity.
EpiphysisExpanded end — spongy bone under a compact shell; forms the joint surface.
MetaphysisZone between them — holds the growth plate in the young.
Medullary cavityCentral space with marrow (red = blood cells; yellow = fat).
PeriosteumOuter covering: outer fibrous layer (absent on articular surfaces & sesamoids) + inner cellular layer (osteoprogenitors — key in healing/remodelling). Anchored by Sharpey's fibres; vascular & innervated.
EndosteumThin membrane lining the inner bony wall, Haversian canals & trabeculae.
Clinical / real-world · why a broken bone hurts so sharply

The periosteum is richly innervated, so trauma to bone produces sharp, well-localised pain that originates from the periosteum — while the mineral interior itself has few pain fibres.

Reasoning · bones fuse with age

Infants have more than 206 bones: many start as separate pieces and fuse later. The hip bone, for instance, begins as three bones — ilium, ischium and pubis — that fuse into one at the acetabulum. Fewer, fused bones trade the flexibility of growth for adult strength.

§4Part B · joints

Joints (articulations)

Classify by how much it moves, and by what holds it together.

A joint is where two bones meet. Two classifications overlap — functional (movement) and structural (tissue + cavity).

Functional classes
ClassMovementExample
SynarthrosisNoneSkull suture
AmphiarthrosisSlightPubic symphysis
DiarthrosisFreeSynovial joints (knee, shoulder)

§4.1Structural classes

  • Fibrous — no cavity, dense connective tissue: sutures (skull), syndesmoses (tibia–fibula), gomphoses (tooth in socket).
  • Cartilaginous — no cavity, joined by cartilage: synchondroses (primary, hyaline — e.g. growth plate) & symphyses (secondary, fibrocartilage — e.g. pubic symphysis).
  • Synovial — a fluid-filled cavity; the freely-movable joints.

§4.2The synovial joint

A synovial joint's parts: hyaline articular cartilage (reduces friction, absorbs shock), a fibrous capsule, a synovial membrane (makes synovial fluid that lubricates), and ligaments (bone-to-bone). Its stability comes from three things: the shape of the articular surfaces, the ligaments, and muscle tone.

The six synovial joint types
TypeExampleMovement
PlanarFacet (intercarpal) jointsGliding
HingeElbow, ankleOne plane (flex/extend)
PivotAtlanto-axial; proximal radio-ulnarRotation
CondyloidWrist (radiocarpal)Two planes
SaddleThumb carpometacarpalTwo planes + opposition
Ball-and-socketShoulder (glenohumeral), hipAll planes

Accessory structures

  • Articular disc — fibrocartilage between articular surfaces (e.g. the knee).
  • Labrum — a fibrocartilage ring that deepens a socket (e.g. the shoulder).
  • Bursa — a fluid-filled sac that reduces friction between bone and tendon/ligament.
Reasoning · shape sets the movement

A hinge is two matched cylinders → one plane of motion; a ball-and-socket removes those constraints → every plane, but less stable (why the shoulder dislocates and a suture never does). You can predict a joint's movements from its geometry alone.

§5Part B · muscle

Muscle

~700 engines — arranged for force or range, attached fixed-to-moving, and named so the name tells you what they do.

The muscular system is roughly 700 skeletal muscles. How the fascicles (fibre bundles) are arranged trades force against range.

Fascicle arrangements
ArrangementPatternExample
ParallelFascicles run the muscle's lengthSartorius
FusiformParallel but the belly bulges, tapering at the endsDigastric
CircularConcentric rings around an orifice (sphincter)Orbicularis oculi
ConvergentBroad origin converging on one tendon (triangular)Pectoralis major
PennateShort fascicles angled onto a tendon — more fibres = more force. Uni (extensor digitorum longus), bi (rectus femoris), multi (deltoid)—

Origin = attachment to the stationary bone (usually proximal); insertion = attachment to the movable bone (usually distal).

Supportive connective tissue

  • Tendon — a rope attaching muscle to bone.
  • Aponeurosis — a broad flat sheet connecting muscle to muscle/bone.
  • Fascia — wraps muscles like a stocking; deep fascia forms intermuscular septa → muscle compartments.

§5.1Reading a muscle's name

Anatomists build muscle names from descriptive terms — region, position, shape, and action — so the name is a clue to the muscle.

Memory hook

Read the name in pieces: Flexor · pollicis · longus = "the long muscle that flexes the thumb." Extensor · carpi · radialis = "extends the wrist, on the radial side." The name is the job description.

§6Part C · upper limb

Upper limb

Regions, bones, fascial compartments, and the nerves & vessels threading through.

The upper limb is organised into regions — pectoral/clavicular/scapular and the axilla, then arm, cubital region, forearm, wrist, palm and digits.

§6.1Osteology

Bones, proximal → distal: clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges — jointed by the elbow, proximal & distal radio-ulnar, wrist, carpometacarpal, metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints.

§6.2Fascia & compartments

Limb fascia encloses and separates muscles into compartments:

  • Superficial fascia — subcutaneous; fat + superficial vessels, nerves & lymphatics.
  • Deep fascia — continuation of the pectoral & axillary fascia: brachial fascia (arm), antebrachial fascia (forearm). Its thickenings at the wrist form the flexor retinaculum (anterior) and extensor retinaculum (posterior).

Transitional zones (clinically important — packed with neurovascular structures)

  • The axilla — gateway between trunk and arm.
  • The cubital fossa — the front of the elbow.
  • The carpal tunnel — under the flexor retinaculum at the wrist.

§6.3Muscles, nerves, dermatomes & myotomes

Muscles sit in fascial compartments; hand muscles are intrinsic (both attachments in the hand) or extrinsic (proximal attachment on the humeral epicondyles/forearm, acting on forearm & hand).

Nerves come from the brachial plexus (roots C5–T1). They're mixed nerves — motor (efferent, brain → muscle) and sensory (afferent, skin/muscle/joint → cord → cortex) — bundled together in connective tissue (epineurium).

Memory hook · dermatome vs myotome

Dermatome = a strip of skin from one spinal nerve. Myotome = the muscle supplied by one spinal nerve.

§6.4Blood supply & drainage

Arteries: subclavian → axillary (at the lateral border of the 1st rib) → brachial, which splits in the cubital fossa into the ulnar (larger) and radial (smaller) arteries → the superficial & deep palmar arches → digital arteries.

Veins: two sets — deep and superficial — linked by perforating veins whose valves stop backflow from deep to superficial during muscle contraction.

§7Part D · development

How a limb is built

From a bump on the flank to a rotated, jointed limb — two mesoderms, one ridge, well-timed cell death.

Development is continuous from a totipotent zygote: embryonic period = weeks 1–8 (organs form — the vulnerable window), foetal = week 9 to birth.

Gastrulation forms the three germ layers; the entire MSK system arises from mesoderm. Limbs begin at the end of week 4, and two mesoderms divide the labour:

  • Lateral plate mesoderm → the limb's skeleton (bones & connective tissue).
  • Paraxial mesoderm (somites) → the muscle (and vascular components) that migrate in.

Each limb bud is a mesodermal core under ectoderm, capped by the apical ectodermal ridge (AER), which signals the mesoderm beneath it to keep growing — driving outgrowth shoulder-to-fingertip.

Memory hook

Lateral Plate → the Platform (skeleton). Somite → the Soft movers (muscle). Each somite splits into sclerotome (skeleton: vertebrae/ribs), myotome (muscle), dermotome (dermis).

Timeline: motor axons enter the bud in week 5; limbs rotate in week 8 (upper dorsally, lower ventrally — why elbows and knees point opposite ways); digits are freed when apoptosis deletes the tissue between the digital rays.

§8Part D · when it goes wrong

Limb anomalies

Each defect is a step from §7 that failed.

Extremes: meromelia (partial absence of a limb) and amelia (whole-limb absence). Causes are genetic, teratogenic, mechanical or vascular, and the embryonic weeks 1–8 are the vulnerable window.

Common limb anomalies
AnomalyMechanism
SyndactylyFailed interdigital apoptosis (§7) — digits stay webbed (cutaneous) or fused (osseous).
PolydactylyExtra digits; inherited as a dominant trait.
Clubfoot (talipes)Multifactorial; ~1:1000 births; foot mis-positioned for weight-bearing.
AchondroplasiaFGFR3 mutation, autosomal dominant — impairs cartilage→bone conversion → short limbs.
Clinical / real-world · thalidomide

Thalidomide, sold in the late 1950s–60s as a "safe" morning-sickness drug, caused catastrophic limb defects (meromelia/amelia) taken in early pregnancy — the defining lesson in teratology, and why preconception medication review matters.

R Recall checklist — the module
  • Skeletal functions — support, protection, storage, blood cell production, leverage. Axial 80 / appendicular 126 = 206.
  • Bone tissue — compact (osteons: Haversian canal, lamellae, lacunae, canaliculi) vs spongy. Cells: osteoprogenitor, osteoblast, osteocyte, osteoclast.
  • Ossification — endochondral (cartilage model) vs intramembranous (direct). Growth at epiphyseal plate → epiphyseal line. Remodel = osteoblast vs osteoclast.
  • Bone shapes — long, short, flat, irregular, sesamoid. Long bone: diaphysis/epiphysis/metaphysis/marrow; periosteum (Sharpey's fibres, innervated) + endosteum.
  • Joints — functional (syn/amphi/di); structural fibrous (suture/syndesmosis/gomphosis), cartilaginous (synchondrosis/symphysis), synovial. Six synovial types; stability = shape+ligaments+tone; disc/labrum/bursa.
  • Muscle — parallel/fusiform/circular/convergent/pennate; origin(fixed)/insertion(moved); tendon/aponeurosis/fascia; names encode region/position/shape/action.
  • Upper limb — clavicle→phalanges; superficial vs deep fascia (retinacula); axilla/cubital fossa/carpal tunnel; brachial plexus C5–T1 (mixed, epineurium); dermatome vs myotome; subclavian→axillary→brachial→radial/ulnar→palmar arches; deep+superficial veins.
  • Limb development — MSK from mesoderm; limbs start wk4; lateral plate → skeleton, somite → muscle; AER → outgrowth; somite → sclero/myo/dermotome; rotation wk8; digits by apoptosis.
  • Anomalies — meromelia/amelia; syndactyly, polydactyly, clubfoot, achondroplasia (FGFR3), thalidomide.
