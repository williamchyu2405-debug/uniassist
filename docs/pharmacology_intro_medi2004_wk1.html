§0Definitions

What is a drug?

Three words students blur together — drug, medicine, drug class — pulled apart.

Pharmacology is the study of drugs and how they interact with living systems. So the first job is to pin down what a "drug" actually is.

A drug is any substance that produces a biological effect when administered to a living organism. Drugs come from:

  • Synthetic chemicals — made in the lab.
  • Natural chemicals — from plants (morphine from the opium poppy), animals, or microorganisms (penicillin from a mould).
  • Products of genetic engineering — e.g. recombinant biologics.

A key subtlety: substances the body makes itself via physiological mechanisms are not drugs — unless they are also administered. Your own insulin is a hormone; injected insulin is a drug.

Label Key — the three words
Drug
The active chemical itself (e.g. morphine, THC) — what produces the biological effect.
Medicine
A drug formulated for therapeutic use — the active drug plus excipients, in a dose form (tablet, injection). A medicine contains a drug.
Drug class
A group of drugs sharing a mechanism, target, or therapeutic use (see §3).
Clinical / real-world · are vitamins, Panadol & herbs "drugs"?

The tutorial asks exactly this. Apply the definition — does it produce a biological effect when administered? Panadol (paracetamol) clearly does — it's a drug. Herbs like cannabis contain active drugs (THC), so they act as drugs. Vitamins & minerals are nutrients at normal intake, but at pharmacological doses they too produce drug-like effects. The label ("supplement", "herbal") doesn't decide it — the effect on the body does.

§1The field

The field of pharmacology

From magical potions to biopharmaceuticals — and the branches that grew along the way.

Pharmacology grew from herbal and "magical" remedies into a rigorous science of biopharmaceuticals — drugs that are purified or synthesised, tested for evidence of effect and safety, and increasingly engineered (biologics, monoclonal antibodies).

Today the field has several subdivisions (Plate 1.1), each asking a different question about the drug–body relationship.

Label Key — the main branches
Pharmacokinetics
How the body handles the drug (§2).
Pharmacodynamics
How the drug acts on the body (§2).
Pharmacotherapeutics
Using drugs to treat disease.
Toxicology
The harmful effects of chemicals & drugs.
Pharmacogenetics
How genetic differences change drug response.
Clinical pharmacology
Drug use and effects in real patients.
§2The core distinction

Pharmacokinetics vs Pharmacodynamics

The single idea the whole unit is built on — get it now and everything else slots in.

Every drug–body interaction has two directions, and pharmacology splits along that line.

The two directions of a drug–body interaction
Pharmacokinetics (PK)Pharmacodynamics (PD)
QuestionWhat does the body do to the drug?What does the drug do to the body?
CoversADME — Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, ExcretionMechanism of action — receptors, targets, dose–response
Mostly determinesThe dose & timing (how much, how often)The effect & side effects

The lecture defines these simply — PK is "how the body handles drugs" and PD is "what the drugs do to the body and how". The deeper handle to carry forward: PK is the ADME journey (how the drug gets in, spreads, is broken down, and leaves), while PD is the action at the target. Plate 2.1 shows both as the basic properties of the drug–biological-system interaction.

Label Key
Drug → body (PD)
The drug binds targets and produces an effect — pharmacodynamics.
Body → drug (PK)
The body absorbs, distributes, metabolises and excretes the drug — pharmacokinetics.
Memory hook · which is which

PK = Kinetics = the body's Kinetics move the drug around (ADME). PD = Dynamics = the Drug's action on the body. Or simply: "Kinetics — what you do to the drug; Dynamics — what the drug does to you."

Reasoning · why the split matters

Two patients given the same dose can have very different blood levels (PK) — and the same blood level can produce different effects (PD). Separating the two lets you reason about a drug cleanly: change the dose/schedule to fix a PK problem, change the drug/target to fix a PD one. Almost every later topic (half-life, receptors, tolerance, interactions) hangs on this distinction.

§3Tutorial · grouping drugs

Drug classes

Thousands of drugs, organised into families by what they do.

A drug class groups drugs that share a mechanism, target, or therapeutic use. Knowing the class tells you a drug's rough job and typical effects. The tutorial's "if you were a drug…" classes, decoded:

Common drug classes — what they do

AnalgesicRelieves painParacetamol, morphine

Memory hook · read the name

The prefix usually gives it away: anti- = "against" something (antihypertensive → against high BP; antineoplastic → against tumours), -lytic/-olytic = breaks/relieves (anxiolytic → relieves anxiety), an-/anaes- = "without" (anaesthetic → without sensation).

Tutorial tasks (for reference)

The week-1 tutorial is mostly group work, not new content: a discussion of whether vitamins/Panadol/herbs are drugs (see §0), a reflection on why pharmacology matters to your career, and creating a PeerWise MCQ on the week's objectives (course ID 29201).

R Recall checklist — week 1
  • Drug — a substance that produces a biological effect when administered (synthetic, natural, or engineered).
  • Endogenous ≠ drug unless administered (insulin).
  • Drug vs medicine vs class — active chemical vs formulated therapeutic product vs a family grouped by action.
  • Pharmacology = study of drugs + their interactions; branches incl. PK, PD, toxicology, pharmacogenetics.
  • PK — body → drug — ADME — sets dose/timing.
  • PD — drug → body — mechanism/effect — sets effect & side effects.
  • Drug classes grouped by mechanism/target/use; read the prefix (anti-, -lytic, an-).
