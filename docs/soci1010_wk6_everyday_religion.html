§0The lens · lived religion · historical backdrop

The sociological lens on religion

Bracket the truth-claims; study what religion does.

A sociology of religion does not ask whether any faith is true, nor rank its dogmas. It asks a different family of questions: what is the relationship between religion and the wider society? How do people use religion to make their lives meaningful? How does religion interlock with other institutions — the economy, the state, the family? And what is religion's role in social change — does it hold society still, or push it forward? Answer those and you are doing sociology, whatever your personal belief.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

If a sociologist studies a prayer, a fasting month or a megachurch light-show, what are they actually looking at — the doctrine, or something else?

Something else: the social meaning and function. The 'lived religion' approach is interested in the material, embodied aspects of religion as they occur in everyday life — and it listens to how people explain themselves rather than testing them against a textbook definition. The doctrine is the believer's business; the sociologist's business is what the practice does for the person and the group.

§0.1Why the history still matters

The lecture opens with a historical backdrop because today's public arguments about religion are built on old foundations. Four moments in particular still resonate in contemporary public discourse: medieval, puritanical Christianity (religion saturating every institution), the Reformation (Protestant break from Rome — the seed of Weber's argument in §3), the Renaissance (the revival of human-centred, classical learning) and the Age of Enlightenment (reason, science and the ideal of a secular public sphere — the seed of the secularisation thesis in §4). When a commentator today calls religion 'backward' or praises secular 'rationality', they are recycling Enlightenment framing.

Reasoning · why bracket the truth-claim?

Sociology is a science of the social, so it can only study observable social facts: practices, meanings, groups, effects. Whether a god exists is not a social fact and not testable by these methods — so the discipline sets it aside ('brackets' it) and studies the part it can see. This is methodological, not a statement of disbelief: it lets a religious and an atheist sociologist reach the same findings about what a ritual does for a community.

Memory hook · three engines

Durkheim = glue, Marx = drug, Weber = engine.

Durkheim: religion binds society together. Marx: religion is the opium that sedates the exploited. Weber: religion was the engine that started capitalism. Three verdicts on the same thing — carry them through the whole week.

R Recall — the lens
  • Sociology of religion — asks about religion's link to society, meaning, institutions and social change, not whether a faith is true.
  • Lived religion — studies the material, embodied practice of religion in everyday life and how people explain themselves.
  • Historical backdrop — four moments still frame debate: medieval Christianity, the Reformation, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment.
  • Three theorists — Durkheim the glue, Marx the drug, Weber the engine.
§1Durkheim · sacred/profane · solidarity

Durkheim — religion as social glue

Worship the sacred, and society is really worshipping itself.

Émile Durkheim (1858–1917), in The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), gives the founding functionalist account: religion is not primarily about gods, it is about society. His definition is worth learning almost verbatim — a 'unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things … which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them', or more compactly, a 'complex system of myths, dogmas, rites and ceremonies'. Two features do the work there: it is unified (systematic, shared) and it unites people into a moral community.

§1.1The sacred and the profane

Durkheim's foundational move is to split the world in two. This binary, not the idea of God, is what he thinks all religions share.

Durkheim's foundational binary
 The sacredThe profane
What it isThings connected to God / dedicated to a religious purpose / belonging to the supernatural worldThe everyday, the mundane — ordinary parts of daily life
How treatedSet apart and forbidden; approached with awe and ritualHandled routinely, without ceremony
ExampleA communion wafer; Mecca; a Torah scrollBread; a street; a newspaper

The point is that nothing is sacred in itself — a wafer is just bread until a community sets it apart. That act of setting-apart is sacralisation, and it is a social act. Which is why Durkheim can make his famous inference: when a group gathers to revere its sacred symbol, the thing it is really revering is the group itself. Religion is society becoming conscious of itself.

Reasoning · how ritual makes solidarity

Assemble people, synchronise them in chant, movement and emotion, and you generate what Durkheim calls collective effervescence — a shared electricity that feels bigger than any individual. People misattribute that very real feeling to the sacred object, but its true source is the gathered collective. The ritual thus recharges the group's social solidarity and stamps its shared values back onto each member. Function precedes belief: the rite works on the group whether or not the theology is 'true'.

Real-world · secular 'sacred'

Because the mechanism is social, not supernatural, it runs in secular settings too. A minute's silence on Anzac Day, the flame at the Olympics, the hush in a courtroom, a football crowd singing as one — all 'set apart' objects and moments that generate effervescence and re-bind the collective. Durkheim predicts that a society losing its churches will grow new sacreds, not none.

R Recall — Durkheim
  • Definition — religion = a unified system of beliefs/practices about sacred things that unites adherents into a moral community ('Church').
  • Sacred vs profane — the sacred is set apart and forbidden; the profane is the everyday.
  • Collective effervescence — ritual generates a shared electricity that recharges group bonds.
  • Real object of worship — society itself; religion's function is social solidarity.
  • Take-home concepts — sacralisation and social solidarity.
§2Marx · opium · alienation · ideology

Marx — religion as false consciousness

The sigh of the oppressed — and the sedative that keeps them oppressed.

Where Durkheim sees glue, Karl Marx (1818–1883) sees a drug. Religion, in his account, is a tool used by the rich to mislead and oppress the poor. It is needed because people cannot find contentment or peace in a natural world in which they are so exploited — so religion offers a compensatory happiness elsewhere. That happiness is an illusion: religion is a form of false consciousness that numbs people to the real pain and suffering caused by capitalist oppression. This is the famous 'opium of the people'.

§2.1Alienation and ideology

Two Marxian concepts sharpen the point:

  • Alienation — the existential condition in which humans find themselves confronted by, and subordinate to, their own creations (the state, the market, religion). We build these things, then feel ruled by them. Religion alienates us specifically because agency is given over to God: our own power to act is projected onto a deity and returns to us as command. And there is a political sting — if social conditions are 'God's will', there is no point in trying to change them.
  • Ideology — a set of beliefs that justify existing power relations and naturalise the status quo, making an arrangement that is historical and changeable look eternal and inevitable ('the poor you will always have with you').
Reasoning · why an illusion can be sincere

'False consciousness' does not mean believers are lying or stupid. It means the comfort is real but its source is misread: the ache it soothes is produced by exploitation, yet religion relocates the cure to heaven instead of to changing the system. The sedative is effective — which is exactly why, for Marx, it is politically dangerous. It converts anger that could fuel structural change into patience.

§2.2The critique — does religion only pacify?

The lecture immediately tests Marx against the evidence, and he does not fully survive:

  • Religion as an agent of rebellion. Does the theory account for religion motivating and sustaining revolt? Martin Luther King Jr — president of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference — fused religion and politics to drive the US civil-rights movement. Islamic clerics came out to support revolution in the Arab Spring. Religion here is a lever against the powerful, not for them.
  • Does it assume all religions preach passivity? That flattening is the theory's weakness — many traditions carry radical, liberationist strands.
  • Oppression without religion. Some of history's worst violence came from explicitly atheist regimes: the Soviet Union's gulag (tens of millions of lives), Nazi Germany, and Pol Pot's Khmer Rouge in Kampuchea. If cruelty flourishes without religion, religion cannot be the essential engine of oppression Marx implies.
Real-world · liberation theology

The clearest live counter-case is liberation theology in 20th-century Latin America, where Catholic priests read the Gospel as a mandate to confront poverty and dictatorship. Same religion Marx called a sedative, deployed as a stimulant for structural change — the exception that his single-verdict model cannot easily absorb.

R Recall — Marx
  • Opium of the people — religion offers an illusory comfort that numbs the pain of exploitation.
  • False consciousness — a real comfort whose source is misread, diverting anger from structural change.
  • Alienation — humans subordinate to their own creations; agency handed to God, so 'God's will' blocks change.
  • Ideology — beliefs that naturalise the status quo and justify existing power.
  • Critique — ignores religion as rebellion (MLK, Arab Spring); oppression also occurs without religion (gulag, Nazism, Khmer Rouge).
§3Weber · predestination · asceticism · rationalization

Weber — the Protestant ethic

How anxiety about heaven accidentally built capitalism.

Max Weber flips Marx. For Marx, the economy (the 'mode of production') drives everything, religion included. Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904/05) argues the arrow can also run the other way: a religious idea helped bring capitalism into being. His puzzle is precise — why did capitalism develop specifically in Europe, and specifically where Protestantism took hold?

§3.1Predestination and the anxiety it bred

The mechanism starts with John Calvin's doctrine of predestination: God has already decided, before you were born, whether you are among the saved ('God's elect') or the damned. Nothing you do can change the verdict. That produced an unbearable salvation anxiety: am I destined for heaven or hell? With no way to earn salvation, believers desperately sought signs that they were already chosen.

And the sign they settled on was worldly success. Material success signalled God's favour; poverty signalled his disfavour. So working hard, being successful and saving money became evidence of election. Wealth was 'the realisation of God's glory and a sign of salvation'.

§3.2Asceticism → the spirit of capitalism

Two more ingredients complete the machine:

  • Asceticism — the Protestant was to avoid spending on luxuries and waste. But if you work hard, succeed, and refuse to spend, the money has only one place to go: reinvestment, which produces further success. Self-denial plus profit equals capital accumulation — the beating heart of capitalism. Weber calls this 'inner-worldly asceticism': the monk's self-discipline brought out of the monastery and turned into everyday life conduct.
  • Work as a calling — labour reframed from a curse into a higher purpose, a vocation performed for God, with a robust, disciplined work ethic and a focus on individual salvation (one's own well-being first).

The upshot: capitalism became a moral good, a moral force — the 'spirit of capitalism'. There is more to capitalism, Weber insists, than Marx's mode of production; there is an ethos, and that ethos had religious roots.

Reasoning · 'elective affinity', not cause

Weber is careful: Protestantism did not cause capitalism the way a match causes a fire. He speaks of an elective affinity — two things (a religious ethic and an economic practice) that fit each other so well they reinforce and select for one another. This matters because it lets ideas and culture be genuine historical forces, not mere reflections of the economy — the exact opposite of Marx's base-determines-superstructure model. (Hold this term: the reading in §6 uses the same 'elective affinity' between religion and capital.)

Memory hook · the two -tions

Rationalization builds the cage; disenchantment empties it of magic.

Rationalization = life increasingly ordered by calculation, efficiency and rules. Disenchantment = the flip side — as calculation spreads, the world loses its mystery and the sacred drains away. Weber's irony: the religious drive for salvation ended up building a rational, disenchanted capitalism that no longer needs the religion that started it.

Real-world · the prosperity gospel

Weber's 'wealth = a sign of God's favour' never left — it is reborn, stripped of the asceticism, in the modern prosperity gospel ('God wants you to be rich'), the very theology of the megachurches in §5 and §6. Weber's ascetic saved; the prosperity believer spends — but both read a bank balance as a spiritual report card.

R Recall — Weber
  • The puzzle — why did capitalism arise in Protestant Europe?
  • Predestination — salvation is pre-decided, breeding salvation anxiety.
  • Signs of election — worldly success (poverty = disfavour), so hard work + thrift became duties.
  • Inner-worldly asceticism — don't spend → reinvest → capital accumulation.
  • Spirit of capitalism — capitalism as a moral good; linked by elective affinity, not simple cause.
  • Take-home — rationalization (the cage) and disenchantment (the lost magic).
§4Secularisation thesis · Bruce · Bouma · Casanova

Secularisation & its critics

Is religion dying, or just changing shape?

The dominant secularisation thesis holds that modernisation brings a shift in the structure and influence of religion — a decline. C. Wright Mills gave it its grand narrative: 'Once the world was filled with the sacred … After the Reformation and Renaissance, the forces of modernization swept across the globe and secularization … loosened the dominance of the sacred. In due course the sacred shall disappear altogether except, possibly, in the private realm.' Note the debt to §3 — this is Weber's disenchantment, scaled up into a prophecy.

§4.1The evidence — and its hidden assumptions

Steve Bruce makes the case with data drawn mainly from European churches: a clear decline in the number of people who are members, who attend, who have their child baptised, who have a Christian wedding. On its face, decisive. But the lecture asks what this 'narrative of decline' takes for granted:

  • Christian-centric — it measures religion by a Christian template.
  • Culturally and geographically specific — European data generalised to the world.
  • A narrow model of religiosity — it assumes religion means institutional, Church-based practice (membership, weekly attendance). It would not translate to less institutionalised forms such as New Age spirituality.
  • Assumed neutrality — it takes for granted that our economic, political and legal systems are religiously neutral, when they carry religious inheritances.

§4.2The critics — change, not decline

Critics argue religion is not losing its significance in modern societies; the thesis just uses the wrong ruler.

  • Bouma (1992) — the thesis is a narrow, Western perspective.
  • Change of form, not decline — what looks like disappearance is religiosity changing its form (privatised, de-institutionalised, DIY).
  • José Casanova — distinguishes secular spheres from religious institutions and norms, and reframes secularisation as the differentiation of religion and its marginalisation to a privatised sphere — a relocation, not an extinction.
Reasoning · why the ruler decides the result

Measurement contains a definition. If you define 'being religious' as 'attends an institutional church weekly', then a world of people doing yoga, meditating, following New Age paths or believing privately without belonging will register as empty — you have defined their religiosity out of existence before you start counting. Casanova's move is to separate three things the thesis fuses: decline in belief, decline in institutional practice, and the differentiation of religion from state and economy. Only the middle one is clearly happening in the West.

§4.3The comparative picture

Cross-national data also break any simple 'modernity kills religion' story: highly modern countries range from intensely religious to barely religious. The 1990s World Values Survey figures the lecture cites:

R Recall — secularisation
  • Secularisation thesis — modernisation shrinks religion's structure and influence (Mills; Bruce's European data).
  • Hidden assumptions — it is Christian-centric, Western-specific, and uses an institutional-Church model.
  • Critics' reply — not decline but a change of form (Bouma); religion is differentiated and privatised, not erased (Casanova).
  • Comparative data — the USA is modern yet highly religious, defeating a simple 'modernity kills faith' story.
§5Census 2021 · new religions · Pentecostal mega-churches · Bouma

Australia's religious landscape

A 'shy' nation of quiet believers — and its loudest new churches.

Apply the §4 debate to Australia and you get a country that is secularising in form while religion keeps changing shape. The 2021 Census marked the tipping point everyone expected: for the first time Christianity fell below half the population, and 'no religion' became the single largest response (roughly two in five Australians) — up from almost nothing in the 1960s. But 'no religion' is not the same as 'no spirituality', and alongside the decline of the mainstream churches sits a boom in new and imported religious forms.

§5.1The proliferation of new religions

New religious movements the lecture lists
19th century20th century
Methodism (John Wesley); Salvation Army (William Booth)Pentecostalism (fastest-growing — see §5.2)
Mormons (Joseph Smith); Seventh Day AdventistsScientology; Raëlianism
Jehovah's Witnesses; Christian ScientistsNew Age; Neo-Paganism; Deep Ecology
Baha'iXiantianism (The Way of Former Heaven); Falun Gong
 Eastern imports — Buddhism, Hare Krishna; plus meditation, yoga, martial arts (and Western movements East, e.g. the Moonies in S. Korea)

The Eastern imports matter for the §4 argument: when a secular Australian meditates or does yoga, the secularisation thesis (institutional ruler) records a religious zero — yet a religious disciplinary practice has plainly migrated into everyday life. This is exactly the 'change of form' Bouma and Casanova mean.

§5.2The Pentecostal mega-church

Pentecostalism is the fastest-growing Christian religious movement in Australia, the USA and comparable countries. Its flagship form is the mega-church, which the lecture describes as 'not institutional, but organised' — it rejects the old denominational bureaucracy yet runs like a slick enterprise around business, entertainment and a success gospel whose goal is personal enrichment ($). Its common elements (Connell, 2005):

  • Emphasis on joyful participation, not stern discipline;
  • Lack of traditional church structure;
  • Music and show-biz elements;
  • A global, entrepreneurial, corporate approach to business development;
  • Charismatic preachers;
  • A flexible approach to gender roles;
  • A young, 'upwardly mobile', 'aspirational' audience — including many 'rejects' from other churches;
  • Regular turnover among the congregation (new recruits in, old ones out).
Real-world · Hillsong

Australia's global export of this form is Hillsong (founded in Sydney, 1983): stadium worship, chart-topping worship music, celebrity attendees and franchised campuses on several continents — before governance and conduct scandals from 2022 split it. It is the textbook Connell mega-church, and the live bridge into the reading (§6): Hillsong's arenas are precisely Sanders' 'non-places'.

§5.3Bouma — Australia's 'shy' religiosity

So what is the character of mainstream Australian religion? Greg Bouma (2006) captures it as low-key and understated — 'shy'. He profiles it across five dimensions, all tending to the muted:

Bouma (2006) — the dimensions of Australia's 'shy' religiosity
DimensionAustralian tendency
IntensityToward the laid-back rather than the fervent
ExpressivityToward the shy, withdrawn, not outgoing
FrequencyToward infrequent or occasional attendance
PeriodicityAnnual/biannual participation more acceptable than weekly
Social locationReligious groups expected to be on the margin, not central
Reasoning · why 'shy' is a norm, not just a fact

Bouma is not only describing behaviour; he is describing an expectation. In Australia religion is supposed to stay quiet, occasional and off to the side. That makes 'shy religiosity' a yardstick — and anyone who is loud, frequent and publicly central looks like they are breaking a rule. Hold that thought: it is precisely why visible Muslim religiosity gets read as excessive in §7. The 'neutral' secular public sphere turns out to have a preferred, Christian-shaped volume setting.

R Recall — Australia
  • Census 2021 — Christianity fell below half; 'no religion' became the largest single response.
  • New religions — 19th-C (Methodism, Salvation Army, Mormons) and 20th-C (Pentecostalism, Scientology, New Age, Eastern imports).
  • Mega-church — 'not institutional but organised': business, entertainment, a success gospel, charismatic preachers, turnover (Connell 2005).
  • Bouma's five dimensions — low intensity, low expressivity, low frequency, tolerant periodicity, marginal social location.
§6Required reading · Sanders (2016) · Augé · Žižek

Reading — the corporate megachurch as a 'non-place'

A church you could mistake for a mall — and that's the point.

The required reading — George Sanders, 'Religious Non-Places: Corporate Megachurches and Their Contributions to Consumer Capitalism' (Critical Sociology, 2016) — takes the Pentecostal mega-church from §5 and runs it through a single sharp idea. His thesis: the corporate megachurch is a 'non-place' that normalises the banality of consumer capitalism. Everything Durkheim, Marx and Weber said is in here, recombined for the age of the shopping mall.

§6.1Augé's 'non-place'

Sanders borrows the concept from the anthropologist Marc Augé. A non-place is a transitory site that lacks historical, cultural or geographic reference points — chain hotels, multi-lane highways, airport waiting areas, suburban shopping malls. They are 'spaces of circulation, consumption and communication'. Their signature feeling: they seem to be everywhere, yet leave you sensing you are 'nowhere in particular'. Augé sets them against the 'anthropological place', which carries 'inscriptions of the social bond' and a 'collective history'.

Augé — anthropological place vs non-place
 Anthropological placeNon-place
Relation to historyRooted in a collective historyA-historical, 'pasteurized' of associations
Relation to identityCarries the 'social bond'; you are someone hereYou are 'no more than what you do or experience'
What it is forDwelling, belonging, memoryCirculation, consumption, communication
ExamplesA village square, an old parish churchAirport lounge, chain hotel, mall — and the corporate megachurch

§6.2The corporate megachurch — the supply side

A megachurch is simply a church hosting at least 2,000 people over a weekend; while mid-size churches shrink, these keep growing, and more US Protestants now belong to them than to any other form. Sanders narrows to the corporate megachurch — one that deliberately adopts the model of the modern corporation. Physically it sheds every marker of a church: no pews, pulpits, stained glass, statues, steeples or crosses; from outside it looks like a medical-office park; inside it resembles a mall (shops, cafés) or a sports arena.

Its history is a marketing history:

  • Bill Hybels & Willow Creek (1975). Hybels used business-school training to poll people on what they disliked about church. The top answer: it was boring. He built Willow Creek (suburban Chicago) to fix that — church as entertainment. It grew into six franchises; the flagship draws 20,000+ a weekend.
  • Growth as mantra. The Willow Creek Association claims 11,000+ 'sister churches' and sells webinars, DVDs, handbooks — even liturgies and sermons for purchase. The Economist profiled it as 'Jesus, CEO': a mission statement, a management team, a seven-step strategy, two MBAs on staff.
  • Rick Warren, the 'pastorpreneur' (The Purpose Driven Life), an admirer of the free-market economists Hayek and von Mises and a friend of management guru Peter Drucker — who called the megachurch 'the most significant sociological phenomenon' of the late 20th century.
Reasoning · this is Weber's elective affinity, upgraded

Recall §3: Weber found an elective affinity between Protestant asceticism and early capitalism — thrift built factories. Sanders finds a new elective affinity, this time between Protestantism and consumer capitalism — the church now built on the template of the corporation, not the factory (Deleuze: the corporation has 'sublimated' the factory). Weber's church made you save and produce; the corporate megachurch makes you consume. Same affinity, opposite economic behaviour — because capitalism itself flipped from a production ethic to a consumption ethic.

Memory hook · McChurch

Franchised · scripted · sermons-for-sale = a McDonaldized faith.

Sanders explicitly notes the 'McDonaldized homogeneity' of buyable sermons across sister franchises. If you did Ritzer's McDonaldization (Week 7's reading), the corporate megachurch is the pew-shaped case study: efficiency, calculability, predictability and control applied to salvation.

§6.3Seekers — the demand side

Supply needs demand, and the demand is the 'seeker': someone searching for meaning, contentment and wholeness who believes a church might provide it. Seekers are prized because they are numerous and can be reified as a consumer type — highly educated, reflexive, individualistic, consumption-oriented, cobbling together a 'patchwork spirituality' and 'more interested in the journey than the destination' (Roof). The church markets to them like any firm chasing customers:

  • 'Christianity Lite.' Fire-and-brimstone is out; a 'prosperity gospel' of felt needs, optimism and good fortune is in. Joel Osteen: 'I'm called to plant a seed of hope … not to expound on deep theological doctrines.'
  • Design for comfort. Warren's Purpose Driven Church advises making it 'easy to attend', letting visitors 'remain anonymous', and using lighting/sound to 'create an attractive atmosphere' — all to reach 'unchurched Harry and Mary', the target demographic.
  • Spectacle over Scripture. A Willow Creek study found the word 'I' used 6,000+ times while specific Scripture was cited only 169 times — the message re-centred on the individual-as-consumer.
Real-world · Durkheim in the light-show

Sanders' field notes describe shuttle buses, countdown clocks, five cameras, a build-up soundtrack, a crowd on its feet singing to projected lyrics — 'as much a concert as a church', where you are 'obligated to perform the role of fan'. That is Durkheim's collective effervescence (§1) — but now manufactured as a commodity spectacle. Sanders' verdict (via Debord): the spectacle has 'displaced the divine', and the very rush that once bound a moral community now sells one.

§6.4The payoff — 'false disidentification'

Why is a church-that-isn't-a-church so attractive? Because a non-place is a 'blank canvas': since it does not look, feel or operate like a traditional church, worshippers can project onto it whatever they like. You can attend without feeling you are doing 'institutional religion' (great for the growing 'spiritual but not religious' crowd), get the benefits — meaning, edification, moral guidance — and tell yourself your spirituality is pure, personal and set apart from the profane marketplace.

Here Sanders lands his key concept, from Slavoj Žižek: false disidentification. You cognitively distance yourself from a system ('this isn't consumerism, it's my private faith') while behaviourally participating in it. And the distancing is exactly what makes the ideology work: 'when we dis-identify with our prescribed social roles we often still perform them — sometimes better … than if we did identify with them' (Fleming & Spicer). Believing you have stepped outside consumer capitalism is what lets you serve it undisturbed.

Reasoning · this is Marx's ideology, updated

Recall §2: ideology naturalises the status quo and false consciousness misreads the source of one's feelings. False disidentification is the 21st-century upgrade. Old ideology said 'accept your place, it's God's will'. The new version doesn't even need you to believe — it lets you feel critical, ironic, above it all, and keep consuming anyway. The felt distance from capitalism is the mechanism by which capitalism reproduces itself. Marx's sedative now comes with the flavour of rebellion.

R Recall — Sanders' megachurch
  • Non-place (Augé) — a transitory, a-historical, a-contextual space (mall, airport) where you feel 'nowhere in particular'.
  • Corporate megachurch — 2,000+ attendees; sheds crosses/pews to model the corporation (CEO-pastors, marketing, franchising).
  • Supply side — Hybels' Willow Creek ('church was boring'); growth mantra; sermons for sale (McDonaldized); Warren the pastorpreneur.
  • Demand side — seekers sold a prosperity gospel/'Christianity Lite' for 'unchurched Harry and Mary'.
  • False disidentification (Žižek) — feel outside consumerism while performing it; the felt distance is what makes the ideology work.
  • Synthesis — Weber's elective affinity + Marx's ideology + Durkheim's effervescence, now as commodity spectacle.
§7Case study · Islamophobia · secular norms · lived religion

Case study — Muslim religiosity in a 'secular' society

'Secular' is not the same as neutral.

The lecture closes on a case study that turns the week's tools on Australia itself: Muslim religiosity in a 'secular' society, and the Islamophobia that surrounds it. The starting observation is a pair of stereotyped opposites in public discourse — a set of familiar tropes about Islam (backward, primitive, regressive, oppressive, draconian) set against a flattering self-image of secularism (enlightened, rational, free, autonomous).

§7.1The trap in the word 'secular'

That opposition assumes secularism is neutral ground — the absence of religion, from which religion can be judged. The week has already dismantled that assumption twice. In §4, the secularisation thesis smuggled in a Christian, institutional definition of religion. In §5, Bouma showed Australia has a norm that religion should be 'shy' — low-intensity, infrequent, marginal. Put those together and 'secular' Australia turns out to have a very specific, inherited, Christian-shaped expectation about how much religion may show in public.

Against that quiet standard, a visibly devout minority — five daily prayers, a headscarf, a public fast — does not read as 'normal religion'. It reads as too much: loud where the norm is shy, frequent where the norm is occasional, central where the norm is marginal. Islamophobia is then better understood not as mere individual prejudice but as a social phenomenon: the policing of who is allowed to be publicly religious, and how much.

Reasoning · why 'lived religion' is the antidote

Every ruler in this week measured minorities against someone else's template — the Christian church, the secular public sphere, the 'shy' Australian norm. The 'lived religion' lens from §0 refuses the template: it studies how Muslim Australians actually practise and how they explain themselves, in the material texture of everyday life, rather than scoring them against an institutional or secular yardstick. Change the ruler and the 'problem' of Muslim religiosity dissolves into ordinary human meaning-making — the sociologist's proper object.

Real-world · the double standard, made visible

The tell that secularism is not neutral is the asymmetry: a Christian cross worn to work, a nativity scene in a town square or 'Merry Christmas' from a politician passes as cultural background, while a hijab or a prayer room is debated as a political statement. Same category — public religious expression — judged by two different volumes. Naming that asymmetry is the sociological payoff of the case study.

R Recall — Islam & the secular
  • The tropes — Islam cast as backward/oppressive; secularism cast as rational/free.
  • Secular ≠ neutral — 'secular' Australia carries a Christian-shaped, 'shy' norm for how much religion may show.
  • Islamophobia — a social phenomenon policing public religiosity, not just individual prejudice.
  • Lived religion — the antidote: study actual everyday practice and self-understanding, not conformity to a template.
