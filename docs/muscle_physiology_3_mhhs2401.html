Reflexes — the spinal cord's fast automatic circuits

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary response wired through the spinal cord — fast because it doesn't wait for the brain. This guide follows the reflex arc from receptor to effector, contrasts the two muscle proprioceptors (spindle vs Golgi tendon organ), builds up from the simplest monosynaptic reflex to the polysynaptic circuits behind reciprocal inhibition and the crossed-extensor reflex, and ends with how central pattern generators drive rhythmic movement.

§0Definition · LO1

What is a reflex?

A rapid, involuntary, spinally-mediated response.

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus, controlled by sensory input to — and motor output from — the spinal cord. Its function is to maintain homeostasis by making rapid adjustments in the position or function of an organ, without waiting for conscious thought.

Reasoning · why route it through the cord, not the brain?

Speed and protection. Routing the loop through the spinal cord (rather than up to the brain and back) removes the longest, slowest leg of the journey, so a withdrawal from a hot stove happens before the pain is even consciously felt. The trade-off — losing conscious control — is worth it for a protective or postural response that must be immediate. The brain is informed in parallel (ascending pathways), so you feel the pain a moment later.

Clinical · can we override a reflex?

Yes — reflexes can be modulated by voluntary (conscious) control from the brain (e.g. holding onto a hot plate long enough to set it down safely). Except when there is a spinal cord lesion above the level of the reflex arc: the brain can no longer reach the circuit, so voluntary modulation is lost — but the reflex arc itself still exists and still fires (indeed reflexes may become exaggerated). This is why testing reflexes helps localise a cord lesion.

R Recall checklist — what is a reflex
  • Definition — rapid, involuntary response; sensory input to and motor output from the spinal cord.
  • Function — maintain homeostasis via rapid positional/functional adjustments.
  • Override — yes, by voluntary brain control — unless a cord lesion sits above the arc (reflex persists, control lost).
§1The circuit · LO1

The reflex arc

Five components: receptor → sensory → integration → motor → effector.

Every reflex runs through the same five-part reflex arc. Plate 1.1 traces it from a stimulus in the skin to the muscle that responds.

Label Key — the five links, in order
1 · Receptor
Detects the stimulus (here, in the skin).
2 · Sensory (afferent) neuron
Carries the signal in to the spinal cord.
3 · Integration center
The synapse(s) in the CNS grey matter; may include an interneuron.
4 · Motor (efferent) neuron
Carries the command out from the cord.
5 · Effector
The muscle (or gland) that produces the response.
Memory hook · in order, always

R–S–I–M–E: Receptor → Sensory → Integration → Motor → Effector. Afferent goes in (A→I), efferent exits (E→E).

Reasoning · the integration center is where reflexes differ

The two ends (receptor, effector) are the same across reflexes; the character of a reflex is set at step 3, the integration center. A single synapse there gives the fastest, simplest reflex; adding interneurons lets the signal diverge to antagonists, to the other limb, and up to the brain — which is exactly how the more complex reflexes in §3–§5 are built.

R Recall checklist — the reflex arc
  • Five components — receptor, sensory neuron, integration center, motor neuron, effector.
  • Afferent = in (sensory); efferent = out (motor).
  • Integration center — the CNS synapse(s), possibly via an interneuron; sets the reflex type.
§2The sensors

Muscle proprioceptors: spindle vs Golgi tendon organ

Two sensors — one for length, one for tension.

Skeletal muscle receptors are proprioceptors — they report the position and movement of the body. There are two types, and the whole logic of muscle reflexes hangs on telling them apart. Plate 2.1 shows both in place.

Muscle spindle vs Golgi tendon organ
Muscle spindleGolgi tendon organ
LocationMuscle bellyTendon (muscle–tendon junction)
StimulusStretch (length)Tension (force)
Afferent fibre1a fibre1b fibre
Active duringIsotonic contraction & resting toneIsometric contraction
Muscle responseContractionRelaxation
Reasoning · opposite jobs, opposite responses

The two sensors guard different variables, so they trigger opposite responses. The spindle guards length: stretch it and it fires the stretch reflex — the muscle contracts to resist being lengthened (this is the knee-jerk). The Golgi tendon organ guards tension: excessive force fires it and the muscle relaxes to protect itself and the tendon from tearing. Spindle → contract (defends length); GTO → relax (defends against over-tension.)

Memory hook · which is which

Spindle = in the belly, senses stretch/length, 1a fibre, causes contraction. Golgi = at the tendon, senses tension (Grip force), 1b fibre, causes relaxation.

R Recall checklist — proprioceptors
  • Muscle spindle — muscle belly · senses stretch/length · 1a fibre · isotonic & resting tone · → contraction.
  • Golgi tendon organ — tendon junction · senses tension/force · 1b fibre · isometric · → relaxation.
  • Both are proprioceptors reporting body position/movement.
§3Circuit complexity

Monosynaptic vs polysynaptic reflexes

One synapse, or many — how many CNS relays sit in the loop.

Reflexes are classified by how many synapses sit between the incoming sensory neuron and the outgoing motor neuron. Plate 3.1 contrasts the two.

Monosynaptic vs polysynaptic
MonosynapticPolysynaptic
Synapses in CNSOne (sensory → motor directly)Two or more
Interneuron?NoYes (≥1)
SpeedFastestSlower (more relays)
ExampleStretch reflex (knee-jerk)Withdrawal, reciprocal & crossed-extensor reflexes
Reasoning · why add synapses at all?

Each extra synapse costs a little time but buys computation. A monosynaptic reflex can only do one thing (contract the stretched muscle). Inserting an interneuron lets the same sensory signal be routed, inverted (excitatory → inhibitory) and copied — so one input can contract one muscle and inhibit its antagonist (§4), and act on the opposite limb (§5). Polysynaptic circuits are how the cord turns a single sensor into a coordinated whole-body adjustment.

R Recall checklist — mono vs polysynaptic
  • Monosynaptic — one CNS synapse, no interneuron, fastest; the stretch (knee-jerk) reflex.
  • Polysynaptic — ≥2 synapses via interneuron(s); withdrawal, reciprocal, crossed-extensor.
  • Interneurons add computation (routing, inhibition, divergence).
§4Same side

Ipsilateral reflexes & reciprocal inhibition

Contract the agonist, relax the antagonist — on the same side.

Ipsilateral = same side. An ipsilateral polysynaptic reflex produces relaxation of the antagonist muscle group at the same time as the agonist contracts — a phenomenon called reciprocal inhibition.

Reasoning · why inhibit the antagonist?

A joint is moved by opposing muscle groups (agonist vs antagonist). If both contracted at once, they would fight each other and the joint would lock. So when the reflex contracts the agonist, an inhibitory interneuron simultaneously switches off the antagonist's motor neuron. The result is unopposed, efficient movement of the joint — the whole point of reciprocal inhibition. (This is why the polysynaptic wiring of §3 is needed: only an interneuron can turn the excitatory input into an inhibitory one for the antagonist.)

Memory hook · reciprocal inhibition

Push the accelerator, ease off the brake. Agonist ON → antagonist OFF = reciprocal inhibition = unopposed movement.

R Recall checklist — ipsilateral reflexes
  • Ipsilateral = same side.
  • Reciprocal inhibition — agonist contracts while the antagonist is inhibited (relaxed).
  • Function — unopposed, efficient movement of the joint; needs an inhibitory interneuron.
§5Opposite side

Contralateral reflexes & the crossed-extensor reflex

Withdraw one limb, brace the other — for balance.

Contralateral = opposite side. A contralateral polysynaptic reflex produces reciprocal inhibition in both the left and right limbs, but in opposite directions — the crossed-extensor reflex. Plate 5.1 shows the classic example: stepping on something sharp.

Label Key — the divergent circuit
1 · Painful stimulus
Activates a nociceptor in the stimulated (ipsilateral) foot.
2 · Sensory neuron diverges
Enters the cord and branches to multiple targets.
3a · Ascends to brain
For sensation (pain) and postural adjustment.
3b · Ipsilateral withdrawal
Flexors contract, extensors inhibited — foot pulls away.
3c · Contralateral extension
Extensors contract, flexors inhibited — the other leg braces as weight shifts.
Reasoning · why brace the other leg?

Withdrawing a foot mid-stance would topple you — so the same sensory signal that pulls one leg up must push the other leg down. Crossing interneurons carry the signal to the opposite side and flip the pattern: extensors contract, flexors inhibit there, so the contralateral limb straightens and takes the shifted weight. Its function is postural stability — protection of one limb without a fall.

Clinical · a window on cord integrity

Because the crossed-extensor reflex depends on intact sensory input, crossing interneurons and bilateral motor output, its presence or absence — and the withdrawal reflex it accompanies — is part of how spinal cord function is assessed after injury.

R Recall checklist — contralateral reflexes
  • Contralateral = opposite side.
  • Crossed-extensor reflex — reciprocal inhibition in both limbs, opposite directions.
  • Stimulated side withdraws (flexors contract); opposite side extends (bears weight).
  • Function — postural stability.
§6Reflex + voluntary

Rhythmic movement & central pattern generators

Locomotion and breathing: reflex on the inside, will on the outside.

Rhythmic movements — locomotion, breathing — are a combination of reflex/involuntary and voluntary movement. Neither pure reflex nor pure will could produce them alone.

Reasoning · dividing the labour

Consciously commanding every step or breath would be exhausting and error-prone, so the nervous system splits the job. The involuntary component (maintenance): once the activity is initiated, central pattern generators (CPGs) — networks in the CNS — activate the reflexes spontaneously, cycling the pattern without further thought. The voluntary component (modulation): conscious control from the brain is needed to initiate or terminate the activity, or change its rate or intensity. So you decide to walk and how fast; the CPG handles the repetitive stepping in between.

Memory hook · CPG

CPG runs the loop; the brain sets the dial. Involuntary = maintain the rhythm; voluntary = start/stop and change rate/intensity.

R Recall checklist — rhythmic movement
  • Rhythmic movement (locomotion, breathing) = reflex + voluntary combined.
  • Involuntary/maintenance — central pattern generators (CPGs) fire the reflexes spontaneously once started.
  • Voluntary/modulation — the brain initiates/terminates and changes rate or intensity.

Week 5 (Muscle Phys III) in one line. A reflex is a fast, involuntary spinal circuit — receptor → sensory → integration → motor → effector — sensed by the muscle spindle (length → contract) or Golgi tendon organ (tension → relax); adding interneurons turns the simplest monosynaptic stretch reflex into polysynaptic circuits that give reciprocal inhibition (unopposed movement), the crossed-extensor reflex (postural stability), and, with central pattern generators, rhythmic movement under voluntary modulation.
