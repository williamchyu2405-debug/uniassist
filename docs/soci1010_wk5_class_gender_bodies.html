§0Framing · the sociology of the banal

The everyday hook — a history of toilets

Read the most mundane object in the house, and the whole social organisation of the body falls out.

Raj's trademark opening: pick a "bizarre banal thing" and show its sociological significance. This week it is the toilet — called "the icon of the twentieth century". It looks like plumbing; it is actually a compact lesson in nature vs culture, class, gender, privacy and power, which sets up everything that follows.

A modern, Western invention

Private, sex-gendered lavatories were not always with us. They are a modern and Western invention (Gershenson & Penner 2009), bound up with four things at once: urbanization (people packed into cities), the rise of sanitary reform, the privatization of bodily functions (defecation moved indoors and out of sight), and the gendered ideology of separate spheres (male/female worlds — hence male/female rooms). Before that, toilets were "out in the bush." Even wealthy homes used chamber pots emptied into buckets left outside for the "night-soil man" — the practice in early-colony Sydney too, which is why some inner-city suburbs still have laneways. Until the 19th century a genuine hazard of the European city was excrement thrown from windows onto the street; the Romans, by contrast, had efficient sewers and public bathing. So the "reciprocal influence" of nature and culture on excretory practice has swung back and forth across history.

Reasoning · why start here?

The toilet is the perfect teaching object because bodily elimination is a human leveller — everyone, regardless of sex, gender, ethnicity or status, must do it. Raj calls this "bodily democracy." That shared animality is exactly why the differences layered on top are so revealing: if the biological act is universal, then every variation — a luxury Japanese toilet vs a pit latrine, a men's room vs a unisex one — must be social and cultural, not natural. The banal thing is a controlled experiment in how society organises the body.

Shove's practice theory — materials, competences, meanings

The sociologist behind the toilet analysis is Elizabeth Shove (Comfort, Cleanliness and Convenience), who studies the sociology of domestic life through practice theory: social life is organised around practices — repeated activities that are collective routines, not just individual acts. Every practice is held together by three elements, and "using the toilet" decomposes cleanly into all three.

Shove's three elements of a practice — applied to the toilet
ElementWhat it isToilet example
MaterialsThe tools, technologies and infrastructure a practice needs.The flush toilet, the bidet, the Japanese luxury model, the pit latrine, water-conservation design, cleaning sprays.
CompetencesThe skills and know-how the practice demands — often bodily.Knowing how to squat vs sit, wipe, wash; "toilet literacy" and hygiene routines. Squatting is a learned bodily competence (Marcel Mauss's "techniques of the body").
MeaningsThe taboos, values and stigmas attached — shifting across time and place.Privacy vs public toilets as places of social interaction; gender norms; disgust; hygiene as moral virtue.

Two of these deserve a second look. On competence: the fact that some cultures can squat "without any problem" is, in Raj's words, "a kind of inscription on the body" — socialisation writing itself into muscle and joint. Hold that thought: it is a preview of Bourdieu's habitus (§2.2). On meanings: the spatial ordering of toilets — male / female / disabled / parents' room — encodes social categories in tile and signage, and gendered toilets can "create barriers for those who do not fit traditional gender categories." Macquarie's Law building answers this with all-unisex cubicles, dissolving the strict binary — a small architectural argument about gender we will meet again in §3.

Memory hook · the toilet trinity

Stuff · Skills · Stories.

Shove's three elements = the stuff (materials), the skills (competences) and the stories (meanings). Any everyday practice — showering, cooking, driving — can be split the same three ways.

Applied / real-world · dirt, COVID & the assignment

"Dirt is matter out of place" — cleanliness is a social judgement, not a chemical fact, and hygiene has become commodified and regimented: surface sprays, floor sprays, hand sanitiser everywhere, peaking in the COVID pandemic when eliminating the virus became priority one. That is the toilet as a live case for the take-home exam's "read the image" task: shown a bathroom scene, you can name the social mechanisms — separate spheres, spatial ordering of gender/ability, dirt-as-matter-out-of-place, the privatization of the body — instead of just describing plumbing. The two clips do the same work: Curb Your Enthusiasm's disabled-stall scene flags the stigma and shifting language around able/disabled bodies ("handicapped" → "disabled"), and South Park's Japanese-toilet scene stages the toilet as a class-marked luxury commodity.

R Recall checklist — the toilet hook
  • The move — start from a banal object; the toilet is "the icon of the 20th century."
  • Modern & Western — private sex-gendered lavatories = urbanization + sanitary reform + privatization of the body + separate spheres.
  • History swings — night-soil man & chamber pots (and colonial Sydney laneways) vs Roman sewers; nature/culture are reciprocal.
  • Shove's practice theory — materials, competences, meanings; squatting = bodily competence "inscribed on the body" (Mauss).
  • Bodily democracy — everyone must go (human leveller), yet the technology & meanings are class- and culture-marked.
  • Dirt = matter out of place; hygiene commodified/regimented (COVID); gendered toilets exclude — MQ Law's unisex answer.
§1Part 1 · Class

Class — Marx, Weber, Bourdieu

Class is not one thing; three theorists give three converging lenses on how it works.

Class is "a very loose term." Raj's working definition packs in five things at once: class is simultaneously a structural position, a distribution of material resources, a relationship of power, a set of cultural dispositions, and a lived experience reproduced through everyday practices and institutions. The theoretical challenge is to explain how large-scale structures become incorporated into ordinary action — because class shows up not only in the factory or office but at the dining table, in your accent and "polish," your friendship circle, your sport (basketball/soccer vs skiing/rowing/golf), your housing, your aspirations, and finally on your body (dress, cut of clothes, posture, "an air of confidence"). Three theorists answer the challenge from three angles. This is also the terrain of the week's reading, Archer's "Class and Class Relations," which uses Marx, Weber and Bourdieu to puncture the Australian myth that we are a "fair-go" society in which class is a "dirty word."

Three theories of class at a glance

WeberMarket-determined life chances, plus honour and authority.Class (property + skills) · Status (prestige) · Party (power).Two extra axes Marx omits — status & power.

§1.1Karl Marx — profits vs wages

Marx (writing in the late 1800s) founded conflict theory: society consists of two great classes based on "the difference between those who make profits and those who earn wages."

  • Bourgeoisie — own the means of production (factories, shops, corporations). Their capital is "money that produces more money": it is invested to grow, not used to satisfy human need.
  • Proletariat — workers who sell their labour power for wages and own none of the means of production; the retail assistant, the barista.

The crucial move is that class is relational: it is defined by your relationship to production, property, labour and capital — not simply by how much money you have. Two mechanisms follow.

Reasoning · exploitation & surplus value (the engine)

Workers produce commodities and value; owners appropriate the surplus value — the gap between what the work is worth and what the worker is paid. Raj's figure: an iPhone may cost ~$200 to make (labour included) yet sell for ~$1700; that gap is profit flowing to the corporation. Crucially, exploitation is structural, not personal — the boss need not mistreat anyone; it is "built into the capitalist relationship" itself, because capital only increases by extracting value from labour. This is why, when Australian manufacturing wages rose in the 1980s, production moved offshore to cheaper labour: the same logic, not villainy.

Reasoning · alienation (the felt cost)

Once a woodworker made a whole chair and had a direct relationship to it, even bartering it with a neighbour. Under the factory/market system the worker's labour disappears into a product sold to strangers, so the work "doesn't make any sense" to them — they feel alienated from what they produce. Raj's 2020s parallel: writing your own essay feels like yours; handing the task to GPT and claiming the output does not — "you know how alienated you feel." Class, then, is "explained not merely through income but through the organisation of everyday existence."

Applied / real-world · the retail worker & the cafe

Picture a clothing-retail assistant: the company owns the store, stock, brand and supply chain; the worker owns none of it and sells 8 hours of labour for a fixed wage while the firm keeps the revenue. A barista makes 100 coffees a shift and is paid by the hour; the owner takes the sales. "There's never a short supply of labour," so the owner "doesn't lose much" — which is precisely why the relationship persists and generates class conflict. Marx lets you look at an ordinary shift and ask: who controls your time? why is some work highly paid and other work poorly paid? These are exam-ready questions to put to an everyday scene.

§1.2Max Weber — life chances, status & power

Weber (whom we met on the Protestant ethic and rationalisation) agrees the economy matters but argues class is multidimensional. He splits stratification into three:

Weber's three dimensions of stratification
DimensionBasisEveryday marker
ClassMarket-determined life chances — your property (income, assets, shares) and your skills/education/credentials (which buy commercial opportunities like a well-paid job).Owning vs renting; a degree that opens a professional career.
StatusSocial honour/prestige granted for membership of a group; based on consumption & lifestyle and the "us vs them" distinction. Taken to the extreme, status groups become castes.The prestige of "being a doctor" — a title that elevates you even without wealth.
Party / PowerAuthority to dictate others' behaviour — through law, rules, or traditional/charismatic authority.Holding an office that commands obedience.
Reasoning · why status and power must be separate from class

If money were the whole story, prestige and authority would always travel with it — but they come apart, which is why Weber needs three axes. His own examples: King Charles has enormous status but little power; the Pope has both high status and real power as head of the Church. A newly-qualified doctor may have modest income yet high status. Weber's point is that class "manifests in other ways" than ownership — so stratification is a three-dimensional space, not a single ladder. That is more complicated than Marx, but it fits a world where a violinist or a lecturer is revered without being rich.

Memory hook · Weber's 3 Ps

Property · Prestige · Power.

Class = Property (+ skills), Status = Prestige, Party = Power. Marx has only the first; Weber adds the other two.

§1.3Pierre Bourdieu — habitus, field & four capitals

The French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu (1930–2002) maps class across a far wider spectrum, and locates it inside people. Two structural concepts frame it:

  • Habitus — the "mental, or cognitive structures" through which people perceive and make sense of the world: "internalized, 'embodied' social structures." It is the durable product of long occupation of a social position, so people in the same position share a similar habitus. It shapes what feels "normal, possible, embarrassing, sophisticated, inappropriate, or desirable" — the sense of "people like us."
  • Field — a social space or arena organised around specific types of capital. Your position in any field depends on the amount and relative weight of the capital you hold; the field is where your class dispositions either "shine" or work against you.

Bourdieu then breaks capital into four convertible forms — and convertibility is key: economic capital can be turned into the others, and vice versa.

Bourdieu's four forms of capital
CapitalDefinitionOne-line shorthandExample
EconomicMaterial resources — money, property, savings, shares.What you haveA wealthy person (closest to the everyday sense of "class"; ≈ Weber's property).
SocialNetworks of influence/support from group membership.Who you knowA huge LinkedIn network; an influencer with millions of followers.
CulturalKnowledge, educational credentials, skills and taste.What you knowDiscerning good wine from a $7 bottle; reading literature; playing an instrument.
SymbolicSocially recognised legitimacy — prestige, honour.How you're reveredA top athlete or influencer (≈ Weber's status).
Reasoning · why the capitals convert (and why that entrenches class)

The capitals are not four separate scoreboards; they are exchangeable currencies. Money buys the ski holidays, private schooling and violin lessons that become cultural capital and prestige; a prestigious network (social capital) opens the well-paid job (economic capital). Because the rich can convert one advantage into all the others, class "reproduces itself" across domains — which is exactly why it is durable and hard to escape. This is also how a musician or lecturer, "not super rich," can still hold high cultural or symbolic capital: the currencies are genuinely different, so you can be rich in one and poor in another.

Memory hook · the three questions (+ one)

Have · Know-people · Know-things — then Revered.

Economic = "what do you have?", Social = "who do you know?", Cultural = "what do you know?" — and Symbolic = "how much are you revered?" These four together compose the habitus.

Applied / real-world · the two students & the white tablecloth

Habitus is felt as (dis)comfort. Two students of equal ability: Student A thinks "obviously I should apply to the most prestigious university"; Student B thinks "people like me don't really belong there." Same test scores, different disposition — and that unease steers real choices (which suburb, which restaurant, which career). Anyone who has felt out of place at a white-tablecloth dinner with a full set of cutlery, or in a crowd talking operas and novels, has felt their habitus meeting a field it was not built for. For the exam: this is how to explain, sociologically, why "people know their place" without any law forcing them to — Archer's whole point against the "fair-go" myth.

R Recall checklist — class
  • Class = many things at once — structure + resources + power + dispositions + lived experience; the puzzle is how big structures enter ordinary action.
  • Marx — profits vs wages; bourgeoisie (own production) vs proletariat (sell labour); class is relational.
  • Surplus value & exploitation — owners appropriate the value workers create; structural, not personal (iPhone $200→$1700).
  • Alienation — losing a meaningful relation to what you produce (the GPT-essay feeling).
  • Weber's 3 Ps — Class (property + skills = life chances), Status (prestige), Party (power). Charles = status/no power; Pope = both.
  • Bourdieu — habitus (embodied cognitive structures) + field (arena of capital).
  • Four capitals — economic (have), social (know people), cultural (know/taste), symbolic (prestige); all convertible.
  • Reading — Archer uses these three to bust the Australian "fair-go" myth that class is a "dirty word."
§2Part 2 · Embodiment

Embodiment — how the social gets into the body

Four theorists on the body as disciplined, class-marked, performed and gendered.

Our bodies carry us through life, yet "no matter how much we attempt to manage" them they excrete, tire, age, sicken and transform. Far from a passive vessel, the body is actively constructed through daily routines, interactions and institutional practices — which makes it a critical site for analysing how social life is lived. Sociologists study bodies that are pregnant, disabled, fat, tattooed, scarred, sick, diseased, surgically modified, used for sport or for profit. Four theorists give four complementary readings of embodiment.

§2.1Michel Foucault — the medical gaze & biopower

Foucault's abstract themes — power, discourse, knowledge — have direct purchase on everyday life, because "seemingly mundane routines, norms and institutions shape who we are and how we behave." Power, for him, "is everywhere and not just repressive."

Discipline & docile bodies

Institutions — school, prison, hospital, asylum — work as disciplinary mechanisms that produce docile bodies: bodies that are not resistant but compliant. Think of school: sit quietly, sit properly, speak only when asked (the teacher who trains the class to fall silent at a single clap). In Discipline and Punish Foucault traces the modern prison, and the Panopticon — a 19th-century watchtower design in which prisoners, never sure whether they are being watched, police themselves. The endpoint is that power is no longer exercised from outside — "you do it yourself": we check our own behaviour, glance around for cameras, feel never alone.

Reasoning · The Birth of the Clinic & the "medical gaze"

Around the 1700s–1800s, hospitals began to dissect the cadaver and map the body's systems — those famous paintings of a crowd watching a corpse opened while a doctor explains the cause of death. This gave medicine the power "to look into your body" and supply a rational explanation. But it comes at a cost. Under the medical gaze, "the patient is no longer viewed as a whole person" with feelings — the person becomes "a site of observable phenomena," "the body to be studied and diagnosed," "a text to be read." The doctor's authority rests not only on knowledge but on the power to see and interpret the body in ways the patient cannot (the impersonal Hippocratic distance is part of this). The clinic thus "became the laboratory of the human body," correlating symptoms with anatomical pathology (autopsy, blood tests, X-ray).

Applied / real-world · biopower & medicalization in healthcare

Foucault's punchline: knowledge and power are inseparable. Because medical discourse is so authoritative, most of us "receive the doctor's instruction as gospel." That makes it a tool of social control — biopower (bio = life/body, welded to power): "don't sit in the sun more than 20 minutes," "wipe your hands," masks and social-distancing in COVID, childhood vaccination mandates. Doctors become "gatekeepers of what counts as health, sanity, fitness, morality," defining the normal vs the pathological — this is medicalization. It is exactly why some experienced COVID rules as state "overreach." For a healthcare-facing student this is the sharpest applied point in the lecture: the clinical encounter is never only clinical — it is a power relation that can heal and depersonalise, and being aware of the gaze is part of treating the patient as a person.

§2.2Bourdieu again — body habitus

Bourdieu returns because the body is "part of our own personal identity," and habitus is bodily before it is anything else: "The body is in the social world, but the social world is in the body." Body habitus names socially indoctrinated bodily dispositions, and Bourdieu piles up phrases to capture how deep they go:

  • "Durable, transposable dispositions" — stable, and carried from one situation to another.
  • History transformed into nature — social history that comes to feel biological; "second nature" / "forgotten history."
  • A "conductorless orchestration" — many people's conduct co-ordinated as if by a conductor, with no conductor present (no rule is being consciously followed).

Cultural capital, in particular, is embodied through "a labour of inculcation and assimilation" that "costs time" — acquiring a muscular body means the gym; acquiring a taste for opera means years of reading, listening and learning the instrument, until the knowledge "becomes embodied in yourself." So class is legible on the body: dress and the "cut" of clothes, material choices (a $200 shirt vs a $15 one), and above all how we carry ourselves — posture, an "air of confidence." Raj's memorable example is the etiquette teacher who told women to imagine "an invisible string pulling you up"; shoulders down "already indicates" someone "not quite at ease about their body" — someone not "presenting the body," as Goffman would say.

Reasoning · why "second nature" is the whole trick

Habitus is powerful precisely because it is forgotten. If class were only a bank balance, it could be redistributed; but when class is sedimented into posture, taste and reflex — "second nature" — it feels like who you simply are, not something society did. That is how "social power works through human bodies," inscribing dispositions that "enable people to act in certain ways yet also set limits upon their abilities and aspiration." Yet it is not a prison: bodily knowledge is "creative, dynamic and changeable," so by being reflexive we can resist and even mobilise political change (with effort — and often economic resources — you can learn to taste "the plum and morning grass" in the wine).

Applied / real-world · reading class off a body

Bourdieu's Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste is the machinery here. A working-class habitus may show a strong work ethic and frugality legible in the body and the kind of work it does; a wealthy habitus may show "a sense of entitlement," well-dressed ease and confidence in a crowd. Even gendered ways of sitting — taking up less space vs more — are habitus (a bridge to Young in §2.4). This is the take-home exam skill in a nutshell: shown a person in a photo, you can argue what class is "marked" on the body — and, crucially, explain that it was acquired socially, not born.

§2.3Erving Goffman — the dramaturgical metaphor

For Goffman (symbolic interactionism), "in everyday life we are all on a stage." Social life is a theatrical performance, and the body is the central prop.

Goffman's dramaturgy — the stage kit
ConceptMeaning
Front stageWhere we perform to an audience, following the scene's conventions.
Back stageOut of role, where the performance relaxes.
"Face"The positive social value we claim in an interaction — to be maintained and not lost.
Impression managementActively controlling the impression others form of us.
Social contextSelf-presentation shifts with the setting and audience.

The body conveys the performance: posture and bearing signal "professional," "approachable" or "don't disturb me." Someone "standing upright, looking at you straight in the eyes" is presenting the body — and, as §2.2 showed, that bearing is also class working. Goffman and Bourdieu dovetail: dramaturgy is the performance, habitus is the rehearsal you did not know you were doing.

Reasoning · why "performance" is not fakery

Calling social life a performance does not mean everyone is lying. Goffman's claim is that the self is produced in interaction, not sitting fully-formed behind it — we assemble a presentable self out of props, scripts and bodily bearing, tuned to the audience. The reason a poised body "reads" as competent is that we are all, all the time, doing impression management; the performance is how the social self exists at all, which is why breaking its rules (§ the Week-3 breaching experiments) is so unsettling.

Memory hook · Goffman = "life is theatre"

Front of house vs back of house.

Restaurant image: front stage = the dining room (the waiter performs); back stage = the kitchen (the mask drops). "Face" is what you'd lose if the audience saw back stage mid-performance.

§2.4Iris Marion Young — "Throwing Like a Girl"

The feminist philosopher Iris Marion Young takes the everyday phrase "throw like a girl" and builds from it a phenomenology of feminine body comportment, motility and spatiality ("Throwing Like a Girl," Human Studies 1980). Applied to a boy, the phrase means his throw is weak, his technique "not manly enough." Young's question: why do girls typically throw that way? Her answer flatly rejects biology.

Restricted / inhibited modalities

Against the phenomenologist Straus — who called the difference "biological, not acquired" (even citing the Amazons) — Young argues it is entirely a social construction. Women exhibit restricted modalities of movement: "the whole body is not put into fluid and directed motion," but rather "the motion is concentrated in one body part," and the movement "tends not to reach, extend, lean, stretch, and follow through in the direction of her intention." The same style recurs in "running like a girl, climbing like a girl, swinging like a girl, hitting like a girl." Why? Because girls are socialised to "close" their bodies and take up less space (keep your knees together, mind your space), while boys are encouraged to be open, rough and playful. Femininity is thus an embodied practice: "for the most part, girls and women are not given the opportunity to use their full bodily capacities in free and open engagement with the world, nor are they encouraged as much as boys to develop specific bodily skills" (Young 1990, 154).

Reasoning · the "I cannot" and the body as encumbrance

Young builds on two philosophers: Merleau-Ponty's lived body (the taken-for-granted "I can" that reaches into the world) and de Beauvoir's account of woman as Other, caught between immanence (being tied to the body as object) and transcendence (projecting freely into the world). The feminine modality inserts an "I cannot" alongside the "I can": movement is begun with an anticipation of failure. Women experience the body "as a fragile encumbrance" that must be watched ("saving itself from harm") rather than a transparent medium for their aims — attention divided between the task and the body doing it. Hence a "greater feeling of incapacity, frustration and self-consciousness," and a self-fulfilling underestimation of one's own strength. Under patriarchy, as lived bodies, "we are physically inhibited, confined, positioned and objectified… not open and unambiguous transcendences that move out to master a world that belongs to us" (Young 1990, 153).

Applied / real-world · sport, socialisation & what has changed

The stakes are concrete. Give a girl a ball from a young age — a dad who plays football with her — and "you realise they can be just as good": the modality is trainable because it was taught in the first place. And the phrase harms boys too, teaching them there is a praised "manly" way and a demeaned "girly" way to move. Young wrote this in 1980/1990; Raj flags that its assumptions are now being challenged by the surge of women's professional sport — AFLW, NRLW, women's rugby union and soccer — where women perform at elite calibre. Perfect exam material: shown a sporting image, you can name comportment, motility, spatiality and the social production of "throwing like a girl," and note the historical shift.

§3Part 3 · Sex / gender & contemporary bodies (i)

Sex, gender & sexuality redefined

One biological difference, tied to a social arrangement that hands out privilege unequally.

Gender is "another huge concept," increasingly questioned over the last two decades. The dominant move in contemporary sociology is to see gender as a construction, not something simply "biological you're born with." The organising idea is the sex-gender system: a biological phenomenon — the anatomical difference between the sexes — "has come to be tied up with a social arrangement which awards and withholds privileges inequitably." The biological difference is interpreted socially and "translated into an order of value, where men are primary." Nature favours neither sex for evolutionary success, "but cultures have favoured one sex over the other in terms of social success, or relative wealth, power and freedom." (The Australian feminist philosopher Liz Grosz calls the body's dynamic nature "volatile bodies.")

Sex vs Gender vs Sexuality — three separate axes
AxisDefinitionRange of terms
SEXA person's biological status, typically assigned at birth on external anatomy.Female · Male · Intersex (an umbrella term for differences in reproductive anatomy, chromosomes or hormones that don't fit typical definitions).
GENDERThe social construction of norms, behaviours and roles.Femininity · Masculinity · Nonbinary · Agender · Gender-expansive. Cisgender = identity aligns with birth-assigned sex; Transgender = differs from it; gender transition = a process to align body/self with identity.
SEXUALITYOrientation of sexual desire.Straight (heterosexual) · gay · lesbian · bisexual.

The point of three columns is that they are independent: sex does not dictate gender, and neither dictates sexuality. There is no automatic one-to-one line from "one sex" to "one gender" to "one orientation" — which is why the older overlay that fused them (and assumed heterosexuality as default) has become contested, alongside same-sex relationships and marriage.

Reasoning · why "sex-gender system" and not just "gender"

The word system does real work. The claim is not merely that gender roles vary; it is that a raw biological fact (bodies differ) gets plugged into a social machine that converts difference into hierarchy — "an order of value" in which one side is treated as primary and privileges flow unequally. So you cannot fix gender inequality by pointing at biology (biology is not the inequality); the inequality lives in the social arrangement that interprets and ranks the difference. That is also why sex, gender and sexuality must be prised apart analytically: collapsing them is precisely how the "system" naturalises its rankings.

Applied / real-world · sport as the sharpest gatekeeper

Nowhere is gender coding more rigid than sport — "male sport, female sport… you can't be in between." So sport is where the sex-gender system's policing becomes visible:

• Caster Semenya — an intersex woman subjected to sex-verification and testosterone rules by the IAAF while competing in women's track. • Laurel Hubbard — the first transgender athlete at the Olympics (NZ; began hormone therapy in 2012), accused by competitors of an unfair advantage. • The 2024 women's boxing controversy. • Beach handball: the rules required women to wear bikini bottoms "with a close fit and cut on an upward angle," sides no more than four inches — literally "don't cover up too much," a rule "for the male audience." When Norway's team wore shorts at Euro 2021 they were fined. • Historical parallel: women were banned from the 1960s Boston Marathon (fear it would damage the uterus) until a woman entered under a man's name and was physically chased off the course. This is the exam's "read the image" gift — a single sports photo can be unpacked as gatekeeping, the sex-gender system and the policing of the binary.

Reasoning · the everyday reproduction of gender

The system is maintained less by dramatic rulings than by mundane repetition. Paid vs unpaid work: waged work outside the home is coded male (the "breadwinner"), while domestic labour — cooking, cleaning, laundry, care, done largely by women — is unpaid, naturalising separate male/female "domains." Gendered marketing: at Kmart/Target, blue, "tough," Bob-the-Builder for boys; pink, pastel, flowers for girls. And the "emotional woman / rational man" binary polices feeling itself. Each is a small "gatekeeping intervention" that, repeated, holds the whole arrangement in place — and shows the body is "not merely a biological entity but a social construct deeply embedded in everyday life."

R Recall checklist — sex, gender & sexuality
  • Sex-gender system — a biological difference tied to a social arrangement that awards privilege unequally; difference "translated" into an order of value (men primary).
  • Sex = biological status (female/male/intersex); Gender = social construction of norms/roles (cis/trans/nonbinary); Sexuality = orientation. Three separate axes.
  • Grosz — "volatile bodies" for the body's dynamic nature.
  • Sport gatekeeps — Semenya (intersex, testosterone rules), Hubbard (first trans Olympian), beach-handball bikini rule (Norway fined), Boston Marathon ban.
  • Everyday reproduction — paid (male) vs unpaid domestic (female) work; blue/pink marketing; "emotional woman / rational man."
§4Part 3 · Sex / gender & contemporary bodies (ii)

Contemporary bodies — the toned body, capitalism & material culture

From the gym to the sneaker to the care-robot: the body as project, commodity and thing.

The final movement applies the whole toolkit to bodies in contemporary culture. The thread is capitalism: once "people's bodies are used to sell products," a "Marxian exploitation of the body for profit" is in play, generating norms and expectations we then labour to meet. Five contemporary body-types round out the lecture.

The toned body as cultural icon

"Working out" is presented as a "glamourized & sexualized yuppie activity." The firm, developed body reads as a symbol of "correct attitude" — that one "cares" about oneself and how one appears — and so signals willpower, energy, control over infantile impulse and "the ability to 'shape one's life.'" This, the deck states plainly, is the logic of neoliberal capitalism.

Reasoning · cultivating the neo-liberal self

Neoliberalism (roughly post-1970s) shifts responsibility from society onto the individual: to better yourself, you spend the time and money — gym, coaching college, yoga. So the body "becomes commodified" and must be continually refashioned to keep up with fashion and appearance; it is never finished. That is why a toned body signals virtue: it visibly proves you have done the neoliberal work of self-improvement. Dating apps like Tinder distil this to a glance — judgement on a superficial portrait, with even the bio "constructed" — so the "production of the neo-liberal self" runs on cosmetics, curation and the promise (and anxiety) that you could always optimise more.

Applied / real-world · Jean Kilbourne & the body used to sell

Because bodies sell products, they are turned into objects. Jean Kilbourne's analysis of advertising (shown in class): ads "sell values… concepts of normalcy," telling women "what's most important is how we look," surrounding us with an unattainable ideal of "flawlessness" (no pores, achieved by airbrushing/Photoshop — "no one looks like this, including her"). Women's bodies are "turned into things," even "just parts of things" (a body becomes the Michelob bottle), and dehumanisation "is almost always the first step toward justifying violence." The result is a public-health problem — an epidemic of eating disorders and the tyranny of the ideal image. Kate Winslet's refusal to be digitally slimmed is the counter-example. This is the Marxian point in flesh: the body exploited to generate profit and, with it, the norms that keep the fitness and cosmetics industries running (five 24-hour gyms in one suburb).

The early- vs late-capitalist body

Our relationship to the body has transformed with capitalism.

"Going to the gym is undertaking a project": you enter "a skinny person," commit to protein diets and milkshakes, and hope to emerge "somebody different… beautifully toned" — investment that fits the neoliberal logic exactly. Tattoos and piercings "express your identity, your uniqueness" — the body "as a project" of hedonistic self-expression.

The body, consumption & material culture — Hockey's trainers

The week's third reading, Hockey, Dilley, Robinson & Sherlock ("There's not just trainers or non-trainers, there's like degrees of trainers," Journal of Material Culture 2014), takes the body-as-consumer into material culture. Its twist on the usual question ("how do people make objects?") is the reverse: how do trainers "make" the individual? The trainer is a "body prosthetic"; identity "begins at the body" (Csordas, Ingold), and footwear promotes "the body techniques by which we relate to the earth" (Tenner). The paper's engine is Kopytoff's pair of concepts:

Reasoning · why a sneaker is a sociology of the body

Worn, "the shoe merges with the body, no longer a commodity, instead becoming personalized through embodied use." So a trainer is not just bought (Marx/consumption) — it is lived, an extension of the moving body (Bourdieu's habitus, Young's motility). Ingold's reframing seals it: better than "object" is "thing" — "a place where several goings-on become entwined." The trainer is where consumption, class, gender, sexuality, age and bodily movement all knot together, which is exactly why "there's like degrees of trainers": the single category holds a whole map of social differentiation.

Applied / real-world · reproducing and resisting distinction

Crucially, trainers do both jobs. They reproduce gender/class/sexuality (branding confers status; wearers are ranked — "nice trainers vs horrible"). But through singularisation they can resist those same locations: US senator Wendy Davis wore "hot pink" running trainers for an 11-hour anti-abortion filibuster (sales then spiked); a gay man hunts for the "most butch" trainers as an in-joke; hospital porters use forbidden trainers to unsettle an institutional hierarchy. So the same shoe can "make" the wearer and subvert the norms it usually signals — the reading's punchline that material culture is where distinction is both "reproduced and resisted."

Able/healthy vs ill/disordered bodies — and non-human bodies

Two final body-types keep the picture honest. The body matters in contemporary culture through "major changes in medical practice, medical technology, the changing conceptions of disease and illness, finally the greying population." Bodies excrete, tire, age, sicken and die; sick, diseased or dead bodies "conjure different emotions" — the body breathing life vs "a sick person in the ICU" produce very different social relations (a callback to Foucault's medicalization in §2.1). And the biological body is increasingly supplemented by non-human bodies: socially-assistive robots. In Japanese aged-care, robots "care and even express emotion" — they listen, converse, prompt exercise — to offset the cost of employing people; robot teachers instruct via an interface. This is "a new frontier" for the sociology of the body.

Memory hook · the five contemporary bodies

Toned · Selling · Consuming · Ageing/Ill · Robotic.

Walk §4 as five body-types: the toned (neoliberal self) → the body used for selling (Kilbourne) → the consuming body & material culture (Hockey's trainers) → the ageing/ill body (medicalization, greying population) → the robotic (non-human) body.

  • Neo-liberal self — responsibility shifted to the individual; body commodified & continually refashioned (gym, Tinder).

§5Conclusion

Conclusion — the conceptual lenses & the sociological imagination

One body; many lenses; an intersectional whole.

The lecture's payoff is that there is no single "sociology of the body" — there are diverse conceptual lenses, each catching a different facet. Line them up and the whole week resolves into three ways of "seeing" the body.

Three lenses on the body — the week in one frame
LensThe body seen as…Theorist(s) & concept
Lived experienceFelt from the inside — movement, space, capacity.Young — phenomenology; motility & the "I cannot."
Social phenomenonShaped & marked by the social order.Bourdieu — habitus; Goffman — dramaturgy.
Political subjectDisciplined, governed, policed.Foucault — medical gaze & biopower; the sex-gender system.

The concepts to carry forward: embodiment, habitus, phenomenology, dramaturgy, identity, medicalization and ageing — each names a distinct route by which "the social gets into the body." And the class–gender–race–ability threads are not parallel lines but overlapping ones.

Reasoning · why intersectionality is the point, not a footnote

Every case this week refused a single axis. The beach-handball ruling is gender and the male-audience economy; Bourdieu reads class off the body; the "Fair & Lovely" skin-whitening ad and racialised bodies fuse race, gender and consumption; the ICU body is ability, ageing and medicalization together. So an intersectional reading — class × gender × race × ability co-producing how a body is lived, valued and policed — is not an add-on; it is what the evidence forces. You get there by using the sociological imagination: linking the private bodily trouble (a fined uniform, an unwelcome medical gaze, a habitus that feels out of place) to the public social structure that produced it.

Applied / real-world · your take-home exam, exactly

This section is the assessment brief. The instructions are literally "look at the pictures… can you see sociological concepts jumping out of them? Tell us the mechanism, the social organisation, the processes taking place under our nose." So for any image: (1) name the lens (lived / social / political) and the concept (habitus, medical gaze, dramaturgy, sex-gender system, commoditisation…); (2) explain the mechanism beneath the scene, not just its appearance; (3) layer the intersecting axes; and (4) be concise — 200 words rewards brevity and clarity, and three references are enough (one may be a Week-1 definition of the sociology of everyday life). That is the whole guide, turned into a method.
