§0Orientation · compartments

The three compartments of the thigh

One rule runs the whole lecture: compartment = nerve = action.

Deep fascia and its intermuscular septa wall the thigh into three compartments, and — as in the arm — each compartment is a package: its own muscles, its own nerve, its own movement theme. Learn the package and every muscle below has a home.

The three thigh compartments
CompartmentMain actionNerve
AnteriorFlex thigh at hip; extend leg at kneeFemoral nerve
MedialAdduct thigh at hipObturator nerve
PosteriorExtend thigh at hip; flex leg at kneeSciatic nerve

This lecture takes the anterior and medial compartments (the posterior/hamstring compartment is L12), then the knee joint they act on.

Reasoning · why compartment predicts nerve and action

The limb bud's muscle mass splits into a flexor and an extensor sheet, and each keeps the nerve that grew into it — so muscles that share a compartment share a nerve and a broad job. In the thigh that gives a clean map: the femoral nerve runs the front (knee extensors/hip flexors), the obturator the inside (adductors), the sciatic the back (hip extensors/knee flexors). Name the compartment and you can predict both the nerve and what a lesion of it will knock out.

R Recall checklist — compartments
  • Anterior — femoral nerve; flex hip, extend knee.
  • Medial — obturator nerve; adduct the thigh.
  • Posterior — sciatic nerve; extend hip, flex knee.
§1Anterior compartment · femoral nerve

Anterior compartment of the thigh

The hip flexors and the knee extensor — all on the femoral nerve.

Everything here is supplied by the femoral nerve and works to flex the thigh at the hip, extend the leg at the knee, or both. Five players: iliopsoas, sartorius, the four-headed quadriceps, and tensor fasciae latae.

§1.1Iliopsoas & sartorius

Iliopsoas is the chief flexor of the thigh at the hip. It is really two muscles that fuse: psoas major (from the transverse processes, bodies and intervertebral discs of T12–L5) and iliacus (from the iliac fossa). They join, pass under the inguinal ligament, and insert together on the lesser trochanter. Nerve supply is split: psoas major by the anterior rami of L1–L3, iliacus by the femoral nerve.

Sartorius is the most superficial anterior thigh muscle — a long strap from the ASIS running obliquely down to the pes anserinus on the medial proximal tibia. Because it crosses both joints it assists flexion of the hip and the knee, and it also abducts and laterally rotates the thigh — the combination that puts you in the cross-legged "tailor's position" (Latin sartor = tailor). Nerve: femoral.

Memory hook · sartorius = the tailor

"Sit like a tailor." To cross your legs you flex the hip and knee, abduct and laterally rotate the thigh — every action of sartorius in one pose.

§1.2Quadriceps femoris & tensor fasciae latae

Quadriceps femoris — the great knee extensor — has four heads that all converge on the quadriceps tendon → patella → patellar ligament → tibial tuberosity:

  • Rectus femoris — from the AIIS; the only head that crosses the hip, so it flexes the thigh as well as extending the knee.
  • Vastus lateralis — from the intertrochanteric line, greater trochanter, gluteal tuberosity and linea aspera; extends the knee.
  • Vastus medialis — from the intertrochanteric line, linea aspera and medial supracondylar line; extends the knee (its inferior fibres steady the patella).
  • Vastus intermedius — from the upper anterior and lateral femur, deep between the other two vasti; extends the knee.

Tensor fasciae latae (TFL) sits anterolaterally: from the outer iliac crest just behind the ASIS into the iliotibial tract, which it tenses to stabilise the hip and knee. It is the exception to the femoral-nerve rule — supplied by the superior gluteal nerve (it is developmentally a gluteal muscle).

Reasoning · why the patella sits in the tendon

The patella is a sesamoid bone embedded in the quadriceps tendon. Riding over the front of the knee, it holds the tendon away from the joint's axis, lengthening the extensor's lever arm — so the quadriceps generates more turning force for the same pull. It also spreads compressive load and protects the tendon. Lose or fracture it and knee extension is markedly weakened.

Clinical / real-world · femoral nerve & psoas

A femoral nerve lesion abolishes knee extension (quadriceps) and weakens hip flexion, with sensory loss over the anterior thigh and medial leg — the patient's knee buckles and stairs become hard. Because psoas runs from the spine to the lesser trochanter, a psoas abscess (e.g. from spinal TB) tracks down into the groin and gives a painful, flexed hip that resists extension.

§1.3Femoral triangle & adductor canal

The femoral triangle is the key gateway of the anterior thigh: bounded by the inguinal ligament (above), sartorius (laterally) and adductor longus (medially), with a floor of iliopsoas and pectineus. It transmits (lateral → medial) the femoral nerve, artery and vein. Distally the vessels leave the apex of the triangle and run in the adductor canal (carrying the femoral artery, femoral vein and saphenous nerve) down to the knee.

§2Medial compartment · obturator nerve

Medial compartment of the thigh

The adductor group — one origin region, one nerve, one job (mostly).

The medial compartment is the adductor group. Its muscles share a common origin around the ischiopubic ramus, pull the thigh toward the midline, and are supplied by the obturator nerve — with two informative exceptions.

  • Gracilis — the most superficial, a thin strap from the pubis running vertically to the pes anserinus; because it crosses two joints it adducts the hip and flexes the knee.
  • Pectineus — from the pecten pubis to the pectineal line of the femur; it forms part of the floor of the femoral triangle.
  • Adductor longus — the most superficial of the three named adductors; anterior pubis → linea aspera.
  • Adductor brevis — deep to longus; body of pubis → upper linea aspera.
  • Adductor magnus — the big one, in two parts: an adductor part (ischiopubic ramus → linea aspera) and a hamstring part (ischial tuberosity → adductor tubercle). It adducts and medially rotates the thigh.
  • Obturator externus — a deep lateral rotator of the thigh at the hip.
Reasoning · adductor magnus, a muscle with two identities

Adductor magnus is a hybrid, and its nerve supply proves it. The adductor part behaves like the rest of the compartment and takes the obturator nerve; the hamstring part — which arises from the ischial tuberosity like the true hamstrings and runs to the adductor tubercle — takes the sciatic (tibial) nerve. So one muscle straddles two compartments developmentally, and its dual innervation is the clue.

Clinical / real-world · pes anserinus & groin strain

The pes anserinus ("goose's foot") on the medial tibia is where sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus converge — three muscles from three compartments on three different nerves (femoral, obturator, tibial), a favourite exam point. Clinically, adductor longus is the muscle most often torn in a groin strain (kicking, rapid direction change).

R Recall checklist — medial compartment
  • Group action — adduct the thigh; common origin at the ischiopubic ramus; obturator nerve.
  • Gracilis — most superficial; two-joint; inserts on the pes anserinus.
  • Pectineus — forms the floor of the femoral triangle.
  • Adductor magnus — adductor part = obturator; hamstring part = sciatic.
  • Obturator externus — a lateral rotator of the thigh.
§3The knee joint

The knee joint

A modified hinge held together far more by ligaments and muscle than by bone.

The knee is a synovial modified hinge joint with two articulations: the tibiofemoral (the femoral and tibial condyles) and the patellofemoral. Its bony surfaces are nearly flat, so it depends on menisci, ligaments and muscle for congruence and stability.

§3.1Articular surfaces & menisci

The rounded femoral condyles sit on the shallow tibial plateau, deepened by two menisci — fibrocartilage crescents that improve congruence, spread load and absorb shock. The medial meniscus is more C-shaped and, crucially, is attached to the tibial (medial) collateral ligament; the lateral meniscus is more circular and more mobile.

§3.2Ligaments

Divide them into those outside the capsule and those within it:

The collateral ligaments resist side-to-side (varus/valgus) stress; the cruciates cross inside the joint and control front-to-back glide (§4).

§3.3Movements & stability

  • Flexion — hamstrings; extension — quadriceps.
  • Medial rotation (of the flexed leg) — semitendinosus & semimembranosus; lateral rotation — biceps femoris.
  • Popliteus "unlocks" the fully extended knee (laterally rotating the femur on the fixed tibia) to allow flexion to begin.

Stability comes from the surrounding muscles and tendons — the single most important stabiliser is the quadriceps femoris, especially the inferior fibres of vastus medialis and lateralis — together with the ligaments. The joint is also cushioned by a capsule, synovial membrane and numerous bursae.

Reasoning · the "screw-home" mechanism

In the last few degrees of extension the femur medially rotates slightly on the tibia (or the tibia laterally rotates on the femur), tightening the cruciates and collaterals so the knee "locks" into a stable, close-packed position you can stand on with little muscle effort. To flex again, popliteus must first "unlock" it by rotating the femur back. It's why standing is cheap but rising from a locked knee needs a deliberate un-twist.

Clinical / real-world · bursitis

The knee's bursae inflame in characteristic patterns: prepatellar bursitis ("housemaid's knee") from kneeling upright, and infrapatellar bursitis ("clergyman's knee") from more upright kneeling. Swelling inside the joint (an effusion) instead points to internal derangement — a meniscal or ligament injury (§4).

R Recall checklist — knee joint
  • Type — synovial modified hinge; tibiofemoral + patellofemoral.
  • Menisci — medial is attached to the tibial collateral ligament; both deepen & cushion.
  • Movements — flex (hamstrings), extend (quadriceps), medial rotate (semi's), lateral rotate (biceps femoris).
  • Popliteus — unlocks the extended knee.
  • Chief stabiliser — the quadriceps (inferior vastus medialis/lateralis fibres).
§4Applied anatomy

Knee joint — clinical

The ligaments you just learned, failing at their weak points.

Because the knee is soft-tissue-dependent, its classic injuries are ligament and meniscus injuries — and each maps to a specific structure and a specific test.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

A footballer is hit on the outside of a planted, slightly bent knee. Which three structures are most likely torn together?

The "unhappy triad": the tibial (medial) collateral ligament, the medial meniscus, and the ACL. A blow to the lateral side forces the knee into valgus, tearing the MCL — and because the medial meniscus is attached to the MCL, it tears too, often with the ACL.

The unhappy triad

A lateral blow to a planted, flexed knee ruptures the medial collateral ligament, tears the medial meniscus (dragged by its MCL attachment), and tears the ACL — the three "unhappy" structures together.

Cruciate injuries & their tests

  • ACL — resists anterior translation of the tibia on the femur; torn in non-contact pivoting/hyperextension. Tested by the anterior drawer and Lachman tests.
  • PCL — resists posterior translation of the tibia; torn in dashboard injuries (a blow driving the flexed tibia backward). Tested by the posterior drawer test.
  • Collaterals — tested by valgus (medial/tibial collateral) and varus (lateral/fibular collateral) stress.

Osgood–Schlatter disease

In active adolescents, repeated quadriceps traction on the immature tibial tuberosity (where the patellar ligament inserts) causes a painful traction apophysitis — Osgood–Schlatter disease — with a tender bump below the knee.

Reasoning · why the drawer tests name the cruciates

Each cruciate is named for its tibial attachment and stops the tibia sliding one way. The ACL runs up-and-back to stop the tibia sliding forward — so if you can pull the tibia forward (anterior drawer/Lachman), the ACL is gone. The PCL stops the tibia sliding backward — so abnormal backward glide (posterior drawer) means a PCL tear. The direction of abnormal movement names the torn ligament.

R Recall checklist — knee clinical
  • Unhappy triad — MCL + medial meniscus + ACL; from a lateral blow to a planted knee.
  • ACL — stops anterior tibial glide; anterior drawer / Lachman test.
  • PCL — stops posterior tibial glide; dashboard injury; posterior drawer test.
  • Osgood–Schlatter — traction apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity in adolescents.
