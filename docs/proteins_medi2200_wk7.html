§0Orientation · learning outcomes

Sequence, shape & the jobs proteins do

One rule underlies the whole lecture: sequence dictates shape, and shape dictates job.

Proteins are the cell's doers. Almost every active task — catalysing a reaction, pumping an ion, reading a signal, holding the cell in shape, generating movement — is a protein doing it. This lecture builds the logic from the ground up: what proteins are made of, how a flat sequence folds into a working 3D machine, how enzymes accelerate chemistry, and how the cell turns that activity up and down.

The organising principle, stated once so everything hangs off it: a protein's function is derived from its 3D structure, and that structure is determined by the primary amino acid sequence together with intramolecular noncovalent interactions. Change the sequence and you can change the fold; change the fold and you change (or destroy) the job.

The lecture's learning outcomes

  • Discuss the levels of protein structure and how they relate to function.
  • Outline how the amino acid sequence determines 3-D structure.
  • Describe the properties of an enzyme and how they are regulated.
  • Recognise different enzyme groups and the reactions they catalyse.

Six jobs the cell's proteins perform

The opening overview frames why proteins matter — the same molecule class covers an astonishing range of tasks:

  • Organising 3-D space — scaffolds and the cytoskeleton position the genome, organelles, cytoplasm, protein complexes and membranes.
  • Controlling protein activity — regulators switch other proteins on and off (the whole of §4).
  • Monitoring & transmitting information — receptors and signalling proteins sense the environment and relay it.
  • Membrane transport — moving small molecules and ions across membranes.
  • Catalysis — enzymes accelerate cellular reactions (the whole of §3).
  • Generating force — motor proteins produce movement.
🤔 Predict · before you read on

If you were handed only a protein's amino acid sequence, could you (in principle) know its final 3D shape?

In principle yes — the native fold is encoded in the sequence, and it is usually the conformation of lowest free energy. Predicting it from sequence alone is the field's "holy grail" (now largely cracked by structure-prediction AI), but experimentally we still confirm structures by NMR or X-ray crystallography, or model on a known relative (§2.1).

Reasoning · why start with the sequence

Every later idea is a consequence of this one. Secondary structure forms because of the backbone the sequence lays down; tertiary structure forms because of where the sequence places hydrophobic vs polar side chains; an enzyme works because the sequence positions the exact catalytic residues in space. Learn the sequence-to-structure-to-function chain first and the rest of the lecture is that chain playing out in detail.

Clinical / real-world

Because the sequence encodes the fold, a single wrong amino acid can be pathological. Sickle-cell disease is one changed residue in haemoglobin's primary structure; and the misfolding diseases of §2 (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's) are what happens when the sequence-to-shape step goes wrong. The rule "sequence dictates shape dictates function" is also the rule "get the sequence wrong and you get disease."

R Recall checklist — orientation
  • Core rule — function comes from 3D structure, set by the primary sequence + noncovalent interactions.
  • Protein jobs — organise space, control other proteins, signal, transport, catalyse, generate force.
  • Four levels — primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
§1Part 1 · hierarchical structure

The four levels of protein structure

From a flat sequence, up through local folds, to a packed 3D machine — and assemblies of machines.

Protein structure is built in a hierarchy, each level constraining the next. A guide to the whole of Part 1: primary sequence, the three secondary-structure elements, how those combine into motifs, domains and whole tertiary folds, and finally how separate chains assemble into quaternary and supramolecular structures.

§1.1Primary structure

The primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids, joined head-to-tail by peptide bonds, written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. It is deceptively simple — just an order of residues — yet it carries all the information needed to specify the higher levels, because function is derived from the 3D structure, which is determined by the primary sequence and intramolecular noncovalent interactions.

§1.2Secondary structure — α helix, β sheet, β turn

Secondary structures are stable, local spatial arrangements of segments of the polypeptide chain, held together by hydrogen bonds between backbone amide (N–H) and carbonyl (C=O) groups — note it is the backbone, not the side chains, that H-bonds here. There are three:

  • α helix — the chain coils; backbone H-bonds run along the coil. R groups project outward from the helix surface, so they set the chemical character of the helix's faces. Proline cannot participate in backbone H-bonding and is usually excluded from an α helix (a "helix breaker").
  • β sheet — extended strands lie side by side, stabilised by H-bonds between backbone O and H atoms of different strands. Alternate R groups project above and below the plane of the sheet.
  • β turn — a compact four-residue element that reverses the direction of the chain, letting a long polypeptide fold back on itself into a compact shape. Glycine (smallest R group) and proline (built-in bend) are commonly found in turns.

Not all of a protein is ordered: proteins also contain intrinsically disordered regions — segments with no fixed secondary structure that can still be functional (often in binding and regulation).

Reasoning · why backbone H-bonds, and why proline breaks the helix

Secondary structure is a way for the backbone to satisfy its own hydrogen-bonding needs while burying nothing yet — the amide and carbonyl groups pair up regularly, which is energetically cheap and repeatable regardless of sequence. Proline is the exception that proves the rule: its side chain loops back to the backbone nitrogen, so that nitrogen has no N–H to donate, and the ring is rigid. No donor + a fixed kink = a residue that cannot sit inside a helix, so it appears at turns and helix ends instead.

Memory hook · the three elements

"Helix hugs, sheet spreads, turn about-faces." The α helix coils with R groups out; the β sheet lies flat with R groups up/down; the β turn is the 4-residue U-turn (think Glycine + Proline).

§1.3Tertiary structure — motifs, domains & whole folds

Tertiary structure is the overall 3D fold of a single polypeptide — how its secondary elements pack together into a functional shape. It can be visualised several ways for the same protein (e.g. the Ras GTPase): a ribbon highlighting β strands and α helices, a surface map showing positive/negative charge, or a space-filling model showing how tightly the chain packs into a small volume.

Structural motifs

A motif is a regular, recurring combination of secondary structures with a specific function, often encoded by a conserved sequence motif. Classic examples:

  • Helix-turn-helix — a DNA-binding motif in transcription regulators.
  • Helix-loop-helix / EF-hand — calcium-binding and DNA-binding regulatory motifs.
  • Coiled-coil — found in intermediate filaments and transcription factors; built from a heptad repeat with a hydrophobic residue at positions 1 and 4, which lets two helices wind around each other.

Domains — the modular units

Motifs build up into domains: independently stable, modular units from which larger proteins are assembled. In the Src protein kinase, distinct regulatory domains sit alongside the kinase catalytic domain, whose two lobes form the ATP-binding site. Domains are shuffled and reused across the proteome — ~75% of eukaryotic proteins have multiple structural domains (e.g. the EGF domain recurs in many proteins).

Fibrous vs globular

Some proteins have elongated, fibrous shapes — collagen is the archetype (in the extracellular space, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and skin), its strength reinforced by crosslinks formed by hydroxylation of lysine. Other, globular proteins polymerise into long filaments — the cytoskeletal filament F-actin is assembled from globular G-actin subunits.

Clinical / real-world · collagen and vitamin C

Collagen's crosslinks depend on hydroxylation of lysine (and proline), and those hydroxylase enzymes require vitamin C. Without it the crosslinks fail, collagen is weak, and the result is scurvy — bleeding gums, poor wound healing, fragile vessels. A defect in the collagen gene itself gives osteogenesis imperfecta (brittle bones). A structural-protein fact with a direct bedside consequence.

§1.4Quaternary structure & supramolecular complexes

Quaternary structure exists when a functional protein is made of more than one polypeptide chain. The influenza surface protein hemagglutinin (HA) is a good example: three HA subunits assemble into a trimer, stabilised by lateral interactions between long α helices that form a triple-stranded coiled-coil stalk, while each distal globular domain binds sialic acid on the surface of the target cell.

At the largest scale, supramolecular complexes can contain tens to hundreds of polypeptide chains (and sometimes nucleic acids), exceeding 1 megadalton (MDa) in mass and 300 nm in size. The transcription initiation complex — core RNA polymerase plus general transcription factors and a mediator — is one such molecular machine.

Reasoning · why build proteins from modular domains and subunits

Modularity is an evolutionary shortcut. A domain that already folds and does one job (bind ATP, bind DNA, bind calcium) can be copied and pasted into a new protein without re-inventing it — which is why ~75% of eukaryotic proteins are multidomain and why the same domains recur everywhere. Quaternary assembly adds more: it lets the cell build huge machines from manageable parts, share regulation across subunits (cooperativity), and swap one subunit to retune the whole complex.

R Recall checklist — structure hierarchy
  • Primary — amino acids joined by peptide bonds, N- to C-terminus.
  • Secondary — backbone hydrogen bonds; α helix (R groups out), β sheet (R groups above/below), β turn (4 residues, reverses direction).
  • Helix breaker — proline (no backbone N–H to donate); glycine + proline favour turns.
  • Motifs — helix-turn-helix (DNA), coiled-coil (heptad repeat, hydrophobic at 1 & 4).
  • Domains — modular units; ~75% of eukaryotic proteins are multidomain.
  • Fibrous vs globular — collagen (lysine-hydroxylation crosslinks) vs F-actin from G-actin.
  • Quaternary — ≥2 polypeptides (HA trimer); supramolecular complexes > 1 MDa.
§2Part 2 · protein folding

Folding, quality control & misfolding disease

The core folds itself hydrophobically; helpers speed it; failures aggregate into disease.

The amino acid sequence determines the 3D structure — but reaching that structure reliably, and disposing of chains that fail, is an active, policed process. This section covers what drives folding, the chaperones and proteasome that supervise it, and what happens when misfolded proteins escape into amyloid.

§2.1The folding problem

Tertiary structure is stabilised primarily by hydrophobic interactions between nonpolar side chains, plus hydrogen bonds involving polar side chains and backbone groups. The driving idea is the hydrophobic effect: nonpolar (hydrophobic) residues cluster together like drops of oil in the folded core, driven away from the surrounding water, while charged and polar side chains sit on the surface making stabilising contacts with water and ions.

Folding is hierarchical and fast: primary → secondary → small motifs → domains → final tertiary structure, with motifs forming before domains. The endpoint is the native state, usually the conformation of lowest free energy (G). Predicting that 3D structure from sequence alone is the field's "holy grail"; structures are otherwise determined experimentally by NMR or X-ray crystallography, or modelled on a known related structure.

Reasoning · why the hydrophobic effect folds proteins

It is really about water, not the protein. Water molecules next to an exposed nonpolar group cannot H-bond freely and must form an ordered cage, which is entropically costly. Burying the nonpolar groups together in the core frees that caged water, raising the system's entropy. So the fold that hides hydrophobic side chains inside and exposes polar ones is favoured — the protein collapses to its lowest-free-energy native state largely because water pushes it there.

§2.2Chaperones & chaperonins

Folding in the crowded cell needs help, supplied by ATP-dependent assistants:

  • Molecular chaperones (e.g. Hsp70, heat-shock protein 70) bind a short exposed segment of a substrate and stabilise unfolded or partly folded proteins, preventing aggregation or premature degradation.
  • Chaperonins are folding chambers — an unfolded protein is enclosed in a protected environment, giving it time to fold properly.

§2.3Protein degradation — the ubiquitin–proteasome system

Proteins that are damaged, misfolded, or simply no longer needed are destroyed by the ubiquitin–proteasome system:

  1. Ubiquitin ligases (E1, E2, E3) attach a chain of ubiquitin molecules to lysine residues on the target protein.
  2. At the proteasome, the ubiquitin chain is removed, and the target is unfolded and translocated into the interior of the proteasome.
  3. Inside, it is degraded into peptide fragments by three threonine proteases.

§2.4Misfolding & amyloid disease

When quality control fails, misfolded or denatured proteins can form well-organised amyloid fibril aggregates built on a cross-β-sheet structure: two long, nearly flat β sheets pack together and twist to form protofilaments, which assemble into thicker fibrils. These accumulate as aggregates or plaques, inside or outside cells, in tissues including the joints, liver and brain.

Clinical / real-world · one shape, many diseases

The striking lesson is that chemically unrelated proteins converge on the same cross-β amyloid architecture and cause disease by aggregating — from the neurodegenerations (Alzheimer's β-amyloid, Parkinson's α-synuclein, Huntington's huntingtin) to type 2 diabetes (islet amylin) and the prion diseases (PrPSc). Because the toxic species is a conformation, not a foreign molecule, these are hard to treat — and it is why the folding/quality-control machinery of §2.2–2.3 matters so much.

R Recall checklist — folding
  • Driving force — the hydrophobic effect: nonpolar core, polar surface.
  • Native state — usually the conformation of lowest free energy.
  • Chaperones — Hsp70 binds exposed segments; chaperonins = enclosed folding chambers; both ATP-dependent.
  • Degradation — E1/E2/E3 ligases add polyubiquitin to lysines → proteasome unfolds & cuts via threonine proteases.
  • Amyloid — cross-β-sheet fibrils; Alzheimer's (β-amyloid), Parkinson's (α-synuclein), T2DM (amylin).
§3Part 3 · binding & catalysis

Enzymes: how proteins catalyse reactions

Bind the substrate, lower the barrier, hand off the product — faster, without shifting the equilibrium.

An enzyme is a protein whose fold positions a precise set of side chains to grip a substrate and speed its reaction. This section covers how enzymes bind and specify substrates, how they lower the activation energy, the chemistry they use, why activity is pH-dependent, and how they are organised for speed.

§3.1Binding, specificity & the active site

Enzyme function depends on binding substrates, and the specificity of ligand binding is set by the protein's fold and its amino acid side chains — the shape and chemistry of the pocket admit some molecules and reject others. Mechanistically: the enzyme (E) binds substrate (S) at a fixed active site to form an enzyme–substrate (ES) complex. The ES complex is in equilibrium with free E + S and is an intermediate on the way to product (P). Crucially, catalysis does not change the equilibrium constant of the reaction — only how fast it is reached.

The active site of trypsin (a serine protease) illustrates the layout: a substrate-binding pocket that selects the substrate, plus a catalytic site holding the catalytic triad — Ser-195, His-57 and Asp-102 — that actually breaks the peptide bond. In some enzymes the binding and catalytic regions overlap; in others they are structurally distinct.

§3.2Lowering the activation energy

All chemical reactions pass through one or more high-energy transition states. The activation energy (ΔG‡) is the free-energy difference between the reactants and that highest point, and the reaction rate is inversely related to the activation energy. Enzymes accelerate reactions by reducing the free energy of the transition state — stabilising it — thereby lowering ΔG‡.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

An enzyme makes a reaction go a million times faster. Does it also make the reaction "go further" — i.e. produce more product at equilibrium?

No. An enzyme lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse directions equally, so it speeds the approach to equilibrium but does not change the equilibrium position. You reach the same product/reactant ratio — just far sooner.

§3.3Catalytic mechanisms — acid–base catalysis

A common strategy is acid–base catalysis, mediated by one or more amino acid side chains that donate a proton (acid) or accept a proton (base) at the right moment. General base catalysis can be performed by the unprotonated forms of Asp, Glu, His, Tyr, Cys and Lys, and a single residue can act as both acid and base at different points in one catalytic cycle.

Lysozyme is the worked case. Found in tears, saliva, sweat and other body fluids, it is a glycoside hydrolase that catalyses hydrolysis of the β-1,4 linkage between N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (NAG) in peptidoglycan — the major component of the gram-positive bacterial cell wall — using both acid and base catalysis. Breaking that wall makes lysozyme an antimicrobial agent.

§3.4Why activity is pH-dependent

Because acid–base catalysis needs its side chains in a particular ionisation state (protonated or unprotonated), enzyme activity is pH-dependent, peaking where the catalytic residues carry the right charge:

  • Lysosomal hydrolases have a pH optimum near 4.5 — matching the acidic interior of the lysosome where they work.
  • Pancreatic serine proteases (e.g. chymotrypsin) have an activity optimum near pH 8, because the active-site His-57 has a pKa ~6.8 and must be in the correct ionisation state for catalysis.
Worked example · why chymotrypsin peaks near pH 8

Chymotrypsin's active-site His-57 must act as a general base (accepting a proton from the catalytic serine). Its pKa is about 6.8. Predict how activity changes from acidic to alkaline pH.

  1. State the rule: to act as a base, His-57 must be unprotonated (deprotonated). A residue is mostly deprotonated when the surrounding pH > pKa.
  2. At low pH (say 4–5, well below 6.8): His-57 is mostly protonated → it cannot accept a proton → little catalysis.
  3. As pH rises above ~6.8: an increasing fraction of His-57 is deprotonated → it can act as a base → activity climbs.
  4. Near pH 8, His-57 is predominantly unprotonated (and other groups still intact), giving maximal activity.

Answer: activity is low in acid and rises to an optimum near pH 8, because the catalytic His must be deprotonated — the pH optimum reports the ionisation state the mechanism needs.

§3.5Organising enzymes for speed — multienzyme complexes

In a pathway where enzymes A → B → C act in sequence, how they are arranged sets the pace:

  • Free in solution — intermediates must diffuse from one enzyme to the next, which can be inherently slow.
  • Multisubunit complex on a scaffold — a scaffold protein holds the enzymes together, minimising or eliminating substrate diffusion time.
  • Genetically fused — the enzymes become domains of a single polypeptide, again removing diffusion delay.

Metabolic enzymes may thus exist as domains of a monomer, subunits of a multimer, or components of a complex assembled on a common scaffold.

Reasoning · why cluster enzymes at all

Diffusion is the hidden tax on a pathway. If intermediate B has to wander through the cytoplasm to find the next enzyme, the pathway runs at the speed of random search — and B can be lost to side reactions or degradation on the way. Physically tethering the enzymes (scaffold) or fusing them (one polypeptide) hands the intermediate straight to the next active site ("substrate channelling"), so the pathway runs faster, cleaner, and can be regulated as a unit.

Clinical / real-world · enzymes as drug targets

Because an enzyme's whole output hinges on its active site, blocking that site is a mainstay of pharmacology — protease inhibitors treat HIV and hepatitis C, statins block a cholesterol-synthesis enzyme, and many antibiotics inhibit bacterial enzymes. The pH-dependence idea matters too: a drug's charge (and therefore its binding) shifts with local pH, which differs between blood, the lysosome and the stomach.

R Recall checklist — enzymes
  • ES complex — E binds S at the active site; catalysis speeds the rate but does not change the equilibrium.
  • How — enzymes lower the activation energy by stabilising the transition state.
  • Acid–base catalysis — side chains donate/accept protons (Asp, Glu, His, Tyr, Cys, Lys).
  • Catalytic triad — serine proteases use Ser, His, Asp (trypsin: Ser-195, His-57, Asp-102).
  • Lysozyme — glycoside hydrolase cutting NAM–NAG β-1,4 in peptidoglycan; antimicrobial.
  • pH optimum — lysosomal enzymes ~4.5; chymotrypsin ~8 (His-57 pKa ~6.8).
  • Speed trick — scaffolds / fused domains cut substrate diffusion time.
§4Part 4 · regulating function

Regulating protein & enzyme function

Four dials — make it, destroy it, bind it, or chemically tag it — turn activity up and down.

A working enzyme is not enough; the cell must run each one at the right time and rate. Proteins are regulated at the level of synthesis, degradation (the polyubiquitin tag → proteasome of §2.3), and through noncovalent and covalent interactions. This section covers the reversible switches: allosteric effectors, covalent modification, and the GTPase and phosphorylation switches.

§4.1Allosteric regulation

Allosteric regulation uses an effector — an inhibitor or an activator — that binds a site other than the substrate-binding site and thereby alters the enzyme's affinity for its substrate. It is reversible and works both ways: negative effectors dial activity down, positive effectors dial it up. In metabolic pathways this is often feedback inhibition — the end product of a pathway binds allosterically to an early enzyme and slows its own production.

Reasoning · why bind somewhere other than the active site

Using a separate site is what makes the switch flexible. The active site is committed to the substrate's shape; a distinct allosteric site can be tuned to recognise a completely different molecule (a pathway's end-product, an energy signal like ATP/AMP) and translate that binding into a conformational change that reshapes the active site. It decouples "what the enzyme does" from "what tells it when," so one enzyme can integrate several signals.

§4.2Covalent modification — ubiquitin & phosphorylation

Proteins can be regulated by covalent addition of chemical groups or other proteins.

Ubiquitinylation

Attaching ubiquitin comes in three flavours, and the specific pattern determines the target's fate: monoubiquitinylation (a single ubiquitin), multiubiquitinylation (several single ubiquitins), and polyubiquitinylation (a chain). All seven lysines of ubiquitin (Lys-6, 11, 27, 29, 33, 48, 63) plus its N-terminus can link chains — different linkages signal different outcomes (classically, Lys-48 chains mark for degradation, while others act in signalling).

Phosphorylation / dephosphorylation

The cell's fastest reversible switch:

  • Kinases transfer the terminal phosphate group from ATP onto specific Ser, Thr or Tyr hydroxyl groups of a target protein — typically activating it.
  • Phosphatases hydrolyse the phosphate back off — typically inactivating it.

The Src protein kinase is a worked example of a kinase whose own activity is controlled by phosphorylation of a regulatory tyrosine (an enzyme regulating an enzyme).

§4.3The GTPase molecular switch

Proteins that bind and hydrolyse GTP (GTPases) are ubiquitous cellular regulators that toggle between two forms:

  • GTP-bound = active "on" conformation — regulates target proteins.
  • GDP-bound = inactive "off" conformation.

Two accessory factors control the toggle, and two "helpers" are worth memorising:

  • Intrinsic GTPase activity hydrolyses bound GTP → GDP (switches itself off over time).
  • GEF (guanine nucleotide exchange factor) stimulates replacement of GDP with GTP → switches ON.
  • GAP (GTPase-activating protein) stimulates GTP hydrolysis to GDP → switches OFF.
Memory hook · GEF vs GAP

"GEF fills the tank; GAP hits the brakes." GEF exchanges GDP for GTP → on. GAP accelerates hydrolysis → GDP → off. GTP-bound is always the active, "on" state.

Clinical / real-world · switches stuck on

These switches are prime cancer genes. Ras is a GTPase; oncogenic mutations cripple its ability to hydrolyse GTP (or its response to GAP), so it is stuck in the GTP-bound "on" state, driving proliferation — one of the commonest oncogenes in human cancer. Kinases are equally druggable: imatinib blocks the BCR-ABL kinase in chronic myeloid leukaemia, and proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib) exploit the degradation arm of §2.3. Regulation isn't abstract — it's where much of oncology and pharmacology live.
