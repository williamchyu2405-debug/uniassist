§0Orientation · the plexuses

The lumbar & sacral plexuses

Anterior rami re-plaited into two networks that run the whole limb.

Somatic motor and sensory supply to the lower limb comes from the anterior rami of the lumbar and sacral spinal nerves, woven into two plexuses on the posterior abdominopelvic wall.

  • Lumbar plexus — anterior rami of L1–L4; its big outputs are the femoral and obturator nerves (front and inside of the thigh).
  • Sacral plexus — anterior rami of L4–S4; its great output is the sciatic nerve (back of the thigh, and the whole leg and foot). Its lower segments supply the perineum.
Reasoning · why a plexus, and why compartment predicts nerve

As in the arm, interleaving the roots lets one peripheral nerve gather several cord segments and run a whole functional group. The pay-off is the clean rule you already met with the muscles: femoral = anterior thigh (extend knee), obturator = medial thigh (adduct), sciatic = posterior thigh + everything below the knee. Name the compartment and you can predict the nerve — and what its lesion will knock out.

R Recall checklist — plexuses
  • Source — anterior rami of lumbar & sacral spinal nerves.
  • Lumbar plexus — L1–L4; gives femoral + obturator.
  • Sacral plexus — L4–S4; gives the sciatic nerve; lower segments → perineum.
§1Lumbar plexus

Femoral & obturator nerves

The anterior and medial thigh nerves — psoas is the landmark for both.

§1.1Femoral nerve (L2–L4)

The femoral nerve emerges from the lateral border of psoas major, supplies iliacus, then passes deep to the inguinal ligament (lateral to the femoral artery) into the femoral triangle and anterior thigh. There it supplies the anterior compartment: quadriceps femoris, sartorius and pectineus. Its terminal cutaneous branch, the saphenous nerve, continues down the medial leg and foot (the only part of the limb below the knee supplied by the lumbar plexus).

§1.2Obturator nerve (L2–L4)

The obturator nerve emerges from the medial border of psoas major and leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen to enter the medial thigh, where it splits into anterior and posterior branches to supply the adductor muscles and obturator externus (and often pectineus).

Memory hook · the psoas borders

"Femoral flanks it, obturator hugs it." The femoral nerve comes off the lateral border of psoas major, the obturator nerve off the medial border — matching their destinations (femoral to the front, obturator to the inside).

Clinical / real-world · femoral vs obturator lesions

A femoral nerve lesion abolishes knee extension (quadriceps) and numbs the anterior thigh and medial leg (saphenous) — the knee buckles. An obturator lesion weakens thigh adduction, so the leg tends to swing outward in gait. Because the femoral nerve runs lateral to the artery under the inguinal ligament, it can be caught by a groin haematoma or compressed during pelvic surgery.

R Recall checklist — lumbar plexus nerves
  • Femoral (L2–L4) — from the lateral border of psoas; supplies the anterior thigh (quadriceps, sartorius, pectineus).
  • Saphenous nerve — terminal cutaneous branch of the femoral; medial leg & foot.
  • Obturator (L2–L4) — from the medial border of psoas; through the obturator foramen; supplies the adductors.
§2Sacral plexus

The sciatic nerve

Two nerves in one sheath — the largest nerve in the body.

The sciatic nerve (L4–S3) is really two nerves — the tibial and common fibular — loosely bound together in one epineurial sheath. They travel down the posterior thigh and separate at the apex of the popliteal fossa.

  • Tibial nerve — supplies the flexor (posterior) muscles of the leg and the intrinsic flexor muscles of the foot.
  • Common fibular (peroneal) nerve — supplies the anterior and lateral compartments of the leg.

The posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh (S1–S3) supplies the skin of most of the posterior thigh and proximal leg. (The sciatic supplies no muscle in the thigh except the hamstrings and the hamstring part of adductor magnus.)

Clinical / real-world · the safe site for IM injection

Because the sciatic nerve leaves the pelvis below piriformis and runs through the lower-medial buttock, an intramuscular injection must go into the upper outer (superolateral) quadrant of the gluteal region — the one square with no major nerve beneath it. A needle placed too low and medial can strike the sciatic nerve, causing pain and foot-drop.

R Recall checklist — sciatic nerve
  • Sciatic (L4–S3) — tibial + common fibular in one sheath; separate at the popliteal fossa apex.
  • Tibial — posterior leg flexors + foot flexors.
  • Common fibular — anterior + lateral leg compartments.
  • IM injection — upper outer quadrant to avoid the sciatic nerve.
§3Leg & foot

Nerves of the leg & foot

The two sciatic divisions split the leg — and each has a signature palsy.

🤔 Predict · before you read on

A patient catches their leg on the outside of the knee and now walks with a high-stepping gait, unable to lift the front of the foot. Which nerve is injured?

The common fibular (peroneal) nerve, where it winds superficially around the neck of the fibula — the most commonly injured nerve in the lower limb. Losing it paralyses dorsiflexion and eversion, giving foot-drop and the compensatory high-stepping gait.

Tibial nerve

The tibial nerve runs through the popliteal fossa and behind the medial malleolus (in the tarsal tunnel) to supply the posterior leg (plantarflexors) and the sole. A tibial lesion means the patient cannot stand on tiptoe (lost plantar flexion), loses inversion and toe flexion, and has sensory loss over the sole.

Common fibular nerve & its branches

The common fibular nerve winds around the neck of the fibula — superficial and unprotected — and divides into two:

  • Deep fibular nerve — the anterior compartment (dorsiflexors + extensor digitorum brevis); sensory to the first web space. Its loss is the main cause of foot-drop.
  • Superficial fibular nerve — the lateral compartment (fibularis longus/brevis, the evertors) and the skin of most of the dorsum of the foot.

The sural nerve (from tibial + common fibular contributions) is purely cutaneous, supplying the posterolateral leg and lateral foot — commonly harvested for nerve grafts.

Reasoning · why the common fibular nerve is so vulnerable

The nerve is pinned in a shallow groove directly against the subcutaneous neck of the fibula, with almost no soft-tissue padding. A tight cast, crossed legs, a direct blow or a lateral knee injury presses it straight onto bone — which is why it tops the list of injured lower-limb nerves, and why its palsy (foot-drop) is a classic exam case.

§4Skin & movement maps

Dermatomes & myotomes

Two maps: a strip of skin per segment, and a movement per segment.

A dermatome is the strip of skin supplied by one spinal segment; a myotome is the muscle group supplied by one segment, tested by a joint movement. Together they localise a root lesion.

Dermatome landmarks worth memorising

  • L4 — the medial malleolus and medial leg.
  • L5 — the dorsum of the foot and the great toe.
  • S1 — the lateral foot, little toe and heel.

Clinical / real-world · localising a disc prolapse

A lumbar disc prolapse presses a nerve root, and the pattern pinpoints the level: an L5 lesion gives numbness over the dorsum of the foot and weak great-toe extension; an S1 lesion gives lateral-foot numbness, weak plantar flexion (can't tiptoe) and a lost ankle jerk. Reading the dermatome and myotome maps is how the level is found without a scan.

R Recall checklist — dermatomes & myotomes
  • Dermatomes — L4 medial malleolus, L5 dorsum/great toe, S1 lateral foot/little toe.
  • Hip flexion — L1–L2; knee extension — L3–L4.
  • Knee flexion — L5–S2; plantarflexion — S1–S2.
  • Digit adduction — S2–S3.
